# Understand the multivariate normal in matrix notation

The multivariate normal extends the familiar bell curve to a vector $\mathbf X=(X_1,\ldots,X_k)$. Its mean vector gives the centre, and its covariance matrix records each coordinate's spread and the way pairs of coordinates vary together.

## Define the ingredients

The mean vector is

$$
\boldsymbol\mu=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_k)^T.
$$

The covariance matrix $\Sigma$ has entries

$$
\Sigma_{ij}=\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)
=E[(X_i-\mu_i)(X_j-\mu_j)].
$$

Here $E$ means a probability-weighted average. On the diagonal, $\Sigma_{ii}=\sigma_i^2$ is variance. Off-diagonal entries describe whether departures from the two means tend to have the same or opposite signs.

For an ordinary full-dimensional normal density, $\Sigma$ must be symmetric and positive definite. This ensures that every nonzero direction has positive variance and that $\Sigma^{-1}$ exists.

## State the full formula

$$
\boxed{
f(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{k/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}
\exp\!\left[-\frac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^T
\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)\right].
}
$$

The superscript $T$ means transpose: it converts a column vector into a row. The matrix product in the exponent is a single nonnegative number, measuring distance from the centre with the model's scales and relationships taken into account.

The determinant controls the overall scale of the ellipsoidal spread. Its square root appears in the normalizing factor.

## Check the one-dimensional case

When $k=1$, $\Sigma=\sigma^2$ and its inverse is $1/\sigma^2$. The formula becomes

$$
\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right],
$$

the ordinary normal density.

## Check the two-dimensional case

For a bivariate normal,

$$
\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}
\sigma_1^2&\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\\
\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2&\sigma_2^2
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det\Sigma=\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2).
$$

Substituting its inverse into the quadratic expression reproduces the bivariate formula. Matrix notation packages the same centre, scaling and tilted-ellipse calculations into a form that works for any number of coordinates.
