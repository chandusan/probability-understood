# Uniform probability in a disk is an area ratio

Choose a point $(X,Y)$ uniformly from the disk $x^2+y^2\le r^2$, where $r>0$. Find the probability that the point lies within distance $r/2$ of the centre, without performing an integral.

## What uniform means here

Every region receives probability proportional to its area. The whole disk has area $\pi r^2$, so the constant density is

$$
f(x,y)=\frac1{\pi r^2},\qquad x^2+y^2\le r^2,
$$

and zero outside the disk.

The event “within $r/2$ of the centre” is

$$
A=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le r^2/4\}.
$$

It is the smaller concentric disk of radius $r/2$.

## Compare the two areas

$$
\begin{aligned}
P((X,Y)\in A)
&=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(A)}{\operatorname{Area}(\text{whole disk})}\\
&=\frac{\pi(r/2)^2}{\pi r^2}
=\boxed{\frac14}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Halving a radius halves two linear dimensions. Area therefore falls by a factor of four.

## Why the answer is not one half

Uniform in area does not mean uniform in distance from the centre. A thin annulus near the outer edge has more area than an equally thin annulus near the centre. There is more room for the point at larger radii.

For any $0\le s\le r$,

$$
P\!\left(\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}\le s\right)=\frac{s^2}{r^2}.
$$

Differentiating gives radial density $2s/r^2$. The density increases with $s$, even though the original joint density is constant across the disk.

## Know when the shortcut applies

The area ratio is valid because the point is uniform over the disk. If the density were higher near the centre, area alone would no longer determine probability; we would integrate that varying density over the smaller disk.
