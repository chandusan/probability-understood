# Multinomial counts come from repeated categorical trials

Repeat an experiment independently $n$ times. Each trial produces exactly one of $k$ categories $O_1,\ldots,O_k$, with fixed probabilities $p_1,\ldots,p_k$ summing to one. Let $X_i$ count how often category $i$ occurs.

## State the assumptions carefully

The multinomial model requires:

- A fixed number $n$ of trials.
- Exactly one category per trial.
- The same category probabilities on every trial.
- Independence between trials.

The category counts satisfy

$$
X_1+\cdots+X_k=n.
$$

Thus they are nonnegative integers whose total is fixed.

## Start with one ordered outcome sequence

Suppose a particular sequence contains $x_i$ occurrences of category $i$. Independence of trials gives its probability as

$$
p_1^{x_1}p_2^{x_2}\cdots p_k^{x_k}.
$$

Every sequence with those same category counts has the same probability, because multiplying the trial probabilities in another order does not change the product.

## Count all sequences with those counts

The number of distinct category sequences is

$$
\frac{n!}{x_1!\cdots x_k!}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
P(X_1=x_1,\ldots,X_k=x_k)
=\frac{n!}{x_1!\cdots x_k!}\prod_{i=1}^k p_i^{x_i},
}
$$

for $x_i\ge0$ integers summing to $n$, and zero otherwise.

## Independent trials do not mean independent counts

If one count increases, fewer trials remain available for the others. In fact, knowing $k-1$ counts determines the last one exactly. The independence assumption applies to the repeated trials, not to the category totals obtained from them.

For two categories, $X_2=n-X_1$ and the formula reduces to the binomial distribution. The multinomial is the same counting argument with more than two categories.
