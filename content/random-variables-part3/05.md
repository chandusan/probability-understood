# Integrate across x, then add the slices

To evaluate $\int_c^d\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$, first treat $y$ as a fixed number and integrate with respect to $x$. The result is a function of $y$, which you then integrate from $c$ to $d$.

## Begin with the entire expression

Consider

$$
I=\int_0^2\int_0^1 (x+2y)\,dx\,dy.
$$

The region is $0\le x\le1$, $0\le y\le2$. The function $x+2y$ is the height of the surface above each point of that rectangle.

## Step 1: hold y fixed

With $y$ fixed, $2y$ behaves like a constant. Therefore

$$
\int_0^1(x+2y)\,dx
=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}+2yx\right]_{x=0}^{x=1}
=\frac12+2y.
$$

This is the area of the cross-section at position $y$. We have finished integrating over $x$; the symbol $x$ should no longer appear in the result.

## Step 2: integrate the remaining function

Now add these cross-sectional areas along the $y$ direction:

$$
I=\int_0^2\left(\frac12+2y\right)dy
=\left[\frac y2+y^2\right]_0^2
=1+4=5.
$$

The final answer is a number because we have integrated over both coordinates.

## A useful diagnostic

While doing the inner integral, do not differentiate or integrate the other variable. For example,

$$
\int x^2y\,dx=y\frac{x^3}{3},
$$

because $y$ is acting as a constant multiplier. This is precisely the ordinary constant-multiple rule from one-variable calculus.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Why is there no arbitrary constant in these calculations?</summary>

We are evaluating definite integrals. An antiderivative's constant cancels when the lower endpoint is subtracted from the upper endpoint.

</details>
