# More coordinates, the same probability ideas

A multivariate distribution describes $k\ge2$ numerical measurements from one random experiment. The notation becomes larger, but the principles remain the same: assign probability to allowed combinations, add over unwanted coordinates, and renormalize after conditioning.

## One observation becomes a vector

For one customer, we might record purchase amount $X_1$, session duration $X_2$, and account age $X_3$. One observed customer gives the vector

$$
\mathbf X=(X_1,X_2,X_3).
$$

The coordinates can be related. Knowing an account's age might change the distribution of its purchase amount, so separate one-variable models may omit useful information.

## A joint density lives on the whole coordinate space

For three continuous variables, the probability of a region $A$ is

$$
P(\mathbf X\in A)=\iiint_Af(x_1,x_2,x_3)\,dx_1\,dx_2\,dx_3.
$$

A tiny rectangular box has approximate probability

$$
f(x_1,x_2,x_3)\Delta x_1\Delta x_2\Delta x_3.
$$

The integral adds those contributions over all boxes in the event. In $k$ dimensions, use one differential and one integration for each coordinate.

## Marginalization keeps the coordinates you need

If we only care about $(X_1,X_2)$, integrate over all $x_3$:

$$
f_{12}(x_1,x_2)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x_1,x_2,x_3)\,dx_3.
$$

If we care only about $X_1$, integrate out both $x_2$ and $x_3$.

## Conditioning fixes the coordinates you know

If $X_3=x_3$ is known, the remaining pair has conditional density

$$
f_{12\mid3}(x_1,x_2\mid x_3)
=\frac{f(x_1,x_2,x_3)}{f_3(x_3)},
$$

where the denominator is positive. It makes the density over the remaining two coordinates integrate to one.

The challenge in higher dimensions is often describing the support clearly. The rules themselves are direct extensions of the two-variable rules.
