# Equal-density contours are ellipses

A contour connects points with the same density height. For a bivariate normal distribution, keeping the density constant is equivalent to keeping its quadratic quantity $Q$ constant, because all other factors in the density are fixed parameters.

## Start with the contour equation

In standardized coordinates,

$$
Q=\frac{z_1^2-2\rho z_1z_2+z_2^2}{1-\rho^2}=q,
$$

where $q>0$ chooses a particular contour. Larger $q$ describes a more distant, lower-density contour.

If $\rho=0$, the equation becomes

$$
z_1^2+z_2^2=q.
$$

This is a circle in standardized coordinates. In the original units it is

$$
\frac{(x_1-\mu_1)^2}{\sigma_1^2}
+\frac{(x_2-\mu_2)^2}{\sigma_2^2}=q,
$$

an ellipse unless the two standard deviations are equal.

## See the tilted directions algebraically

Define two rotated coordinates:

$$
u=\frac{z_1+z_2}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
v=\frac{z_1-z_2}{\sqrt2}.
$$

Here $u$ measures movement in the same-sign direction, while $v$ measures disagreement. Substituting gives

$$
\boxed{Q=\frac{u^2}{1+\rho}+\frac{v^2}{1-\rho}.}
$$

For $\rho>0$, the denominator in the $u$ direction is larger, so a fixed contour extends farther along that direction. The ellipse tilts upward. For $\rho<0$, it extends farther along $v$, giving a downward tilt.

## A contour is not a probability by itself

Every point on one contour has the same density, but the contour line itself has zero two-dimensional area and therefore zero probability. To ask for a positive probability, use the region inside a contour and integrate the density there.

Contours are useful because they show orientation and relative concentration without requiring a three-dimensional plot.
