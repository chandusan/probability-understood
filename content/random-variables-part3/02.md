# A joint density gives probability to regions

Two continuous random variables $X$ and $Y$ have joint density $f(x,y)$ when the probability of every region $A$ is the integral of $f$ over that region. The graph $z=f(x,y)$ is a surface, and probability is volume beneath it.

## State all three requirements

A legitimate joint density satisfies

$$
f(x,y)\ge0,
\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=1,
$$

and probabilities are computed by

$$
\boxed{P((X,Y)\in A)=\iint_A f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.}
$$

Nonnegativity prevents negative probabilities. Total volume one expresses that the pair must lie somewhere. The region $A$ specifies the event we are actually asking about.

## Understand a small patch first

Near a point where the density is continuous, a rectangle of width $\Delta x$ and height $\Delta y$ has approximately

$$
P(x<X<x+\Delta x,\ y<Y<y+\Delta y)
\approx f(x,y)\Delta x\Delta y.
$$

Think of a tiny column: its base area is $\Delta x\Delta y$ and its height is the density. Adding these columns over an event gives its probability.

## A complete simple model

Suppose $f(x,y)=1/6$ on $0<x<2$, $0<y<3$, and zero elsewhere. The support has area $2\cdot3=6$, so the total probability is $(1/6)6=1$.

The event $0<X<1$, $0<Y<1$ is a unit square. Hence its probability is $(1/6)1=1/6$.

The density $1/6$ is probability **per unit of area**, not the probability at a point. Likewise, a density above one is allowed when its support is sufficiently small. Only the integrated probability must lie between zero and one.

## Always include the support

A formula such as $f(x,y)=cx$ is incomplete without its domain. The support determines which pairs are possible and which integration limits are valid.
