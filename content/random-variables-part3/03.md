# A double integral adds tiny columns

For a nonnegative function $f(x,y)$ above a region $R$, the double integral $\iint_R f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$ is the volume between the surface and the plane. This is the two-dimensional counterpart of area beneath a one-variable curve.

## Build the volume from small pieces

Partition $R$ into small rectangles. In one rectangle, choose a representative point $(x_i,y_i)$. If the rectangle is small enough that the height barely changes, its volume is approximately

$$
f(x_i,y_i)\Delta x_i\Delta y_i.
$$

Adding over the rectangles approximates the total volume:

$$
\sum_i f(x_i,y_i)\Delta x_i\Delta y_i.
$$

The integral is the limit as the pieces become arbitrarily small. The symbols $dx\,dy$ remember the two dimensions of each base patch.

## Why two integrations are useful

Rather than add every little column separately, we can group columns into strips. First integrate across one direction to obtain the area of a vertical cross-section. Then integrate those cross-sectional areas along the other direction to obtain volume.

For the constant surface $f(x,y)=4$ over $0\le x\le2$, $0\le y\le3$, every cross-section at a fixed $y$ has area $4\cdot2=8$. Summing these across a length of 3 gives volume $8\cdot3=24$:

$$
\int_0^3\left[\int_0^2 4\,dx\right]dy
=\int_0^3 8\,dy=24.
$$

## Volume is probability only after normalization

The surface in the example is nonnegative, but it is not a probability density: its volume is 24. Dividing it by 24 produces the constant density $1/6$ on that rectangle.

If the integrand can be negative, the double integral is a **signed** total: portions below the plane subtract. Such an integrand may be a valid calculus exercise, but it cannot serve as a probability density wherever it is negative.
