# Integrate horizontally through the triangle

For the surface $x^2y+xy^3$ over the triangle $x\ge0$, $y\ge0$, $x+y\le1$, choose $y$ first and hold it fixed. The horizontal slice then runs from $x=0$ to $x=1-y$.

## Write the nested integral

$$
I=\int_0^1\left[\int_0^{1-y}(x^2y+xy^3)\,dx\right]dy.
$$

The inner integral uses exactly the same antiderivative as the square example:

$$
\int(x^2y+xy^3)\,dx
=\frac{x^3y}{3}+\frac{x^2y^3}{2}.
$$

The changed geometry appears through the endpoint $x=1-y$.

## Substitute the whole upper endpoint

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^{1-y}(x^2y+xy^3)\,dx
&=\left[\frac{x^3y}{3}+\frac{x^2y^3}{2}\right]_{x=0}^{x=1-y}\\
&=\frac{y(1-y)^3}{3}+\frac{y^3(1-y)^2}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The lower endpoint contributes zero. Every occurrence of $x$ has been replaced by $1-y$; the remaining expression depends only on $y$, as an outer integral requires.

## Interpret the result before continuing

Define

$$
S(y)=\frac{y(1-y)^3}{3}+\frac{y^3(1-y)^2}{2}.
$$

This is the area of the vertical cross-section above the horizontal segment at height $y$. At $y=1$ the segment has zero length, so $S(1)=0$. At $y=0$ the surface itself has height zero, so $S(0)=0$ as well.

We now need only an ordinary one-variable integral:

$$
I=\int_0^1 S(y)\,dy.
$$

The important conceptual work is complete: the region has been translated into limits, and one coordinate has been integrated out. Expanding the remaining polynomial is arithmetic, not a new probability principle.
