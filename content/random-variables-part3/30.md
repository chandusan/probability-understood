# Read the bivariate normal formula one piece at a time

A bivariate normal distribution is a smooth probability model for a pair $(X_1,X_2)$. It has a centre, a spread in each coordinate, and a parameter describing how the two coordinates tend to move together.

## Define the parameters before the formula

The five parameters are

| Parameter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $\mu_1,\mu_2$ | Means of the two coordinates |
| $\sigma_1,\sigma_2>0$ | Their standard deviations |
| $\rho$, with $-1<\rho<1$ | Their correlation |

Write the standardized coordinates as

$$
z_1=\frac{x_1-\mu_1}{\sigma_1},
\qquad
z_2=\frac{x_2-\mu_2}{\sigma_2}.
$$

Each $z_i$ records how many standard deviations its coordinate lies above or below its mean.

## State the full density

For all real $x_1,x_2$,

$$
\boxed{
f(x_1,x_2)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{z_1^2-2\rho z_1z_2+z_2^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}\right].
}
$$

The exponent penalizes departures from the centre. The prefactor makes the total volume one. The quantity

$$
Q=\frac{z_1^2-2\rho z_1z_2+z_2^2}{1-\rho^2}
$$

measures departure while accounting for the direction of the relationship.

## Why the cross term matters

If $\rho>0$ and $z_1,z_2$ have the same sign, the term $-2\rho z_1z_2$ reduces $Q$, making such aligned deviations relatively more plausible. Opposite-sign deviations increase $Q$.

For negative $\rho$, the preference reverses. High values of one coordinate tend to accompany low values of the other.

## The special case rho = 0

Setting $\rho=0$ gives

$$
f(x_1,x_2)
=\frac{e^{-z_1^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_1}
\frac{e^{-z_2^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_2}.
$$

The joint density factors into the two normal marginal densities, so the coordinates are independent. Zero correlation implies independence **within the jointly normal family**; that implication is not true for arbitrary distributions.

At $\rho=\pm1$, the pair lies on a line and no ordinary two-dimensional density of this form exists. The strict restriction $|\rho|<1$ keeps the model nonsingular.
