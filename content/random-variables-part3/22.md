# A marginal density adds an entire slice

If $f(x,y)$ is the joint density of $(X,Y)$, the marginal density of $X$ is obtained by integrating over every possible $y$. We retain the coordinate we care about and add all the ways the other coordinate can occur.

## Derive the rule from an interval probability

For any interval $a<X<b$, we allow all $Y$ values:

$$
P(a<X<b)
=\int_a^b\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dy\right]dx.
$$

A density of $X$ must reproduce interval probabilities through $\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx$. Comparing the two expressions gives

$$
\boxed{f_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dy.}
$$

Similarly,

$$
\boxed{f_Y(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dx.}
$$

The infinite limits are a general notation. In a particular model, restrict them to the part of the support available at the fixed coordinate.

## Work through a triangular model

Take $f(x,y)=2$ on $0<y<x<1$. At a fixed $0<x<1$, possible $y$ values run from 0 to $x$:

$$
f_X(x)=\int_0^x2\,dy=2x.
$$

At a fixed $0<y<1$, possible $x$ values run from $y$ to 1:

$$
f_Y(y)=\int_y^1 2\,dx=2(1-y).
$$

Both marginals are zero outside $(0,1)$.

## Read the shapes geometrically

Large $x$ values have longer vertical slices, so $f_X(x)$ increases. Large $y$ values have shorter horizontal slices, so $f_Y(y)$ decreases. A constant joint density on a triangle does not produce constant marginal densities.

As a check,

$$
\int_0^1 2x\,dx=1,
\qquad
\int_0^1 2(1-y)\,dy=1.
$$

Each marginal is itself a complete one-variable probability model.
