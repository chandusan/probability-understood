# Derive the multinomial coefficient by choosing positions

Why does $n!/(x_1!\cdots x_k!)$ count sequences with fixed category totals? We can derive it directly by choosing which trial positions belong to each category, without assuming the formula.

## Fill the category positions in stages

There are $n$ labeled positions: trial 1 through trial $n$.

Choose $x_1$ positions for category 1 in $\binom n{x_1}$ ways. From the remaining $n-x_1$ positions, choose $x_2$ for category 2. Continue until all categories are assigned:

$$
\binom n{x_1}
\binom{n-x_1}{x_2}
\binom{n-x_1-x_2}{x_3}\cdots.
$$

The last category occupies every unfilled position, so its final choice has only one possibility.

## Expand the combinations

The first two factors are

$$
\frac{n!}{x_1!(n-x_1)!}
\frac{(n-x_1)!}{x_2!(n-x_1-x_2)!}.
$$

The remaining-factor factorials cancel successively, leaving

$$
\boxed{\frac{n!}{x_1!x_2!\cdots x_k!}.}
$$

Each final sequence is counted exactly once because its category-1 positions, category-2 positions, and so on uniquely identify it.

## A concrete example

In five trials, suppose we want two A outcomes, two B outcomes and one C outcome. Choose the two A positions in $\binom52=10$ ways, then the two B positions from the remaining three in $\binom32=3$ ways. The C position is forced.

The total is

$$
10\cdot3=30=\frac{5!}{2!2!1!}.
$$

If the category probabilities are $p_A,p_B,p_C$, each sequence has probability $p_A^2p_B^2p_C$, so the event's probability is $30p_A^2p_B^2p_C$.

## Another way to see the division

Imagine temporarily labeling occurrences within each category. There are $n!$ permutations, but the $x_i!$ permutations among identical category-$i$ occurrences do not create new category sequences. Dividing by every such factor removes exactly that repeated counting.
