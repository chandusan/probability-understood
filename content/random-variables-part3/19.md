# Bring double integration back to probability

A joint density turns the volume calculations into probabilities. The geometry tells us where to integrate, and the density tells us how strongly to weight each small piece of that region.

## Start with support and normalization

Suppose

$$
f(x,y)=2,\qquad 0<y<x<1,
$$

and zero elsewhere. The support is the triangle below the line $y=x$, above $y=0$, and to the left of $x=1$. Its area is $1/2$, so the total integral is $2(1/2)=1$.

We can verify that directly:

$$
\int_0^1\int_0^x 2\,dy\,dx
=\int_0^1 2x\,dx=1.
$$

## Intersect the event with the support

Find $P(X<1/2)$. We retain only the part of the triangle with $x<1/2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X<1/2)
&=\int_0^{1/2}\int_0^x2\,dy\,dx\\
&=\int_0^{1/2}2x\,dx
=\frac14.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is not $1/2$: even though the allowed $x$ range has been halved, the available height $0<y<x$ is smaller on the left side.

## Why an impossible region contributes nothing

The event $Y>X$ lies entirely outside the support, so its probability is zero. Integrating the formula 2 above the diagonal would be wrong because that formula applies only on the stated triangle; the density is zero elsewhere.

## A dependable workflow

For any joint-density problem:

1. State the density and its support.
2. Check or determine the normalizing constant.
3. Translate the event into inequalities.
4. Intersect those inequalities with the support.
5. Choose valid slices and integrate.

The main difficulty is often step 4. Once the correct limits are written, the remaining calculation may use only familiar one-variable integration rules.
