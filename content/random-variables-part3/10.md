# A curved boundary makes a limit depend on a variable

Rectangular regions have constant ranges in both coordinates. In a triangle or a curved region, the available range of one coordinate changes with the other, so an inner integration limit becomes a function.

## A triangle makes the issue visible

Consider the first-quadrant triangle

$$
A=\{(x,y):x\ge0,\ y\ge0,\ x+y\le1\}.
$$

Its corners are $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, and $(0,1)$. The sloping boundary is $y=1-x$.

At $x=0.2$, the allowed $y$ values run from 0 to 0.8. At $x=0.7$, they run from 0 to 0.3. Thus a constant upper bound $y=1$ would include points outside the triangle.

The correct vertical-slice integral is

$$
\iint_A f(x,y)\,dx\,dy
=\int_0^1\int_0^{1-x}f(x,y)\,dy\,dx.
$$

## Use a fixed sequence to find the bounds

1. Sketch the region and label its boundaries.
2. Choose horizontal or vertical slices.
3. Find the full range of the outer coordinate.
4. Hold that coordinate fixed and find where a slice enters and leaves the region.
5. Integrate between those inner endpoints.

The inside bounds may use the outside variable because that variable is held fixed during the inner integration. The outside bounds cannot depend on a variable that has already been integrated out.

## Check the geometry with the constant function one

Setting $f(x,y)=1$ should give area:

$$
\int_0^1\int_0^{1-x}1\,dy\,dx
=\int_0^1(1-x)\,dx=\frac12.
$$

That is the familiar area of this right triangle. If a proposed setup instead gives area one, it has probably integrated over the entire square.
