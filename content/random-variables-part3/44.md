# Assemble and check the multinomial distribution

The multinomial probability formula combines a sequence count with a sequence probability. Its support is just as important as its algebra: the category counts must be nonnegative integers summing to the fixed trial count $n$.

## Keep the two factors visible

$$
\boxed{
p(x_1,\ldots,x_k)
=\underbrace{\frac{n!}{x_1!\cdots x_k!}}_{\text{number of sequences}}
\underbrace{p_1^{x_1}\cdots p_k^{x_k}}_{\text{probability of one sequence}}.
}
$$

Outside $x_1+\cdots+x_k=n$, the probability is zero. When a count is zero, its factorial is $0!=1$, and a positive category probability raised to that count contributes one.

If a category has probability zero, its count must be zero with probability one. This can be handled directly rather than relying on an ambiguous $0^0$ notation.

## Why the probabilities add to one

Every ordered sequence of $n$ trial outcomes belongs to exactly one count tuple. Grouping the sequences by their counts neither omits nor duplicates any outcome. Since all sequence probabilities total one, the multinomial masses must total one as well.

Algebraically, the same statement is the multinomial expansion:

$$
(p_1+\cdots+p_k)^n
=\sum_{x_1+\cdots+x_k=n}
\frac{n!}{x_1!\cdots x_k!}\prod_i p_i^{x_i}=1.
$$

## Recover the binomial model

With $k=2$, write $x_1=x$ and $x_2=n-x$, and let $p_1=p$, $p_2=1-p$. The formula becomes

$$
P(X_1=x)=\binom nx p^x(1-p)^{n-x}.
$$

Likewise, in a larger multinomial model, a single category count is binomial: label that category a success and combine all others into failure. Then $X_i\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p_i)$.

## Count tuples are not equally likely

The number of possible count tuples is $\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}$, but their probabilities differ because both the sequence counts and category probabilities can differ. Uniformity of the tuple list is not part of the multinomial model.
