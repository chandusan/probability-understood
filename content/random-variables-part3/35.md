# Conditioning a normal pair shifts the centre and reduces spread

For a bivariate normal pair, the conditional distribution remains normal. Knowing one coordinate changes the predicted centre of the other and leaves a smaller amount of uncertainty, determined by the strength of their correlation.

## State the distribution with its roles clear

For a given value $X_1=x_1$,

$$
\boxed{
X_2\mid X_1=x_1
\sim N\!\left(
\mu_2+\rho\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}(x_1-\mu_1),
\ \sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2)
\right).
}
$$

The second argument is conditional variance. The conditional standard deviation is $\sigma_2\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$.

## Interpret the mean in three steps

1. Measure the observed departure $x_1-\mu_1$.
2. Divide by $\sigma_1$ to express it in standard deviations of $X_1$.
3. Multiply by $\rho\sigma_2$ and add to $\mu_2$.

Thus an observation one standard deviation above the first mean shifts the conditional mean by $\rho$ standard deviations of the second variable.

Positive correlation shifts in the same direction. Negative correlation shifts in the opposite direction. At $\rho=0$, the mean remains $\mu_2$.

## A complete numerical example

Suppose $\mu_1=100$, $\sigma_1=15$, $\mu_2=50$, $\sigma_2=10$, and $\rho=0.6$. Observe $X_1=130$, which is two standard deviations above its mean.

The conditional mean is

$$
50+0.6\frac{10}{15}(130-100)=50+12=62.
$$

The conditional standard deviation is

$$
10\sqrt{1-0.6^2}=10\sqrt{0.64}=8.
$$

So $X_2\mid X_1=130\sim N(62,64)$.

## What information reduced

Before observing $X_1$, the standard deviation of $X_2$ was 10. Afterwards it is 8. The sign of correlation determines the direction of the mean shift; its magnitude determines the spread reduction.

For this model, conditional variance does not depend on the particular observed $x_1$. That is a property of the bivariate normal family, not a rule for every conditional distribution.
