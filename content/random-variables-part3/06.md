# Reverse the order without changing the region

The same rectangular volume can be computed by integrating with respect to $y$ first and then $x$. The slices change direction, but every point of the original rectangle must still be covered once.

## Revisit the complete example

We want the integral of $f(x,y)=x+2y$ over $0\le x\le1$, $0\le y\le2$. With $y$ as the inner variable,

$$
I=\int_0^1\int_0^2(x+2y)\,dy\,dx.
$$

For a fixed $x$, $x$ is constant throughout the inner integration. Thus

$$
\int_0^2(x+2y)\,dy
=\left[xy+y^2\right]_{y=0}^{y=2}
=2x+4.
$$

Now integrate the cross-sectional area over $x$:

$$
I=\int_0^1(2x+4)\,dx
=\left[x^2+4x\right]_0^1=5.
$$

This agrees with the other order.

## What stayed the same?

Both calculations include the same surface heights over the same rectangle. One groups the tiny volume elements into horizontal strips; the other groups them into vertical strips. Regrouping these nonnegative contributions cannot change their total.

For a probability density, this is also why either order computes the same probability. For general functions that can be negative, standard interchange results require conditions such as absolute integrability; continuity on our bounded rectangles is sufficient.

## Choose the simpler inner integral

Sometimes both orders are equally easy. Sometimes one avoids a difficult antiderivative. The geometry determines which orders are valid; algebra helps us choose the easier valid one.

Reversing the order becomes more demanding for a triangle or a curved region. Then the inner limits usually depend on the outer variable, so the region must be described afresh rather than merely swapping symbols.
