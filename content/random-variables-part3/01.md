# Two measurements, one probability model

A bivariate continuous distribution describes two numerical measurements from the same random experiment. Instead of assigning probability to isolated pairs, we assign probability to regions of the plane: for example, the chance that a delivery takes less than 30 minutes and travels less than 5 kilometres.

## First identify the outcome and the two measurements

One delivery is the random outcome. Let $X$ be its distance and $Y$ its duration. The observed pair $(X,Y)$ becomes one point in the plane. Repeating the experiment creates a cloud of points.

Questions about both measurements become regions. “Distance below 5 and duration below 30” means

$$
A=\{(x,y):x<5,\ y<30\}.
$$

A **joint density** $f(x,y)$ tells us how probability is distributed across that plane. Where the surface is higher, a small patch of a given area receives more probability.

## Replace bars by a surface

For discrete variables we add the probability masses of qualifying pairs. For continuous variables we add contributions from increasingly small patches:

$$
P((X,Y)\in A)=\iint_A f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.
$$

This chapter makes that formula usable. We first learn how a double integral adds volume, then how to choose limits for rectangles and triangles. We use those calculations to obtain marginal and conditional distributions, interpret the bivariate normal model, and extend the same ideas to more variables.

## Keep three questions separate

- **Joint:** What happens to $X$ and $Y$ together?
- **Marginal:** What happens to $X$, allowing every possible $Y$?
- **Conditional:** What happens to $Y$ after a particular $X$ is given?

These ask for different operations on the same model: integrate over a region, integrate out a coordinate, or restrict and renormalize a slice.

## Check your understanding

<details markdown="1"><summary>Does a high density at one point mean a positive probability of that exact pair?</summary>

No. An individual point has zero area. For a distribution described entirely by a joint density, its probability is zero. A high density means nearby small regions receive relatively more probability.

</details>
