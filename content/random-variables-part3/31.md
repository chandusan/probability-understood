# Read the normal density surface as a probability landscape

The slide compares bivariate normal surfaces with equal marginal standard deviations and correlations $0$, $0.90$, and $-0.90$. Their shapes reveal how correlation redistributes probability while preserving each coordinate's marginal spread.

## Zero correlation: a round mound

When $\sigma_1=\sigma_2=\sigma$ and $\rho=0$, the exponent depends on

$$
\frac{(x_1-\mu_1)^2+(x_2-\mu_2)^2}{\sigma^2}.
$$

Points at the same distance from the centre have the same density. The surface is rotationally symmetric around the vertical line through $(\mu_1,\mu_2)$.

For this jointly normal model, learning one coordinate does not change the distribution of the other.

## Positive correlation: an upward-oriented ridge

For $\rho=0.90$, the density concentrates near pairs whose standardized coordinates agree:

$$
z_2\approx z_1.
$$

Both-above-average and both-below-average outcomes are common relative to the independent case. Pairs where one coordinate is far above its mean and the other far below are less plausible.

The cloud is long in the matching direction and narrow in the disagreement direction. The overall two-dimensional volume is still one.

## Negative correlation: a downward-oriented ridge

For $\rho=-0.90$, the high-density ridge follows approximately

$$
z_2\approx-z_1.
$$

A high first coordinate tends to accompany a low second coordinate. The concentration is just as strong as for $+0.90$, but its orientation is reversed.

## Why a narrower surface can have a taller peak

At the centre, $Q=0$, so

$$
f(\mu_1,\mu_2)=\frac1{2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}.
$$

As $|\rho|$ increases, $\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$ decreases and the peak rises. Probability is being concentrated into a narrower band; a taller surface compensates to keep the total volume one.

The peak is a density height, not the probability of observing the exact centre. That point still has probability zero.
