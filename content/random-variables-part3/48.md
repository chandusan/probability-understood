# Marginalize a joint distribution by removing coordinates

Given a joint density for $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, the marginal joint density of the first $q$ variables retains those coordinates and integrates over the other $n-q$. Marginalization means allowing every possible value of the coordinates we are no longer tracking.

## State the general rule

$$
\boxed{
f_{1\ldots q}(x_1,\ldots,x_q)
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\,dx_{q+1}\cdots dx_n.
}
$$

The retained coordinates act as constants during these integrations. The support determines the actual bounds for the removed coordinates.

For discrete variables, replace the integrations by sums over the removed coordinates.

## Example: keep two treatment counts

For the multinomial treatment model, retain $(X,Y)$ and remove $Z$:

$$
p_{X,Y}(x,y)=\sum_z p(x,y,z).
$$

For fixed $x,y$, only one value can contribute, because $Z=4-x-y$. Thus

$$
\boxed{
p_{X,Y}(x,y)=\frac{4!}{x!y!(4-x-y)!}
(0.3)^x(0.5)^y(0.2)^{4-x-y},
}
$$

for nonnegative integers $x,y$ with $x+y\le4$.

## Keep only the cure count

If only $X$ matters, each patient is either cured with probability $0.3$ or not cured with probability $0.7$. Hence

$$
\boxed{P(X=x)=\binom4x(0.3)^x(0.7)^{4-x}.}
$$

To see how summing produces the $0.7$ factor, fix the $x$ cured patients. For each remaining patient, sum the two allowed category probabilities $0.5+0.2=0.7$. Across $4-x$ independent remaining patients, this gives $(0.7)^{4-x}$.

## What information is lost?

The marginal of $X$ preserves every probability question involving the cure count alone. It no longer tells us how the noncured patients divide between reduced pain and no change.

Marginalization removes detail intentionally. It does not assume that the removed coordinates are independent of the retained ones.
