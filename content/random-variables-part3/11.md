# Vertical slices: bottom to top, then left to right

A region is vertically simple when every vertical slice passes through one uninterrupted interval. Write it as $a\le x\le b$ and $g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x)$, where $g_1$ is the lower boundary and $g_2$ the upper boundary.

## Translate the two directions into limits

At a fixed $x$, integrate upward from the lower curve to the upper curve. Then move that slice across all allowed $x$ values:

$$
\boxed{
\iint_A f(x,y)\,dx\,dy
=\int_a^b\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}f(x,y)\,dy\,dx.
}
$$

This is often called a **Type I** region. The label is less important than the geometry: $dy$ describes movement up one slice, and $dx$ describes sweeping the slice across the region.

## Worked geometric example

Take the region between $y=x^2$ and $y=x$ for $0\le x\le1$. On that interval $x^2\le x$, so

$$
A=\{(x,y):0\le x\le1,\ x^2\le y\le x\}.
$$

Its area is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_A1\,dx\,dy
&=\int_0^1\int_{x^2}^{x}1\,dy\,dx\\
&=\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx\\
&=\frac12-\frac13=\frac16.
\end{aligned}
$$

The inner integral is exactly the slice's height, upper minus lower. The outer integral adds all these heights times tiny widths.

## When to split a region

If a vertical line enters and leaves a region several times, one interval cannot describe its entire intersection. Split the region into pieces, or choose horizontal slices if they are simpler.

A graph is helpful, but the inequalities are the final check. Test a point that should be inside and a point that should be outside before doing a long calculation.
