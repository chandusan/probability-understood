# Connect the surface, contours and observed point cloud

The slide shows three views of each bivariate normal model: a density surface, equal-height contours, and a scatterplot of simulated observations. These pictures describe the same probability distribution in different ways.

## The surface describes density

The height above $(x_1,x_2)$ measures probability per unit area nearby. To obtain a probability, integrate over a base region and take the volume under the surface.

A tall narrow ridge means probability is concentrated near a preferred relationship between the coordinates. It does not mean all observations lie exactly on that ridge.

## The contours describe equal heights

The middle row flattens the surface into a map. Each ellipse joins locations with equal density. Closely packed narrow contours indicate a direction in which density falls rapidly.

With equal standard deviations and $\rho=0$, contours are circles. With $\rho=0.9$, they stretch upward; with $\rho=-0.9$, they stretch downward.

## The scatterplot describes repeated observations

Each dot is one sampled pair. Dots tend to accumulate where the density is high, but sampling variability means a finite scatterplot will not reproduce the theoretical contours perfectly.

For $\rho=0$, there is no preferred linear direction. For $\rho=0.9$, the cloud is narrow around an increasing trend. For $\rho=-0.9$, it is narrow around a decreasing trend.

## The marginals can stay unchanged

All three examples can have exactly the same normal marginal distribution for each coordinate while having very different joint structures. If we inspect only separate histograms of $X_1$ and $X_2$, these models may look identical.

The joint plot reveals what the separate histograms hide: which first-coordinate values tend to occur with which second-coordinate values.

## Check your understanding

<details markdown="1"><summary>If two variables each have a normal marginal distribution, must their joint distribution be bivariate normal?</summary>

No. Marginals describe each coordinate separately and do not determine how the coordinates are paired. Bivariate normality is an additional statement about the whole joint distribution. Its elliptical contours and normal conditional distributions depend on that joint assumption.

</details>
