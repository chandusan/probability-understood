# Find every inclusion that must be true

You are given four sets but no special relationship between A and B. The challenge is to distinguish a relationship that holds **for every choice** of A and B from one that happens to hold in a particular drawing.

## Translate the four expressions

The slide asks which of these sets are subsets of which others:

$$
A,\qquad B,\qquad A\cup B,\qquad A^c\cap B.
$$

Write $U=A\cup B$ and $D=A^c\cap B=B\setminus A$ to keep the comparison readable. U contains anything in A or B. D is the **B-only** region: outcomes inside B but outside A.

Every member of A is in U; every member of B is in U. Every member of D is in B, and therefore also in U. Thus the nontrivial inclusions guaranteed by the definitions are

$$
A\subseteq U,
\qquad
D\subseteq B\subseteq U.
$$

The second chain also gives $D\subseteq U$ by transitivity. Every set is, of course, a subset of itself.

## The complete answer

Read each cell as “row set is a subset of column set.” **Always** means guaranteed for arbitrary A and B; **not always** means additional information would be needed.

| Row set | A | B | U | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Always | Not always | Always | Not always |
| B | Not always | Always | Always | Not always |
| U | Not always | Not always | Always | Not always |
| D | Not always | Always | Always | Always |

We are using inclusion that permits equality. These are not necessarily proper inclusions: for example, if $A=\varnothing$, then $D=B=U$.

## One example refutes every unsupported inclusion

Take

$$
S=\{1,2,3\},\qquad A=\{1,2\},\qquad B=\{2,3\}.
$$

Then $U=\{1,2,3\}$ and $D=\{3\}$. Here are witnesses for all eight “not always” cells:

| Proposed inclusion | Outcome that disproves it |
|---|---|
| $A\subseteq B$ | 1 is in A but not B. |
| $A\subseteq D$ | 1 is in A but not D. |
| $B\subseteq A$ | 3 is in B but not A. |
| $B\subseteq D$ | 2 is in B but not D. |
| $U\subseteq A$ | 3 is in U but not A. |
| $U\subseteq B$ | 1 is in U but not B. |
| $U\subseteq D$ | 1 is in U but not D. |
| $D\subseteq A$ | 3 is in D but not A. |

This does not mean those inclusions can never hold. It means they cannot be concluded from the problem's information alone.

## A useful structural observation

D cannot overlap A:

$$
A\cap D=A\cap(A^c\cap B)=\varnothing.
$$

A and D together partition the union into two disjoint pieces:

$$
U=A\cup D.
$$

Why does this still include the overlap $A\cap B$? Because it is already inside A. Removing A from B prevents counting that overlap a second time.

## Check your understanding

If you learn that $A\subseteq B$, which of U and D simplify? Does D necessarily become empty?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Use a nested example</summary>

The union simplifies to $U=B$. The difference remains $D=B\setminus A$, the part of B outside A, and need not be empty. For $A=\{1\}$ and $B=\{1,2\}$, we get $U=\{1,2\}$ and $D=\{2\}$. D is empty precisely when B has no members outside A; combined with $A\subseteq B$, that would mean $A=B$.
