# A rule with one answer

A deterministic rule gives the same output whenever all relevant inputs are the same. The key idea is uniqueness: once the complete state and the rule are fixed, there is one resulting outcome.

## Start with a conversion you can trust

Suppose a thermometer reports a temperature in Fahrenheit. Convert it using

$$T_C=(T_F-32)\frac59.$$

For $T_F=77$, the answer is $(77-32)(5/9)=25$. Enter $77$ again and the rule gives $25$ again. There is no distribution of conversion results to choose from.

We express this idea as

$$y=f(\mathbf{x}),$$

where $\mathbf{x}$ is the input, $f$ is the rule, and $y$ is the output. Inputs can be a single number, a vector of measurements, or an entire state of a physical or computational system.

## Deterministic does not mean easy

The slide describes a “closed-form analytic mapping.” That is one example of a deterministic rule, not a requirement. A long computer algorithm, an iterative numerical procedure with fixed inputs, or a differential equation with a uniquely determined solution can also be deterministic. We may have to compute the answer step by step rather than write it in one compact formula.

Likewise, deterministic does not mean practically predictable. If we do not know the inputs accurately, or cannot perform the calculation efficiently, the outcome may remain uncertain to us. A chaotic system can follow deterministic laws while amplifying tiny initial measurement errors.

## The system and our prediction are different things

Under a completely specified deterministic model, there is no remaining conditional variation:

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})=0.$$

But suppose the thermometer rounds to the nearest degree. Its displayed input is not the exact temperature. The conversion rule is still deterministic; uncertainty enters through the measurement.

The slide calls deviations “epistemic.” That label needs care: lack of knowledge about a rule or state is epistemic uncertainty, while a measurement process can itself be modeled with random noise. It helps to name which component is deterministic and which source of uncertainty is being modeled, instead of declaring every prediction error to be the same kind.

Examples include unit conversions, fixed business rules, arithmetic programs, and idealized mechanical laws given a complete state. Whether all latent variables are observable is a practical issue, not part of the definition of determinism.

## Check your understanding

A calculator repeatedly converts the same entered value $77^\circ\mathrm{F}$ to $25^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Does this prove the actual room temperature is known exactly?

No. It proves the conversion is fixed for the entered input. The thermometer may be imprecise or biased, and the temperature may vary across the room. A deterministic transformation preserves the consequences of the information supplied to it; it does not create missing information about the world.
