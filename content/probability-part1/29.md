# Split one event into two nonoverlapping parts

Every outcome in $A$ answers a second question: does it also belong to $B$? The answers yes and no split $A$ into two pieces without losing any part of it or counting anything twice.

## The partition identity

The source diagram says

$$
A=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap B^c).
$$

The first piece is **the part of $A$ inside $B$**. The second is **the part of $A$ outside $B$**. Both pieces remain inside $A$.

For a die, let $A=\{2,4,6\}$ be even numbers and $B=\{1,2,3\}$ be numbers below 4. Then

$$
A\cap B=\{2\},\qquad A\cap B^c=\{4,6\}.
$$

Putting those pieces together restores $\{2,4,6\}$.

## Why nothing disappears

Take any $e\in A$. Either $e\in B$ or $e\notin B$. In the first case it lands in $A\cap B$; in the second, in $A\cap B^c$. Thus every member of $A$ appears on the right.

Conversely, every outcome in either right-hand piece belongs to $A$ by definition. No outsider has been added.

## Why there is no overlap

An outcome cannot belong to both $B$ and $B^c$. Therefore

$$
(A\cap B)\cap(A\cap B^c)=\varnothing.
$$

This disjointness is what makes the decomposition especially useful in probability:

$$
P(A)=P(A\cap B)+P(A\cap B^c).
$$

No independence assumption is required. We are adding probabilities of pieces that cannot happen together.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Derive the identity using the previous laws</summary>

Since $B\cup B^c=S$,

$$
A=A\cap S=A\cap(B\cup B^c).
$$

Distribute intersection across union:

$$
A\cap(B\cup B^c)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap B^c).
$$

This algebra is a compact version of the same yes-or-no membership argument.

</details>

## A bridge to later probability rules

If $A$ means “a customer makes a purchase” and $B$ means “the customer is a member,” the two pieces are purchases by members and purchases by nonmembers. Splitting a complicated event into categories can make its probability easier to calculate. Later, total probability uses the same idea with more than two categories.

Strictly, definitions of a partition often require every piece to be nonempty. If one of these two pieces is empty, the identity still holds; omit that empty piece when describing a partition in that strict sense.

## Check your understanding

Using the same $A$ and $B$, split $B$ according to whether $A$ occurs. What two pieces do you obtain?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the decomposition</summary>

$B=(B\cap A)\cup(B\cap A^c)=\{2\}\cup\{1,3\}$. The pieces are disjoint and together contain every member of $B$. This reverses the roles of the events without changing the underlying principle.

</details>
