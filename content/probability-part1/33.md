# Prove a set identity by following one outcome

A Venn diagram suggests what is true. A proof explains why it stays true for **every** allowed outcome and every pair of sets. The dependable method is to choose an arbitrary outcome, translate set symbols into ordinary words, and follow its membership.

## What containment immediately tells us

If $A\subseteq B$, A has no members outside B. Consequently,

$$
A\cap B=A,
\qquad
A\cup B=B.
$$

The intersection keeps everything in A, because all of it also passes B's test. The union adds nothing new to B, because A contributes no outcome B did not already contain.

For example, take $S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, $A=\{2,4\}$, and $B=\{2,4,6\}$. Then $A\cap B=\{2,4\}=A$ and $A\cup B=\{2,4,6\}=B$.

## Equality needs both directions

To prove $X=Y$, prove $X\subseteq Y$ **and** $Y\subseteq X$. Otherwise, you have shown that one set fits inside the other, but the larger might still contain extra members.

“Let $e$ be arbitrary” means we did not select an especially convenient outcome. The argument must work for any outcome with the stated membership.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full proof of the intersection identity, including its converse</summary>

Assume $A\subseteq B$.

1. Let $e\in A\cap B$. By the definition of intersection, $e\in A$ and $e\in B$. In particular, $e\in A$. Thus $A\cap B\subseteq A$.
2. Let $e\in A$. The assumption $A\subseteq B$ gives $e\in B$. Therefore $e\in A\cap B$, so $A\subseteq A\cap B$.

Both inclusions prove $A\cap B=A$.

**Converse:** suppose $A\cap B=A$. Let $e\in A$. By the assumed equality, $e\in A\cap B$, which implies $e\in B$. Hence $A\subseteq B$.

We have proved the useful equivalence

$$
A\subseteq B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\cap B=A.
$$

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Full proof of the union identity, including its converse</summary>

Assume $A\subseteq B$.

1. Let $e\in A\cup B$. Then $e\in A$ or $e\in B$ (possibly both). If $e\in A$, containment gives $e\in B$. In either case $e\in B$, so $A\cup B\subseteq B$.
2. Let $e\in B$. Then $e$ belongs to at least one of A and B, so $e\in A\cup B$. Therefore $B\subseteq A\cup B$.

Both inclusions prove $A\cup B=B$.

**Converse:** suppose $A\cup B=B$. Let $e\in A$. Then $e\in A\cup B$, and the equality gives $e\in B$. Thus $A\subseteq B$.

Therefore

$$
A\subseteq B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\cup B=B.
$$

</details>

## Taking complements reverses containment

All complements here use the same sample space S. The slide's bar notation $\overline A$ and our notation $A^c$ both mean $S\setminus A$.

$$
A\subseteq B
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
A^c\supseteq B^c.
$$

A bigger event leaves a smaller outside region. In the die example, $A^c=\{1,3,5,6\}$ while $B^c=\{1,3,5\}$. Thus $B^c\subseteq A^c$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Prove both directions of the “if and only if”</summary>

**Forward:** assume $A\subseteq B$. Choose any $e\in B^c$. Then $e\notin B$. If $e$ belonged to A, containment would force $e\in B$, a contradiction. Hence $e\notin A$, meaning $e\in A^c$. This proves $B^c\subseteq A^c$.

**Reverse:** assume $B^c\subseteq A^c$. Choose any $e\in A$. Suppose $e\notin B$. Then $e\in B^c$, so the assumption implies $e\in A^c$. That contradicts $e\in A$. Therefore $e\in B$, proving $A\subseteq B$.

The two directions together establish the “iff.” Proving only the forward direction would not establish the full claim.

</details>

## Check your understanding

A student proves $A\cap B\subseteq A$ and concludes $A\cap B=A$. What is missing? Under what additional condition can they finish?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Find the missing direction</summary>

The first inclusion is true for every A and B, even disjoint sets. They also need $A\subseteq A\cap B$. The condition $A\subseteq B$ supplies it: every member of A then lies in both sets. For a counterexample without that condition, take $A=\{1\}$ and $B=\{2\}$. The intersection is empty, which is contained in A but is not equal to A.

</details>
