# A needle turns chance into geometry

Drop a needle onto parallel lines. The outcome is a position and an orientation, and the event we care about is whether the needle crosses a line. This is the beginning of geometric probability: a question about chance becomes a question about which points in a geometric space satisfy a condition.

## The physical setup

The lines are horizontal, with perpendicular spacing $d$. The needle has length $l$. The picture shows one possible resting configuration. “Buffoon's” in the source is a spelling error; the classical name is **Buffon's needle**.

A needle lying almost parallel to the lines has little vertical reach. A nearly perpendicular needle has more. Its center also matters: a needle far from the nearest line may miss even when it is steeply tilted.

Thus neither angle alone nor center alone tells the whole story.

## Decide what we will record

A complete planar placement can be described by the midpoint's two coordinates and the needle's orientation. For the simpler crossing question, horizontal translation makes no difference. We can eventually reduce the description to:

- $x$: the midpoint's perpendicular distance to the nearest line;
- $\theta$: the smaller angle between the needle and the parallel lines.

These are coordinates of a possible outcome, not probabilities. We must separately specify how placements are randomized.

## What does a random drop assume?

The standard idealized model makes orientation uniform and position uniform within one strip, with position independent of orientation. It ignores needle thickness and treats exact tangency as a zero-probability boundary event.

A real person could favor particular angles or locations. Calling a drop “random” does not automatically establish uniformity; the assumptions define the mathematical experiment we are about to solve.

For the short-needle formula we will assume $0<l\le d$. Longer needles require a different calculation because the conditional crossing probability can saturate at one, and multiple-line crossings become possible.

## Preview the geometry

At angle $\theta$, half of the needle reaches a vertical distance $(l/2)\sin\theta$ from its midpoint. It reaches a nearest line exactly when

$$x\le\frac l2\sin\theta.$$

Later we will shade all $(x,\theta)$ pairs satisfying this inequality and measure their probability. For now, notice how a verbal event has become a precise geometric test.

## Check your understanding

A needle has length $l=1$, and the lines are $d=2$ apart. Its center is $0.4$ from the nearest line. Does it cross at $\theta=30^\circ$? What about $90^\circ$?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

At $30^\circ$, the half-length vertical reach is $(1/2)\sin30^\circ=0.25$, less than $0.4$, so it misses. At $90^\circ$, the reach is $0.5$, greater than $0.4$, so it crosses. Tilting changes the perpendicular reach even though the needle's actual length does not change.

</details>
