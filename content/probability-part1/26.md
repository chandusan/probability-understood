# Translate sentences into membership tests

Words such as “or,” “and,” and “not” tell us how to combine event conditions. The safest translation is to test one possible outcome against the sentence, including an outcome that satisfies both conditions.

## The three basic translations

| Language | Set operation | Membership test |
|---|---|---|
| $A$ or $B$; at least one | $A\cup B$ | In $A$, in $B$, or in both |
| $A$ and $B$; both | $A\cap B$ | In both events |
| Not $A$ | $A^c$ | In the sample space but outside $A$ |

In probability, “or” normally means **inclusive or**. If a die result is both even and less than 4, it still satisfies “even or less than 4.” Thus the result 2 is included.

Everyday language sometimes uses “either … or …” to mean exactly one. If a problem explicitly says **“but not both”**, use the exclusive alternative:

$$
(A\cap B^c)\cup(A^c\cap B).
$$

Do not silently infer exclusivity from the word “either” when the mathematical context allows both.

## Put parentheses around the meaning

Suppose $A$ means “the customer has a membership,” $B$ means “the customer has a coupon,” and $C$ means “the order is eligible.”

“Has a membership or coupon, and the order is eligible” usually means

$$
(A\cup B)\cap C.
$$

The eligibility condition applies to both ways of qualifying. Writing $A\cup(B\cap C)$ would instead admit every member, even with an ineligible order. Parentheses preserve the intended scope.

## Negating a sentence changes its connector

“Neither $A$ nor $B$” means not $A$ and not $B$:

$$
(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c.
$$

“Not both” allows either one alone or neither:

$$
(A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c.
$$

These are De Morgan's laws, which we will prove. For now, a simple membership check makes the distinction clear: an outcome in $A$ only satisfies “not both,” but it does not satisfy “neither.”

## Check your understanding

Let $A$ mean “even” and $B$ mean “less than 4” on a die. Is the result 2 included in “$A$ or $B$”? Is it included in “either $A$ or $B$, but not both”?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the translation</summary>

Two belongs to both $A$ and $B$, so inclusive “or” includes it. “But not both” excludes it. The inclusive union is $\{1,2,3,4,6\}$; the exclusive alternative is $\{1,3,4,6\}$.

</details>
