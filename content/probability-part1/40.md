# Counting works when the outcomes have equal weight

“Favorable outcomes divided by total outcomes” is a consequence of an equally likely model. It is not the definition of probability for every situation. Before counting, ask why each elementary outcome deserves the same weight.

## Derive the familiar fraction

Suppose the sample space has N distinct outcomes:

$$
S=\{o_1,o_2,\ldots,o_N\},\qquad N\ge1.
$$

Now make the crucial assumption: these outcomes are **equally likely**. If their common probability is q, their singleton events are disjoint and exhaust S. Additivity and normalization give

$$
1=P(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}P(\{o_i\})=Nq.
$$

Therefore

$$
P(\{o_i\})=\frac1N.
$$

The probability attaches formally to the singleton event $\{o_i\}$. Writing $P(o_i)$ is a common shorthand when the meaning is clear.

If an event E contains m of these elementary outcomes, add their equal weights:

$$
P(E)=\underbrace{\frac1N+\cdots+\frac1N}_{m\text{ terms}}
=\frac mN
=\frac{n(E)}{n(S)}.
$$

Here $n(A)$ means the number of elements of A; it is also written $|A|$. The slide calls this a **simple** or **equally likely sample space**.

## The same event can have different probabilities

Take $S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ and $E=\{2,4,6\}$. For a fair die, $P(E)=3/6=1/2$.

Now suppose a loaded die assigns probabilities

$$
(0.10,\ 0.10,\ 0.10,\ 0.10,\ 0.10,\ 0.50)
$$

to faces 1 through 6. The sample space and event have not changed. But

$$
P(E)=0.10+0.10+0.50=0.70.
$$

There are still three favorable faces out of six. Counting alone fails because their weights are unequal. The general finite-space rule is

$$
P(E)=\sum_{o_i\in E}P(\{o_i\}).
$$

The equally likely formula is the special case in which every summand equals $1/N$.

## Keep the level of outcomes consistent

A model can begin with equally likely elementary outcomes and then group them into categories of unequal size. Those categories need not be equally likely.

For two independent fair coin flips, $HH,HT,TH,TT$ each have probability $1/4$. The three categories “zero heads,” “one head,” and “two heads” have probabilities $1/4,1/2,1/4$, because their groups contain one, two, and one elementary outcomes. Three possible categories do not mean probability $1/3$ each.

## Check your understanding

A spinner has four labeled sectors with angles 180°, 60°, 60°, and 60°. Under a uniform-angle model, what is the probability of landing in the first sector? Why isn't it $1/4$?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Use the weights implied by the model</summary>

The first sector occupies $180/360=1/2$ of the full circle, so its probability is $1/2$. The remaining sectors each have probability $60/360=1/6$. The four labels are possible outcomes but are not equally likely. Equal angular portions, rather than unequal sectors, receive equal weights under the stated model. Boundary points have probability zero here.
