# How many possible outcomes?

A sample space is the set of all distinct outcomes of an experiment. Some spaces fit in a finite list, some require an endless list, and some are too rich to fit in any such list at all.

## Finite, countably infinite, uncountably infinite

| Type | Example | What “listing” means |
|---|---|---|
| Finite | One die: $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ | A list that ends |
| Countably infinite | Trial number of the first head: $\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$ | An endless list that includes every element |
| Uncountably infinite | An exact position in $[0,1]$ | No endless numbered list includes every real value |

The standard waiting-time model records a finite trial number for the first success. For independent tosses with positive head probability, the possibility of never seeing a head has probability zero; one can include an additional $\infty$ outcome in a more complete construction without making the space uncountable.

## Bounded does not mean finite

The interval $[0,1]$ includes $0.5$, $1/3$, $\sqrt2/2$, and every other real value between its endpoints. It contains uncountably many values despite having length one.

Likewise, all exact angles between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ form an uncountable set. By contrast, a device that rounds angles to the nearest whole degree records only finitely many outputs. The continuous physical model and the rounded measurement have different outcome spaces.

## Decimals alone do not decide the type

Rational numbers, the numbers expressible as fractions of integers, are countably infinite. A clever ordering can list them all, despite their density along the number line. The uncountability of the real interval comes from including all real numbers, not merely from having infinitely many fractions or arbitrarily close values.

Later, probabilities on a discrete space can be described with point masses. Continuous models often describe probabilities by density over regions. Cardinality itself does not force a particular probability law: an uncountable space can carry distributions with atoms, so “uncountable” and “has a density” are not synonyms.
