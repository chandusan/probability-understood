# From the data we saw to the data we could see

[Data Summary](../slides/01.html) taught us to describe one collection of observations. Probability asks what might happen before we collect the next one. The bridge is variation: a different sample can give a different mean, standard deviation, or proportion, even when the underlying population stays the same.

## One dataset, then a new question

Recall the eight heart rates from our [worked Data Summary exercise](../slides/29.html):

$$50,51,57,61,70,71,80,84.$$

Their mean is $65.5$ and their sample standard deviation is approximately $12.8$. These are descriptions of those eight students. If we randomly select eight other students, we will probably get different numbers.

Now we need a language for questions such as: how much might the mean change? How close is our sample mean to the population mean? Will a larger sample improve the estimate?

| Part of the story | Central question |
|---|---|
| Descriptive statistics | What does this observed sample look like? |
| Probability | Under a specified model, what outcomes could occur, and how likely are they? |
| Statistical inference | Given the observations, what can we conclude about the population, with what uncertainty? |

Probability supplies the mathematics that makes inference possible. It studies the consequences of an assumed model; inference uses observations to learn about the model or its unknown parameters.

## A proportion has two roles

In the heart-rate data, three values exceed $70$: $71,80,84$. The observed proportion is $3/8=37.5\%$.

Let $A$ mean “a randomly selected student's heart rate exceeds $70$.” Then $P(A)$ concerns the selection process and population. The number $3/8$ describes our sample; using it to estimate $P(A)$ requires a suitable sampling model. If the eight students were selected from a running club, they may not represent all students.

Our route through this chapter is therefore: describe the experiment, identify possible outcomes, collect interesting outcomes into events, and attach probabilities consistently. Means and variances will return as properties of random quantities, including sample means themselves.

## Optional review: recover two missing observations

This review exercise is a useful bridge back to the previous chapter. Five values have sample mean $104$ and sample variance $16$. Three are $102,100,105$. Find the other two.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Use the mean and variance together</summary>

Call the missing values $a,b$. The mean fixes their sum:

$$a+b=5(104)-(102+100+105)=213.$$

The sample variance uses $n-1=4$, so the total squared deviation is $4(16)=64$. The known values contribute

$$(102-104)^2+(100-104)^2+(105-104)^2=4+16+1=21.$$

Thus $(a-104)^2+(b-104)^2=43$. Their average is $106.5$, so write $a=106.5+t$, $b=106.5-t$. Substituting gives

$$(2.5+t)^2+(2.5-t)^2=12.5+2t^2=43.$$

Therefore $t^2=61/4$, and the missing values, in either order, are

$$\boxed{\frac{213-\sqrt{61}}2,\quad\frac{213+\sqrt{61}}2}$$

or approximately $102.595$ and $110.405$. Their sum determines their center; the variance determines how far apart they must be. There is no requirement that observations be integers.

</details>

## Check your understanding

Does observing three heart rates above $70$ out of eight prove that exactly $37.5\%$ of all students have heart rates above $70$?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

No. It establishes the sample proportion. It can be a point estimate of the population probability under an appropriate sampling model, but another sample could give a different proportion. Probability helps quantify that sampling variation. The uncertainty concerns what the sample tells us about the population; the arithmetic $3/8$ itself is exact.

</details>
