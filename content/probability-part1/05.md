# Unpredictable tosses, stable proportions

A single coin toss can remain uncertain while the proportion of heads becomes increasingly stable across many tosses. Probability connects those two scales: it describes the behavior of individual trials and the regularity of their accumulation.

## First name the model

For one toss, a simple sample space is

$$S=\{H,T\}.$$

Assume a fair coin and independent tosses. Then $P(H)=P(T)=1/2$, and the outcome of one toss does not change the chances on the next.

Let $H_n$ be the number of heads in $n$ tosses. The relative frequency is

$$\widehat p_n=\frac{H_n}{n}.$$

The law of large numbers says that under this model, $\widehat p_n$ approaches $1/2$ as the number of tosses grows. It does not require an exact half at any particular finite stopping point.

## The coin does not repair the past

If the first five tosses are heads, the next toss still has probability $1/2$ of heads. The coin has no balancing mechanism. A long run becomes proportionally less important as more observations are added.

For example, an excess of five heads changes a proportion by $5/10$ when the denominator is ten, but by only $5/10{,}000$ when the denominator is ten thousand. New randomness continues, yet the denominator grows too.

The absolute difference $H_n-n/2$ need not shrink. Its typical size grows roughly like $\sqrt n$, while the *fractional* difference shrinks roughly like $1/\sqrt n$. This is why “closer in proportion” does not mean “fewer excess heads.”

## Try a sequence, then start over

Add one toss and watch how strongly the earliest outcomes move the proportion. Add larger batches and compare the line with the model probability. Start a new run to see a different path. ## What does the slide mean by regularity?

The slide says that probability “does not depend on interpretation.” A careful operational reading is that once an experiment and a probability model are specified, the model has definite mathematical consequences. Deciding that a coin is fair, choosing which information to condition on, and interpreting probability in applications still require judgment.

A $50\%$ long-run claim requires the fair-coin assumption. An unfair coin with independent tosses and $P(H)=0.7$ instead has relative frequencies stabilizing near $0.7$. Independence and a stable probability are also substantive assumptions, not consequences of calling an experiment random.

After $100$ fair independent tosses, there have been $60$ heads. What is the probability of heads on toss $101$? Must the next $100$ tosses contain exactly $40$ heads?
