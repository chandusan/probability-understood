# Set laws are rules about who gets admitted

A set identity says that two different-looking membership tests accept exactly the same outcomes. We prove it by checking an arbitrary outcome, rather than relying on a particular drawing or example.

## The full collection of laws

All complements below are relative to the same sample space $S$.

| Law | Identities |
|---|---|
| Commutative | $A\cup B=B\cup A$; $A\cap B=B\cap A$ |
| Associative | $A\cup(B\cup C)=(A\cup B)\cup C$; $A\cap(B\cap C)=(A\cap B)\cap C$ |
| Distributive | $A\cap(B\cup C)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)$ |
| Other distributive law | $A\cup(B\cap C)=(A\cup B)\cap(A\cup C)$ |
| Idempotent | $A\cup A=A$; $A\cap A=A$ |
| Double complement | $(A^c)^c=A$ |
| De Morgan | $(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c$; $(A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c$ |
| Complement or inverse laws | $A\cup A^c=S$; $A\cap A^c=\varnothing$ |

The source explicitly lists union associativity; intersection has the corresponding rule shown here. Notice the bar over the **whole** union or intersection in De Morgan's laws. Negating the whole test is different from negating just one event.

## Why the simple laws work

Commutativity says that checking “$A$ or $B$” gives the same answer as “$B$ or $A$.” The analogous statement holds for “and.” Order of checking does not change membership.

Associativity says that grouping three “or” tests differently still asks whether at least one succeeds. Grouping three “and” tests still asks whether all three succeed. Parentheses can move when the connector is the same; they cannot be moved indiscriminately when union and intersection are mixed.

Repeating one test adds no information: “$A$ or $A$” and “$A$ and $A$” both reduce to $A$. That is idempotence. An outcome is outside the outside of $A$ precisely when it is in $A$, giving double complementation.

Finally, an outcome is either in $A$ or outside it, but cannot be both. That proves the two complement laws.

## Distribute “and” across “or”

Consider a customer who must be eligible ($A$) and must have either a membership ($B$) or a coupon ($C$). This is equivalent to qualifying through the eligible-member route or the eligible-coupon route.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Prove the first distributive law element by element</summary>

Take any $e\in S$. Then

$$
e\in A\cap(B\cup C)
$$

means $e\in A$ and either $e\in B$ or $e\in C$. If the first alternative holds, $e\in A\cap B$; if the second holds, $e\in A\cap C$. Thus $e$ belongs to the right-hand union.

Conversely, if $e$ belongs to $(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)$, it belongs to $A$ and to at least one of $B,C$. Therefore it belongs to $A\cap(B\cup C)$.

Both sides have exactly the same elements, proving the identity.

</details>

## Distribute “or” across “and”

The second identity can feel less familiar:

$$
A\cup(B\cap C)=(A\cup B)\cap(A\cup C).
$$

<details markdown="1"><summary>Prove the second distributive law by two cases</summary>

Take an arbitrary outcome $e$.

If $e\in A$, it belongs to the left side immediately. It also belongs to both $A\cup B$ and $A\cup C$, so it belongs to the right side.

If $e\notin A$, the left side accepts it only when it belongs to both $B$ and $C$. On the right, $A$ can no longer help either union, so the first union requires $B$ and the second requires $C$. Again, both sides accept exactly when $e\in B\cap C$.

The two cases cover every outcome, proving equality.

</details>

## Negating changes “or” into “and”

To be outside $A\cup B$, an outcome must fail both event tests. To be outside $A\cap B$, it only needs to fail at least one.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Prove both De Morgan laws</summary>

For any $e\in S$,

$$
e\in(A\cup B)^c
\iff e\notin A\cup B
\iff(e\notin A\text{ and }e\notin B)
\iff e\in A^c\cap B^c.
$$

Similarly,

$$
e\in(A\cap B)^c
\iff e\notin A\cap B
\iff(e\notin A\text{ or }e\notin B)
\iff e\in A^c\cup B^c.
$$

The “or” in the second proof is inclusive. An outcome outside both sets certainly fails the condition of belonging to both.

</details>

## Why an example is a check, not a proof

Trying a few die sets can reveal a mistake, but a correct result for those sets does not establish a law for every set. The arbitrary-element arguments above work without choosing special sets or special outcomes, which is what makes them proofs.

## Check your understanding

Is $(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cup B^c$ correct? Use an outcome in $A$ but outside $B$ to test it, then write the correct law.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the counterexample</summary>

Such an outcome is in $A\cup B$, so it is not in its complement. But it is in $B^c$, so it does belong to $A^c\cup B^c$. The two sides differ. The correct law is $(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c$.

</details>
