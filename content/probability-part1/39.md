# Three rules make probability coherent

Sets describe which outcomes count as an event. A probability measure supplies a number describing how much probability that event receives. The three axioms ensure those numbers fit together: no negative probability, one whole unit in the sample space, and no double-counting when disjoint pieces are combined.

## Start with a sample space and its events

Let S contain the possible outcomes of a random phenomenon. An event E is an allowed subset of S, and $P(E)$ is its probability. The same sample space can support different probability models: a fair die and a loaded die both have outcomes 1 through 6 but assign different weights.

The axioms are:

1. **Nonnegativity:** $P(E)\ge0$ for every event E.
2. **Normalization:** $P(S)=1$.
3. **Countable additivity:** if $E_1,E_2,\ldots$ are pairwise disjoint events, then

$$
P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_i\right)
=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(E_i).
$$

“Pairwise disjoint” means $E_i\cap E_j=\varnothing$ whenever $i\ne j$. No outcome belongs to two different pieces, so adding their probabilities does not count any probability twice.

## Disjoint means “at most one,” not “exactly one”

On a die, let $A=\{1,2\}$ and $B=\{5,6\}$. They are disjoint: one roll cannot lie in both. But a roll of 3 lies in neither. Disjointness does not require the events to cover S.

For a fair die, $P(A\cup B)=2/6+2/6=4/6$. If instead $C=\{2,3\}$, then A and C overlap at 2. Adding $P(A)+P(C)$ counts that outcome twice; their union is $\{1,2,3\}$, with probability $3/6$, not $4/6$.

Disjointness and independence are different ideas. The first is about overlap between sets; the second is about how probabilities relate. Positive-probability disjoint events cannot be independent, since knowing one happened rules out the other.

## Useful rules follow from the axioms

The impossible event has probability zero:

$$
P(\varnothing)=0.
$$

An event and its complement partition S into disjoint pieces, so

$$
P(E)+P(E^c)=1,
\qquad P(E^c)=1-P(E).
$$

If $A\subseteq B$, then $B=A\cup(B\setminus A)$ with disjoint pieces. Hence

$$
P(B)=P(A)+P(B\setminus A)\ge P(A).
$$

This is **monotonicity**: containing an event cannot make its probability smaller. In particular, $\varnothing\subseteq E\subseteq S$ gives $0\le P(E)\le1$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Why the empty-event and finite-addition rules really follow</summary>

Apply countable additivity to the pairwise disjoint sequence $S,\varnothing,\varnothing,\ldots$. Its union is S, so

$$
1=1+\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}P(\varnothing).
$$

Nonnegative summands force $P(\varnothing)=0$. For finitely many disjoint events, append empty events forever and apply the countable axiom. The added terms contribute zero, leaving the finite-addition rule used above.

For complements, $E\cap E^c=\varnothing$ and $E\cup E^c=S$, so the finite-addition rule gives $1=P(E)+P(E^c)$.

For monotonicity, every point of B is either inside A or in the remainder $B\setminus A$. Nonnegativity of the remainder's probability proves the inequality.

</details>

## Countable is a real restriction

A countable collection can be listed as a sequence, possibly with a finite end. Positive integers are countable. Rational numbers are also countable, although their listing needs more care. The real interval $[0,1]$ is uncountable: no sequence lists all its points. A bounded interval need not be a finite or countable set.

Imagine a uniformly chosen point in $[0,1]$. Every particular point has probability zero, yet the whole interval has probability one. There is no contradiction:

$$
[0,1]=\bigcup_{x\in[0,1]}\{x\}
$$

is an **uncountable** union. The axiom does not allow replacing its probability with a countable sum of singleton probabilities. In this model, any countable collection of specified points does have probability zero. Probability zero is therefore not the same as being an impossible, empty event.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>A light note about which subsets are events</summary>

For our finite sample spaces, every subset can be treated as an event. In more advanced continuous models, P is defined on a chosen collection of measurable subsets, called a sigma-algebra. This collection includes S and is closed under complements and countable unions, so the operations in the axioms remain legitimate.

De Morgan's laws are set identities even for uncountable families. That does not imply every uncountable union of measurable sets is measurable, or that countable additivity extends to such unions. We will use ordinary intervals and other familiar measurable events here.

</details>

## Check your understanding

Suppose $A\subseteq B$, $P(A)=0.35$, and $P(B)=0.60$. Find $P(B\setminus A)$ and $P(B^c)$. Can A and B be disjoint?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Partition B, then complement it</summary>

The disjoint partition gives $P(B\setminus A)=0.60-0.35=0.25$. Also, $P(B^c)=1-0.60=0.40$. Since $A\subseteq B$, their intersection is A, which has probability 0.35 and is nonempty. Thus they cannot be disjoint.

</details>
