# A prediction can be a distribution

When the same recorded inputs leave several outcomes possible, predicting one number is only part of the story. A stochastic model describes how probability is distributed across those possible outcomes.

## Same information, different outcomes

Imagine two customers with the same recorded account age and recent activity. One renews; the other leaves. The available inputs do not determine the outcome. We model

$$P(Y\in A\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}),$$

the chance that $Y$ belongs to a specified collection $A$, given the information $\mathbf{x}$.

A common numerical model is

$$Y=f(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon.$$

Here $f(\mathbf{x})$ gives a central prediction and $\varepsilon$ is the remaining deviation. This additive form is convenient, but stochastic models need not all have this form. A churn outcome is binary, for example, and is often modeled directly by a probability of leaving.

## Why the mean returns

If the goal is to predict a numerical $Y$ using squared error, the best population-level constant at a given $\mathbf{x}$ is

$$f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}].$$

This is the previous chapter's [“mean minimizes squared error” principle](../slides/04.html) applied to the distribution of possible responses at the same inputs. It does not say the conditional mean will actually occur. A forecast of $2.4$ clicks can be sensible even though the observed count is an integer.

Other aims call for other summaries: a conditional median for absolute error, a class probability for a yes/no decision, or an entire predictive distribution when tail risks matter.

## What does irreducible error mean?

If

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})>0,$$

then no single prediction based only on these inputs can equal the outcome every time. Under squared error, the remaining conditional variance is the minimum attainable expected error at that input.

The phrase **based only on these inputs** matters. Adding information can reduce uncertainty. Unobserved variables and measurement limitations can make a deterministic process appear stochastic. Chaotic dynamics are not, by themselves, proof of intrinsic randomness. The slide's examples—churn, economic forecasts, and language generation—motivate probabilistic modeling; they do not establish that every underlying mechanism is fundamentally random.

A model can also deliberately introduce randomness, as when a language generator samples its next token from a distribution. Distinguish that mechanism from uncertainty about what a human will write.

## Check your understanding

Two equally likely outcomes are $0$ and $10$. The squared-error prediction is $5$. Is predicting $5$ a failure because it is never observed?

No. Its expected squared error is $(25+25)/2=25$. Predicting $0$ gives $(0+100)/2=50$, and predicting $10$ does the same. The best point prediction balances errors; it need not be a possible individual outcome. Reporting the two-point distribution tells the fuller story that the mean alone hides.
