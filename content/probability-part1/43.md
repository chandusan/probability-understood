# From describing data to reasoning about uncertainty

This chapter ends with a practical promise: when a finite experiment has equally likely elementary outcomes, probability becomes a counting problem. But the deeper skill is choosing a model that makes the counting meaningful—and understanding how that model connects back to observed data.

## The chapter's counting recipe

The source recap requires three ingredients:

1. A finite sample space S with N elementary outcomes.
2. A justified assumption that all those outcomes are equally likely.
3. An event E translated into the outcomes that satisfy it.

Then

$$
P(E)=\frac{n(E)}{n(S)}
=\frac{n(E)}N
=\frac{\text{number of favorable elementary outcomes}}
{\text{total number of elementary outcomes}}.
$$

The notation $n(A)$ means the number of elements of A. A well-specified probability question therefore asks us to count two things: the size of the full space and the size of the event. It also asks us to avoid counting an outcome twice.

The cars example used ordered turn sequences. The dice example used ordered face pairs. Their grouped summaries—number of right turns, or total on the dice—were not automatically equally likely.

## Keep three different jobs separate

| Job | Starting point | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Describe data | Measurements already observed | What center, spread, and shape does this sample have? |
| Model probability | Assumptions about possible outcomes and their weights | What might happen, and with what probability? |
| Make an inference | Observed data plus assumptions about how they were collected | What can we learn about a larger population, and how uncertain is that conclusion? |

The first PDF focused on the first job. This PDF builds the language for the second. Those tools allow us to connect data to uncertain conclusions rather than treating one observed sample as the whole population.

## Follow the heart-rate example across the bridge

The eight observed heart rates from the data-summary lesson were

$$
50,51,57,61,70,71,80,84.
$$

Their mean is $65.5$, and their sample standard deviation is approximately $12.8$. These are descriptions of the eight values we actually saw.

Exactly three values—71, 80, and 84—exceed 70. The **observed sample proportion** is therefore $3/8=37.5\%$. The value 70 itself does not count because the condition is strictly greater than 70.

Now consider a different question: “What is the probability that a randomly selected student from the population has a heart rate above 70?” The eight measurements alone do not reveal that probability exactly. Using $3/8$ as an estimate is an inference, whose quality depends on how the students were sampled and how representative the observations are.

If we instead choose uniformly from these **eight recorded observations**, the probability of selecting a value above 70 is exactly $3/8$ under that specified experiment. The same fraction can be an exact probability in one experiment and an uncertain estimate in another.

## Repeated samples explain why probability matters

A new group of eight students will generally produce a different mean and proportion. Probability models describe that variation: how sample summaries can change, how close they tend to be to population quantities, and how sample size affects their uncertainty under suitable assumptions.

A larger sample does not guarantee that its particular estimate is closer to the truth. It can reduce typical sampling variability in an appropriate model. That distinction—what is guaranteed for this sample versus what happens across repeated samples—is central to sound statistical reasoning.

The source's next-step message is to develop the ability to count $n(E)$ and $n(S)$. Keep that concrete task in view while preserving the broader connection: observed summaries describe what happened; a probability model helps us reason about what could happen.

## Check your understanding

You observe 6 heads in 10 coin flips. Is the probability of heads now exactly 0.6? What probability is exactly 0.6 if you choose uniformly from the ten recorded results?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Distinguish the coin model from resampling the record</summary>

The observed proportion of heads is $6/10=0.6$. It can estimate an unknown heads probability, but it does not prove that the coin's probability equals 0.6. Under a stipulated fair independent model, the next flip still has heads probability 0.5.

If you choose one of the ten recorded results uniformly, six of those ten equally likely positions contain heads. In that resampling experiment, the probability of selecting a recorded head is exactly 0.6. The sample space and probability model determine which claim is justified.
