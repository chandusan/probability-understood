# Negation swaps “at least one” with “every”

De Morgan's laws are rules for translating a negative statement without changing its meaning. **“None of the events happen”** means every event fails. **“Not all events happen”** means at least one event fails. This distinction survives any number of events.

## Two laws, two different statements

Let $\{A_i:i\in I\}$ be a family of subsets of the same sample space S. The index set I labels the events. It can be finite, countably infinite, or uncountably infinite.

$$
\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i\right)^c
=\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i^c,
$$

$$
\left(\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i\right)^c
=\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i^c.
$$

The complement is always relative to S. Moving a complement through a union or intersection changes the operation **and** complements every participating set.

For a concrete story, suppose $A_i$ means “alarm i detects the problem.” The complement of the union is “no alarm detects it”: every alarm fails. The complement of the intersection is “not every alarm detects it”: one failure already qualifies, even if the other alarms succeed.

## Watch one outcome travel through the logic

The first identity follows from this chain, valid for any $x\in S$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x\in\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i\right)^c
&\Longleftrightarrow x\notin\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\text{there is no }i\in I\text{ with }x\in A_i\\
&\Longleftrightarrow x\notin A_i\text{ for every }i\in I\\
&\Longleftrightarrow x\in\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i^c.
\end{aligned}
$$

Every arrow works both ways, so this establishes equality, not merely one inclusion. The logical rule is $\neg\exists=\forall\neg$: “not even one succeeds” means “every one fails.”

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Prove the second identity with the same method</summary>

For an arbitrary $x\in S$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
x\in\left(\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i\right)^c
&\Longleftrightarrow x\notin\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\text{it is not true that }x\in A_i\text{ for every }i\in I\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\text{there exists }i\in I\text{ with }x\notin A_i\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\text{there exists }i\in I\text{ with }x\in A_i^c\\
&\Longleftrightarrow x\in\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i^c.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here the logical rule is $\neg\forall=\exists\neg$. To fail a requirement that every condition holds, at least one condition must fail. The argument does not depend on listing the indices one by one, which is why it also works for uncountable I.

</details>

## See both laws on eight outcomes

Start with $S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8\}$ and

$$
A=\{1,2,3,4\},\quad B=\{3,4,5,6\},\quad C=\{2,4,6,8\}.
$$

The union contains every outcome except 7. Thus

$$
(A\cup B\cup C)^c=A^c\cap B^c\cap C^c=\{7\}.
$$

The intersection contains only 4. Consequently,

$$
(A\cap B\cap C)^c=A^c\cup B^c\cup C^c
=\{1,2,3,5,6,7,8\}.
$$

The two resulting sets are very different: “none” and “not all” are not interchangeable.

## Check your understanding

A course completion event requires all three assignments submitted. What event means the course is incomplete? Does it require all three assignments to be missing?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Negate the whole requirement</summary>

Let $A_i$ mean assignment i is submitted. Completion is $A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3$, so incompletion is $A_1^c\cup A_2^c\cup A_3^c$: at least one assignment is missing. All three missing would be $A_1^c\cap A_2^c\cap A_3^c$, a smaller event. Someone missing just the second assignment is already incomplete.

</details>
