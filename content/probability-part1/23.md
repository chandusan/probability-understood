# Intersection keeps the shared outcomes

An intersection is a stricter filter than a union. An outcome must pass both membership tests at once. The overlap of two circles represents outcomes for which two statements are simultaneously true in the same experiment.

## Read “and” literally

The definition is

$$
A\cap B=\{e\in S\mid e\in A\text{ and }e\in B\}.
$$

For a die, suppose $A=\{2,4,6\}$ means “even” and $B=\{1,2,3\}$ means “less than 4.” Only 2 passes both tests:

$$
A\cap B=\{2\}.
$$

Four belongs to $A$ but fails $B$; one belongs to $B$ but fails $A$. Membership in only one event is insufficient.

## “Both occur” does not mean two outcomes were produced

A single roll of 2 makes both “even” and “less than 4” true. The experiment still produced one outcome. We are describing two properties of that same outcome.

For a richer experiment, the elementary outcome may itself have several coordinates. If we observe two cars, an outcome could record both their directions. The event “first car turns right” intersected with “second car turns right” selects the joint outcome $RR$. The word “joint” refers to simultaneous conditions, not a new rule that events must be independent.

## Event probability is not density

Once a probability model is assigned, $P(A\cap B)$ is the probability that both conditions hold. The slide calls this a “joint probability mass or density,” but **the probability of an event and a density are different quantities**.

In a discrete model, obtain the intersection's probability by adding the probability masses of its outcomes. In a continuous model with joint density $f(x,y)$, obtain it by integrating over its region:

$$
P((X,Y)\in A\cap B)=\iint_{A\cap B}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.
$$

The density's height is not itself the probability of the intersection. As in our angle discussion, probability comes from density accumulated over a region.

Nor may we automatically write $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$. That multiplication requires independence, a condition to establish rather than infer from the word “and.”

## Check your understanding

Let $A=\{1,2,3\}$ and $B=\{3,4,5\}$ for a fair six-sided die. Find the intersection and its probability. Does one roll have to produce two numbers for both events to occur?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the answer</summary>

$A\cap B=\{3\}$, so its probability is $1/6$ under the fair-die model. A single result of 3 makes both event descriptions true. There is no need for two numbers or two rolls.

</details>
