# Two dice: count pairs before counting sums

The sum of two dice hides information about how that sum was produced. A total of 12 has one route, while a total of 7 has six. To use equal-outcome counting correctly, start with the two individual results, not a list of possible sums.

## Build the sample space as a grid

Label the dice first and second, or give them different colors. An outcome is an ordered pair $(i,j)$, where $i,j\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$:

$$
S=\{(i,j):1\le i\le6,\ 1\le j\le6\},
\qquad n(S)=6\times6=36.
$$

The source picture displays exactly this six-by-six grid of face pairs. Assuming the dice are fair **and independent**, every pair has probability

$$
P(\{(i,j)\})=\frac16\cdot\frac16=\frac1{36}.
$$

Fairness gives each die's individual probabilities. Independence is what lets us multiply them for the pair. It is the standard intended model for separately rolled fair dice.

## Find every pair with sum at least ten

The requested event is

$$
A=\{(i,j):i+j\ge10\}.
$$

“Greater than or equal to” includes the boundary total 10. Grouping the favorable ordered pairs by their sums gives:

| Sum | Favorable ordered pairs | Count |
|---|---|---:|
| 10 | $(4,6),(5,5),(6,4)$ | 3 |
| 11 | $(5,6),(6,5)$ | 2 |
| 12 | $(6,6)$ | 1 |

Thus

$$
P(A)=\frac{3+2+1}{36}=\frac6{36}=\frac16\approx0.1667.
$$

Pairs such as $(4,6)$ and $(6,4)$ are different outcomes because the first die has a different value, even though the sums agree.

## Why “three favorable sums out of eleven” fails

The possible sums are 2 through 12: eleven values. Three of them satisfy the event. But the fraction $3/11$ would assume those eleven sums were equally likely. They are not.

The numbers of ordered pairs for the successive sums are

$$
1,2,3,4,5,6,5,4,3,2,1.
$$

They total 36. For example, sum 2 can only come from $(1,1)$, whereas sum 7 can come from $(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)$.

It is valid to work with the smaller sample space of sums **if** you give each sum its correct weight. Then the answer is $3/36+2/36+1/36$, not $3/11$.

## Check your understanding

Under the same model, find the probability of a sum strictly greater than 10. Then find the probability of a sum less than 10.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Separate the boundary from the complement</summary>

Strictly greater than 10 includes only sums 11 and 12, with $2+1=3$ ordered pairs. Its probability is $3/36=1/12$.

A sum less than 10 is the complement of the original event $\{\text{sum}\ge10\}$. Its probability is $1-6/36=30/36=5/6$. Notice that “less than 10” and “greater than 10” are not complements: together they omit the three outcomes with sum exactly 10.
