# Three ways the future can be hard to know

The slide places deterministic rules, stochastic systems, and unpredictable chaos side by side. Read the picture as a comparison of mechanisms and predictability, rather than three mutually exclusive boxes.

## Read the three panels

The left panel shows gears, a trajectory, and a physical law. It represents a rule-based system: a complete state evolves according to a fixed rule. The center shows dice, multiple paths, and a distribution, representing a model with several possible outcomes. The right shows a Lorenz-style attractor and emphasizes sensitivity to initial conditions.

The most important distinction is this: **a fixed rule can produce a future that is hard to predict from imperfect measurements**. Chaos is compatible with determinism.

| Question | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Is the model deterministic? | Does a complete specified state have one resulting trajectory? |
| Is the model stochastic? | Does the model assign probabilities to possible trajectories or outcomes? |
| Is the model chaotic? | Can nearby initial states separate dramatically over time? |
| Can we predict accurately? | Are our measurements, model, and computation sufficient for the forecast? |

These questions do different jobs. The left panel's “perfectly predictable” description is an idealization, not a promise about every deterministic system.

## A small error can become a big disagreement

Suppose two weather models start from temperatures that differ by only a tiny amount. If the dynamics amplify that difference, their long-term forecasts can diverge. There need not be a random coin toss hidden in the equations. The uncertainty can arise because we never knew the exact initial state.

Probability remains useful: instead of pretending one starting state is exact, we can model a range of plausible states and examine the range of resulting forecasts. A distribution can describe our information even when the evolution of each individual state is deterministic.

## Keep the questions separate

For each example, ask first what is fixed, then what varies, then what is observed. A deterministic unit conversion may process noisy measurements. A stochastic simulation may be generated by a deterministic pseudorandom algorithm. The scientific model, the implementation, and our knowledge of its inputs are related but distinct.
