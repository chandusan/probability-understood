# Random chords need a sampling rule

Two chords cross inside a circle exactly when their endpoints alternate as we travel around its boundary. That geometric fact solves the problem once “random chord” has been given a precise meaning.

## Specify the experiment before answering

The slide asks for the probability that chords $AB$ and $CD$ intersect inside the circle. It does not specify how they are sampled. Different ways of generating random chords can give different distributions, so the wording alone does not determine a unique numerical answer.

We will use this explicit model: choose $A,B,C,D$ independently and uniformly on the circumference, then pair $A$ with $B$ and $C$ with $D$. Uniform means equal-length arcs have equal probabilities. Exact coincidences of endpoints have probability zero.

Under this model, the answer is

$$\boxed{P(AB\text{ crosses }CD)=\frac13.}$$

## See the alternating-endpoint rule

The endpoints $A,B$ split the circle into two arcs. The second chord crosses the first precisely when one of $C,D$ lies on each arc. If both lie on the same arc, their joining segment stays on the same side of $AB$.

The diagram above shows a crossing example: walking around the circle meets the labels in alternating chord order, such as $A,C,B,D$.

## Three pairings, one crossing

Imagine four distinct boundary points, temporarily labeled by their cyclic positions $1,2,3,4$. There are three ways to split them into two unordered pairs:

| Pairing | What the segments do |
|---|---|
| $(1,2)$ and $(3,4)$ | Do not cross inside |
| $(1,3)$ and $(2,4)$ | Cross inside |
| $(1,4)$ and $(2,3)$ | Do not cross inside |

Independent uniform endpoint labels make these three pairing patterns equally likely. Exactly one crosses, giving $1/3$.

This proof is about the distribution of endpoint labels. We cannot transfer the result to an unspecified chord-generating mechanism merely because the drawing looks similar.

## Try repeated endpoint sampling

Generate one pair and inspect whether the endpoints alternate. Then generate batches. The running fraction will fluctuate around $1/3$; it need not equal $1/3$ after a small number of trials. Every trial in this experiment uses four independent uniform endpoint angles.

<summary>Derive the same answer from arc lengths</summary>

Fix $A$ by rotating the picture. Let $U$ be the clockwise fraction of the circumference from $A$ to $B$. Since $B$ is uniform, $U$ is uniform on $[0,1]$.

Given $U=u$, one arc occupies fraction $u$ and the other occupies $1-u$. A crossing occurs in two disjoint ways: $C$ lands on the first arc and $D$ on the second, or vice versa. Independence gives

$$P(\text{crossing}\mid U=u)=u(1-u)+(1-u)u=2u(1-u).$$

Averaging over $u$ gives

$$P(\text{crossing})=\int_0^1 2u(1-u)\,du
=\left[u^2-\frac23u^3\right]_0^1=\frac13.$$

When $AB$ is nearly a diameter, the two arcs are similar in length and crossing is more likely. When $A$ and $B$ are close, one arc is tiny and a crossing is less likely. The integral accounts for both types of first chord.

## Check your understanding

Suppose the first chord is deliberately fixed as a diameter, and the second has two independent uniform endpoints. Is the crossing probability still $1/3$?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

No. Each semicircle has probability $1/2$. The two endpoints must fall in different semicircles, so the probability is $2(1/2)(1/2)=1/2$. The first chord is no longer sampled by the original four-endpoint experiment. Changing the mechanism changes the answer.
