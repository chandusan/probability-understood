# Containment is an order with branches

An event is a set of outcomes. Saying **“A is contained in B”** means that whenever A happens, B must happen too. That implication creates an order among events—even though some pairs of events have no containment relationship at all.

## Read inclusion as a guarantee

We write

$$
A\subseteq B
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{for every }e\in S,\ e\in A\Rightarrow e\in B.
$$

Equivalently, $B\supseteq A$: B is a superset of A. In the slide's picture, the entire circle A lies inside B. No part of A sticks out. For a die roll, $A=\{2\}$ is contained in $B=\{2,4,6\}$. Rolling a 2 guarantees an even result; an even result does not guarantee a 2.

The slide uses $\subset$ for inclusion that allows equality. We use $\subseteq$ to make that convention explicit. A **proper** subset, written $A\subsetneq B$, additionally requires $A\ne B$.

## How can containment count as an order?

“Order” need not mean arranging numbers from smaller to larger. Mathematically, a **partial order** is a relation satisfying three rules:

| Rule | Meaning for containment |
|---|---|
| Reflexivity | $A\subseteq A$: every member of A belongs to A. |
| Antisymmetry | If $A\subseteq B$ and $B\subseteq A$, then $A=B$. Neither has an extra member. |
| Transitivity | If $A\subseteq B$ and $B\subseteq C$, then $A\subseteq C$. Every member of A passes through B into C. |

For example, $\{1\}\subseteq\{1,2\}\subseteq\{1,2,3\}$ is a chain. Moving upward adds possible outcomes without losing any. Antisymmetry does **not** say “the relation never works both ways”: it says that working both ways forces equality.

A **total order** also requires every pair to be comparable. For any A and B, either $A\subseteq B$ or $B\subseteq A$ must hold. Containment does not have this property on a general power set.

## The smallest diamond shows why

For $S=\{1,2\}$, the power set—the collection of every subset—is

$$
\mathcal P(S)=\{\varnothing,\{1\},\{2\},\{1,2\}\}.
$$

Picture a diamond: $\varnothing$ at the bottom; $\{1\}$ and $\{2\}$ side by side; $\{1,2\}$ at the top. The bottom is contained in both middle sets, and both middle sets are contained in the top. The middle sets are incomparable.

**But isn't 1 less than 2?** Yes: $1<2$ compares two numbers. It says nothing about whether the set $\{1\}$ is contained in the set $\{2\}$. The number 1 is missing from $\{2\}$, and 2 is missing from $\{1\}$. Therefore neither set contains the other. An order on elements and an order on sets are different relations.

## Check your understanding

Let $A=\{1,3\}$, $B=\{1,2,3\}$, and $C=\{2,4\}$. Which of A and B guarantees the other? Are A and C comparable by inclusion?

<summary>Work through the membership tests</summary>

Every member of A belongs to B, so $A\subseteq B$: A happening guarantees B. The reverse fails because $2\in B$ but $2\notin A$.

A and C are incomparable. For example, $1\in A$ but $1\notin C$, while $2\in C$ but $2\notin A$. One counterexample is enough to disprove each proposed inclusion. The fact that B has more elements than A is consistent with inclusion, but counting elements alone would not prove it.
