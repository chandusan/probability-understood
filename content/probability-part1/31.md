# Disjoint means at most one can occur

Disjoint events have no outcomes in common. A single trial therefore cannot make both events happen. It can still make neither happen, which is the distinction behind your question about “mutually exclusive.”

## The definition

Events $A$ and $B$ are **disjoint**, or **mutually exclusive**, when

$$
A\cap B=\varnothing.
$$

For one die roll, take

$$
A=\{1,2\},\qquad B=\{5,6\}.
$$

There is no shared face, so the events are disjoint. A result of 3 makes neither event occur. Hence the definition means **at most one**, not **exactly one**.

For exactly one of two disjoint events to occur on every trial, they must also cover the whole sample space:

$$
A\cup B=S.
$$

A complementary pair $A,A^c$ does both: no overlap and complete coverage.

## Compare with a genuine overlap

Keep $A=\{1,2\}$ but use $C=\{2,3\}$. Then

$$
A\cap C=\{2\}.
$$

The result 2 makes both events occur, so $A$ and $C$ are not disjoint. It only takes one shared outcome to defeat disjointness, regardless of how many other outcomes differ.

## Return to the three cars

The source's earlier events were

$$
A=\{LLR,LRL,RLL\}\quad\text{(exactly one right turn)},
$$

$$
C=\{LLL,RRR\}\quad\text{(all turns the same)}.
$$

They share no outcome and are therefore disjoint. Outcomes such as $LRR$ belong to neither: they have two right turns and are not all the same.

## Why disjointness simplifies addition

When events are disjoint, there is no overlap to count twice:

$$
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B).
$$

For the fair-die example $A=\{1,2\}$ and $B=\{5,6\}$, the union probability is $2/6+2/6=4/6$. The remaining $2/6$ belongs to neither event.

## Disjoint is different from independent

Disjointness is a **set relationship**: no outcome satisfies both descriptions. Independence is a **probability relationship**:

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).
$$

If two disjoint events both have positive probability, they cannot be independent. Their intersection has probability zero, but $P(A)P(B)>0$. Learning that one occurred rules the other out completely.

For example, if the die result is in $\{1,2\}$, its chance of also being in $\{5,6\}$ is zero. Before that information, the latter event had probability $1/3$ on a fair die.

Events of probability zero are a degenerate exception to the positive-probability statement. Also, an intersection with probability zero need not be literally empty in a continuous model. Disjointness requires an empty intersection, not merely a zero-probability one.

## Check your understanding

For a die, compare “even” and “odd” with “less than 3” and “greater than 4.” Which pair is disjoint? Which pair also guarantees exactly one occurs?

Both pairs are disjoint. Even and odd also cover all six faces, so exactly one occurs every roll. Less than 3 and greater than 4 leave faces 3 and 4 uncovered, so neither event occurs on those results. Coverage is an extra condition beyond disjointness.
