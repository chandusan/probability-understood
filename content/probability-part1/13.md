# An infinity you can list

Countably infinite means every outcome can be assigned its own position in one endless list. You never finish counting, but no outcome is left without a finite-numbered place.

## Decode the bijection

The slide defines a one-to-one and onto correspondence between the sample space $S$ and the positive integers $\mathbb{Z}^+=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$. This correspondence is called a **bijection**.

- One-to-one means distinct list positions name distinct outcomes.
- Onto means every outcome appears somewhere.

We can write

$$S=\{O_i:i\in\mathbb{Z}^+\},$$

where $O_i$ is the outcome at position $i$. The letter $O$ names an outcome; it is not the number zero. Some conventions include $0$ among the natural numbers; the choice of starting index does not change countability.

## Counts and waiting times

If $N$ is the number of arrivals during an interval, an idealized model may allow $N=0,1,2,\ldots$, without a fixed upper bound. We can list these by setting $O_i=i-1$.

If $T$ is the trial number of the first success, possible finite values are $1,2,3,\ldots$. Both spaces are countably infinite, although their first values differ.

The Poisson distribution is often used for counts, the geometric distribution for waiting until a first success, and the negative binomial for waiting until a specified number of successes or for related count models. Their assumptions differ. The fact that a variable is a count does not, by itself, establish any one of these distributions.

## Why infinitely many outcomes cannot all have equal positive probability

Suppose every natural number had the same probability $p$. If $p>0$, adding enough disjoint outcomes would make the total exceed $1$. If $p=0$, countable additivity gives total $0$. Neither works.

There is therefore no uniform probability distribution over all positive integers. A valid distribution on this countable space must allocate unequal masses, possibly with some zero masses. For example,

$$P(T=k)=2^{-k},\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,$$

is valid because $1/2+1/4+1/8+\cdots=1$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Why fractions are countable but real intervals are not</summary>

For positive fractions $p/q$, arrange numerator and denominator in a grid. Visit diagonals of increasing $p+q$, skipping duplicate fractions. Each positive rational appears on a finite diagonal. Add zero and negative counterparts to list all rationals.

A real interval defeats any proposed complete list. Given decimal expansions $x_1,x_2,\ldots$ in $[0,1]$, construct a new decimal whose $i$th digit is $1$ when the $i$th digit of $x_i$ is not $1$, and $2$ otherwise. Use a consistent expansion for numbers with two decimal representations. The constructed number differs from row $i$ at digit $i$, so it is absent from the list. Using only digits $1,2$ avoids the terminating-versus-repeating-$9$ ambiguity for the new number.

This diagonal argument shows why an endless list can exhaust the rationals but cannot exhaust all real values in even one bounded interval.

## Check your understanding

Does putting the integers in the order $0,1,-1,2,-2,3,-3,\ldots$ prove they are countably infinite?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

Yes. Every integer appears exactly once and at a finite position in this endless list. Negative values and the lack of a largest integer do not prevent countability. The central test is whether one numbered list can include every element.
