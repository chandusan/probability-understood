# Two rectangles, two different regions

A point belongs to a rectangle when both its coordinate conditions are true. To build an intersection, require both rectangles' conditions. To build a union, allow either rectangle to accept the point.

## Draw the source rectangles exactly

The slide defines

$$
A=\{(x,y):0<x<4,\ 0<y<3\},
$$

$$
B=\{(x,y):2<x<4,\ 2<y<4\}.
$$

$A$ is four units wide and three high. $B$ is a two-by-two rectangle in the upper-right portion of the picture. Every inequality is strict, so each rectangle excludes its own boundary. Dashed edges are a useful drawing convention.

## Find the overlap by tightening each coordinate

For $x$ to pass both tests, it must satisfy $0<x<4$ and $2<x<4$, leaving $2<x<4$.

For $y$ to pass both, it must satisfy $0<y<3$ and $2<y<4$, leaving $2<y<3$.

Thus

$$
A\cap B=\{(x,y):2<x<4,\ 2<y<3\}.
$$

The overlap is two units wide and one high, with area 2.

## Find the union without filling the missing corner

The union contains all of $A$ plus the part of $B$ extending above it. It is an L-shaped region, not the entire four-by-four square. The upper-left region is still missing.

Its exact membership condition is

$$
(x,y)\in A\cup B
$$

when

$$
(0<x<4\text{ and }0<y<3)
\quad\text{or}\quad
(2<x<4\text{ and }2<y<4).
$$

The areas are

$$
|A|=12,\quad |B|=4,\quad |A\cap B|=2,
$$

so

$$
|A\cup B|=12+4-2=14.
$$

Adding the two rectangle areas counts their overlap twice. Subtract one copy to count every point's area contribution once.

## Test points, especially on boundaries

| Point | In $A$? | In $B$? | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| $(3,2.5)$ | Yes | Yes | In the intersection and union |
| $(1,1)$ | Yes | No | In the union only |
| $(3,3.5)$ | No | Yes | In the union only |
| $(1,3.5)$ | No | No | In the missing upper-left region |
| $(3,3)$ | No | Yes | On $A$'s top edge, but inside $B$ |
| $(2,2.5)$ | Yes | No | Inside $A$, but on $B$'s left edge |
| $(4,2.5)$ | No | No | Excluded right boundary of both |

A boundary of one event may lie inside the other. Therefore “it is on an edge” is not enough information; ask whose edge.

## Area becomes probability only under a model

These area calculations do not alone define a probability experiment. If we additionally choose a point **uniformly** in $S=(0,4)\times(0,4)$, whose area is 16, then

$$
P(A\cap B)=\frac{2}{16}=\frac18,
\qquad
P(A\cup B)=\frac{14}{16}=\frac78.
$$

With a nonuniform density, regions of equal area need not have equal probabilities. The strict boundaries have zero area under this uniform model, but can still matter for membership or for other models that place positive probability on boundary points.

## Check your understanding

Does $(3,2)$ belong to the union, the intersection, both, or neither?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the boundary reasoning</summary>

It is inside $A$ because $0<3<4$ and $0<2<3$. It is not inside $B$, whose condition is $y>2$, not $y\ge2$. Therefore it belongs to the union but not the intersection.
