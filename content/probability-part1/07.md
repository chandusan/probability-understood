# Count successes, not every detail

When you roll ten dice and record only the number of sixes, you compress a complicated outcome into a count. The binomial distribution describes that count when the trials are independent and each has the same success probability.

## Turn each die into a yes/no trial

Call “shows a six” a success. For die $i$, define $I_i=1$ for a six and $I_i=0$ otherwise. Then

$$K=I_1+\cdots+I_{10}$$

counts successes. The original outcome is a ten-entry vector with $6^{10}$ possibilities. The count has only eleven possible values: $0,1,\ldots,10$.

For fair independent dice, every trial has success probability $p=1/6$. We write

$$K\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(10,1/6).$$

The symbol $\sim$ means “is distributed as”; it is not an approximation sign here.

## Build the probability of exactly two sixes

A particular pattern, such as successes on dice $1$ and $2$ and failures on the other eight, has probability

$$\left(\frac16\right)^2\left(\frac56\right)^8.$$

But there are $\binom{10}{2}=45$ ways to choose which two dice succeed. Those patterns are mutually exclusive, so their probabilities add:

$$P(K=2)=45\left(\frac16\right)^2\left(\frac56\right)^8\approx0.2907.$$

In general, for $n$ independent trials with common success probability $p$,

$$\boxed{P(K=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\quad k=0,\ldots,n.}$$

The three factors mean: choose the successful positions, make those positions succeed, and make the others fail. A success is simply the outcome we decided to count; it need not be good news.

## The average count

Each indicator has average $p$, so adding their averages gives

$$\mathbb{E}[K]=np.$$

For ten dice, this is $10/6\approx1.67$. Nobody rolls $1.67$ sixes; it is the long-run average count across repetitions. Under independence,

$$\operatorname{Var}(K)=np(1-p).$$

The variance quantifies how much the count fluctuates around its expectation.

## Translate dice into email clicks

The slide proposes clicks among $1{,}000$ emails. A binomial model is plausible when the number of emails is fixed, each contributes one yes/no response, the responses are independent, and all have the same click probability.

Actual recipients can have different propensities, and shared campaigns or social influences can induce dependence. If the probabilities differ but independence remains, the count is generally Poisson-binomial rather than ordinary binomial. The familiar formula is a consequence of assumptions, not a rule for every collection of yes/no observations.

The complement is “no sixes.” Each die avoids a six with probability $5/6$, so independence gives $P(K=0)=(5/6)^{10}$. Therefore

$$P(K\ge1)=1-\left(\frac56\right)^{10}\approx0.8385.$$

The complement bundles all positive counts into one calculation. Using $10(1/6)$ would be wrong: that is the expected count and exceeds $1$, whereas a probability cannot exceed $1$.
