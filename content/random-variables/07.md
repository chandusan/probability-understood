# Read the dice grid as a map from 36 outcomes to 11 sums

Roll two standard six-sided dice and let $X$ be the sum of their upward faces. The source grid places an ordered outcome $(i,j)$ in each cell and writes the corresponding value $i+j$ below it. It is a picture of the random variable itself: many different cells can lead to the same numerical sum.

## Label the dice before making the sample space

Call them Die 1 and Die 2, perhaps using different colors. An outcome $(i,j)$ records the result on Die 1 first and Die 2 second. There are six choices for each coordinate, giving

$$S=\{1,\ldots,6\}\times\{1,\ldots,6\},\qquad |S|=36.$$

The mapping is

$$X(i,j)=i+j.$$

Its values in the grid are:

| Die 1 \ Die 2 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
| 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 |
| 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 11 |
| 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 11 | 12 |

Rows identify Die 1; columns identify Die 2. The entries are **values of $X$**, not probabilities.

## Add a probability model to the grid

Assume the dice are fair and independent. Fairness gives each face probability $1/6$, and independence gives

$$P((i,j))=P(\text{Die 1}=i)P(\text{Die 2}=j)=\frac16\cdot\frac16=\frac1{36}.$$

Every cell then has the same probability. The grid would still describe the mapping for loaded or dependent dice, but its cell probabilities could differ. The equal-weight assumption is what allows us to calculate probabilities by counting cells.

## Follow one sum through all its outcomes

The event $X=5$ contains the four cells

$$\{(1,4),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1)\}.$$

The event $X=7$ contains six:

$$\{(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)\}.$$

Therefore their probabilities are $4/36$ and $6/36$. A sum is more likely when more equally likely original outcomes lead to it.

The cells for each sum lie along a diagonal from upper right to lower left. Moving one row downward increases the first face by one; moving one column left decreases the second face by one, leaving the sum unchanged.

## Why unordered pairs do not give an equal-weight model

Even if the dice are rolled simultaneously, $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$ are two distinguishable outcomes once the dice are labeled. The unordered description “one 1 and one 2” groups two cells and has probability $2/36$.

By contrast, “two 1s” groups only $(1,1)$ and has probability $1/36$. Unordered face pairs are therefore not equally likely under independent fair rolls. Treating them as equally weighted would lose the distinction that produces the correct sum distribution.

Likewise, the 11 possible sums $2,3,\ldots,12$ are not 11 equally likely outcomes. The minimum sum occupies one cell; the middle sum occupies six.

## Check your understanding

Which cells satisfy $X=11$, and what probability do they have together?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

The cells are $(5,6)$ and $(6,5)$. Each has probability $1/36$, so $P(X=11)=2/36=1/18$. The sum 11 is one value of the random variable, supported by two distinct original outcomes.
