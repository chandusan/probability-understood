# When does differentiating a CDF give a density?

Every random variable has a CDF, but not every distribution has a density. For a distribution that does have a density, integrating the density gives its CDF, and differentiating the CDF recovers the density wherever the appropriate derivative relation holds.

## Separate properties shared by all CDFs from density assumptions

For every random variable, its CDF $F(x)=P(X\le x)$ is nondecreasing and right-continuous, with

$$
\lim_{x\to-\infty}F(x)=0,
\qquad
\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)=1.
$$

Nondecreasing means that increasing a threshold cannot reduce the event. The limits mean that a sufficiently far-left threshold accumulates almost no probability, while a sufficiently far-right threshold accumulates almost all of it.

A density exists when we can find an integrable nonnegative function $f$ such that

$$
F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt
$$

for every real $x$. This is the stronger property called **absolute continuity** of the distribution's CDF.

## The differentiability statement needs a qualification

The source summarizes the relationship as “$F$ is differentiable and $f=F'$,” while also noting that corners may occur. The precise version is:

- If a density $f$ is continuous at $x$, then $F'(x)$ exists and equals $f(x)$.
- For an arbitrary integrable density, $F'(x)=f(x)$ holds **almost everywhere**: possible exceptions occupy a set of total length zero.

Almost everywhere does not mean that we have identified every point where the derivative works. It means that the exceptional set does not affect any area integral. Isolated points are one familiar example of such an exceptional set.

Thus we differentiate the smooth branches of a piecewise CDF and examine the joins separately. We do not need to force every join to possess a two-sided derivative.

## A valid density whose CDF has corners

For $X$ uniform on $[0,1]$,

$$
F(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x<0,\\
x,&0\le x\le1,\\
1,&x>1.
\end{cases}
$$

The slopes are 0, then 1, then 0. At 0 the left and right slopes differ, and at 1 they differ again. The CDF is continuous but not differentiable at those two points.

Nonetheless,

$$
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
1,&0<x<1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

is a density because its integral gives exactly the stated CDF. Assigning different nonnegative finite values to $f(0)$ or $f(1)$ would not change the distribution. Density values at individual points are not uniquely determined by probabilities.

## A continuous CDF alone does not guarantee a density

A continuous CDF has no jumps, so the distribution assigns probability zero to every individual value. But “no jumps” does not by itself guarantee that all probability can be represented as area under an ordinary density.

There are more unusual distributions, such as the Cantor distribution, that have a continuous CDF but concentrate probability on a set of total length zero. Any ordinary integrable density gives zero probability to a set of length zero, so it cannot describe that distribution. Its CDF derivative alone misses the accumulated probability.

For the piecewise smooth examples in this chapter, the practical check is straightforward: compute a nonnegative candidate density, then verify that integrating it reproduces the whole CDF and gives total mass 1. This checks more than continuity alone.

<summary>Check: what if the CDF has a jump?</summary>

A jump at $c$ has size $P(X=c)=F(c)-F(c^-)$, where $F(c^-)$ is the limit from the left. An ordinary density cannot create a jump because a single point has zero area. Differentiating only the smooth branches would lose the probability sitting at that point.
