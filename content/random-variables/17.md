# A density assigns probability to intervals through area

A probability density function, or PDF, describes how probability is distributed along the number line. Its height is a rate of probability per unit of the variable. To obtain a probability, we integrate that rate over a range of values.

> Calculus refresher: [how an integral adds up area](../appendix/calculus.html#integration-add-up-small-contributions).

## The three requirements in the definition

A density $f$ is a function satisfying

$$
f(x)\ge0\quad\text{for all }x,
\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1.
$$

Its connection to the random variable is

$$
\boxed{P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx}
\qquad(a\le b).
$$

Nonnegativity ensures that regions under the graph have nonnegative probability. The total integral being 1 ensures that all possible values together carry the whole probability. The interval formula tells us how to use the function, rather than merely what its graph must look like.

A distribution that admits such a density is called **absolutely continuous**. Introductory courses often shorten this to “continuous.” Later we will distinguish having a density from merely having a continuous CDF.

## Why density height is not a point probability

For a variable with a density,

$$
P(X=c)=\int_c^c f(x)\,dx=0.
$$

The interval has zero width, so its area is zero. This does not say that $X$ cannot take the value $c$. It says that no individual point receives a positive share of probability.

If $f$ is continuous near $c$ and $h>0$ is small, then

$$
P(c<X\le c+h)=\int_c^{c+h}f(x)\,dx\approx f(c)h.
$$

The height $f(c)$ must be multiplied by a width. If $X$ is measured in seconds, $f$ has units of probability per second; the integral multiplies by seconds to produce a dimensionless probability.

## A fully normalized example

Consider

$$
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
2x,&0\le x\le1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

This function is nonnegative, and

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx
=\int_0^1 2x\,dx
=[x^2]_0^1=1.
$$

For this model,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(0.7\le X\le0.8)
&=\int_{0.7}^{0.8}2x\,dx\\
&=0.8^2-0.7^2=0.15.
\end{aligned}
$$

At the midpoint, $f(0.75)=1.5$. This is a valid density height greater than 1. The probability over the interval is $0.15$, not $1.5$. Here midpoint height times width gives the exact area, $1.5(0.1)=0.15$, because the density is linear.

## Which bounds must stay between zero and one?

Every **probability**, including $P(X\le x)$, must lie in $[0,1]$. A density height has no such upper bound. For example, a uniform distribution on an interval of width $1/10$ has constant density 10 there: height 10 times width $1/10$ gives total area 1.

The constraints on a density are nonnegativity and total area, rather than a height ceiling. Concentrating probability into a narrower interval requires a taller density.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Check: do open and closed interval endpoints matter?</summary>

For a variable with a density, $P(X=a)=P(X=b)=0$. Consequently $P(a<X<b)$, $P(a\le X<b)$, $P(a<X\le b)$ and $P(a\le X\le b)$ are all equal to the same integral. This conclusion uses the density assumption; a discrete variable can have positive probability at an endpoint.
