# Build the distance CDF one radius range at a time

For a uniformly chosen point in the unit square, $X$ is the distance from the lower-left corner. The CDF measures the area inside both the square and a disk of radius $x$. Three different geometric configurations explain the branches of its formula.

## The two easy extremes

A distance cannot be negative, so

$$
F(x)=P(X\le x)=0\qquad(x<0).
$$

At $x=0$, the only qualifying point is the origin. A single point has area zero, so $F(0)=0$ as well. A possible outcome need not have positive probability in a continuous model.

At the other end, the largest possible distance is $\sqrt2$, attained at the upper-right corner. Thus

$$
F(x)=1\qquad(x\ge\sqrt2).
$$

The CDF remains at 1 beyond $\sqrt2$ because increasing the threshold cannot add outcomes once every point already qualifies.

## Radii from 0 to 1: a quarter disk

When $0\le x\le1$, the circle has not crossed the top or right side. Its first-quadrant quarter disk lies entirely inside the square.

A full disk of radius $x$ has area $\pi x^2$. The angle inside the square is $\pi/2$, one fourth of the full angle $2\pi$. Therefore

$$
F(x)=\frac{\pi/2}{2\pi}\,\pi x^2
=\frac{\pi x^2}{4},\qquad0\le x\le1.
$$

There is no additional denominator: the total square area is 1. In particular,

$$
F(1)=\frac{\pi}{4}\approx0.78540.
$$

So about $78.54\%$ of the square lies within one unit of its lower-left corner. The remaining probability $1-\pi/4$ belongs to distances between 1 and $\sqrt2$.

## Radii from 1 to $\sqrt2$: find the overlap area

Call the shaded intersection area $A(x)$. For these radii, the quarter disk extends outside the square along the top and right. We must use its overlap with the square:

$$
F(x)=A(x),\qquad1<x<\sqrt2.
$$

This placeholder is meaningful: it identifies exactly which area remains to be calculated. The next lesson divides it into two triangles and one sector, following the source diagram.

A useful error check already follows. At $x=\sqrt2$, the unrestricted quarter-disk formula would give

$$
\frac{\pi(\sqrt2)^2}{4}=\frac{\pi}{2}>1.
$$

That cannot be a probability. The difficulty is not the disk-area formula; it is applying that formula to a region that includes points outside the sample space.

## Assemble the structure before doing the harder geometry

We now know

$$
F(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x<0,\\
\dfrac{\pi x^2}{4},&0\le x\le1,\\
A(x),&1<x<\sqrt2,\\
1,&x\ge\sqrt2.
\end{cases}
$$

The missing formula must start at $A(1)=\pi/4$ and end at $A(\sqrt2)=1$. It must also increase as $x$ increases: every point already within distance $x$ remains within any larger distance. These geometric facts will help us check the algebra later.

## A probability we can calculate already

Both thresholds $1/2$ and 1 use the quarter-disk branch. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(1/2<X\le1)
&=F(1)-F(1/2)\\
&=\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{16}\\
&=\frac{3\pi}{16}\approx0.58905.
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtracting the smaller quarter disk leaves the part of a quarter annulus inside the square. It is the geometry of the event, expressed through a difference of cumulative probabilities.
