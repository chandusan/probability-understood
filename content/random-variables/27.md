# A probability mass function assigns probability to individual values

For a discrete random variable, each possible value carries a probability mass. To find the probability of a larger event, identify the values that satisfy it and add their masses.

## What a PMF describes

Let $X$ take values in a finite or countably infinite set $S_X$. Its probability mass function, or PMF, is

$$
p(x)=P(X=x).
$$

For values outside the support $S_X$, set $p(x)=0$. For example, a count of customers might take values $0,1,2,\ldots$; values such as $1.5$ cannot occur. A discrete variable need not be integer-valued, however: a variable taking only $0.5$, $1.5$, and $3.5$ is also discrete.

The two basic validity requirements are

$$
p(x)\ge0\quad\text{for every }x,
\qquad \sum_{x\in S_X}p(x)=1.
$$

These also imply $p(x)\le1$. If one mass exceeded one, the sum of that mass and the other nonnegative masses would exceed one.

## Why adding masses gives an event probability

Suppose we want $P(a\le X\le b)$. The event is the union of all singleton events $\{X=x\}$ with $a\le x\le b$. Two different singleton events cannot both occur in one outcome: $X$ cannot equal two different numbers at once.

They are therefore disjoint, so additivity gives

$$
\boxed{P(a\le X\le b)
=\sum_{\substack{x\in S_X\\a\le x\le b}}p(x).}
$$

The support restriction matters. We add only possible values, not every real number between the endpoints. This is a sum of point probabilities, rather than an integral of density heights.

As a small example, suppose

| $x$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $p(x)$ | $0.1$ | $0.2$ | $0.4$ | $0.3$ |

The entries are nonnegative and sum to one. The event $\{1\le X\le2\}$ includes exactly the values one and two, giving $0.2+0.4=0.6$.

## Endpoints can carry probability

In the source illustration, the dashed boundaries sit at $a=4$ and $b=7$. The inclusive interval collects the bars at $4,5,6,7$; the strictly interior interval collects only the bars at $5,6$. No numerical bar heights are supplied, but their roles are clear:

$$
P(4\le X\le7)=p(4)+p(5)+p(6)+p(7),
$$

$$
P(4<X<7)=p(5)+p(6).
$$

More generally, for $a<b$,

$$
P(a\le X\le b)-P(a<X<b)=p(a)+p(b).
$$

The two probabilities agree only when the excluded endpoints have zero mass. In the small numerical example above, $P(1<X<2)=0$: there is no possible value strictly between one and two, even though $P(1\le X\le2)=0.6$.

## Use the graph correctly

A PMF graph often draws bars or stems to make the masses visible. Their **heights** are the individual probabilities. Their display widths are a drawing choice and do not enter the calculation. This differs from a probability density graph, where interval probability comes from area.
