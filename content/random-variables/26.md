# A mixed spending model combines a jump and a density

Some users spend nothing, while positive spending varies continuously. The cumulative distribution must include both groups: a jump at zero for non-paying users, followed by a smooth increase for positive spending.

> Calculus refresher: [antiderivatives and upper-minus-lower evaluation](../appendix/calculus.html#integration-add-up-small-contributions).

## State the model and its units

Let $X$ be one randomly chosen user's daily spending, measured in **hundreds of dollars**. Thus $X=1$ means 100 dollars and $X=2$ means 200 dollars. The model specifies

$$
P(X=0)=0.4,
\qquad f(x)=0.3x\quad\text{for }0<x\le2.
$$

Here $f$ describes the continuous contribution to the distribution across **all users**. Its area is

$$
\int_0^2 0.3x\,dx
=\left[0.15x^2\right]_0^2
=0.6.
$$

Adding the point mass gives $0.4+0.6=1$. The model therefore assigns probability $0.6$ to being a paying user and no probability to negative spending or spending above 200 dollars.

The source calls $0.3x$ a density “for paying users.” That wording needs care: a density **conditional on already being a paying user** must integrate to one. Here that conditional density is

$$
f_{X\mid X>0}(x)=\frac{0.3x}{P(X>0)}
=\frac{0.3x}{0.6}=0.5x,
\qquad0<x\le2.
$$

Multiplying $0.3x$ by $0.6$ again would count the paying-user probability twice.

## Part (a): build the cumulative distribution

By definition, $F(x)=P(X\le x)$. Work from left to right.

For $x<0$, the event is impossible, so $F(x)=0$. At $x=0$, all non-paying users enter the event, giving $F(0)=0.4$. For $0<x\le2$, include that same point mass plus spending between zero and $x$:

$$
F(x)=0.4+\int_0^x0.3t\,dt
=0.4+0.15x^2.
$$

The letter $t$ is the integration variable; $x$ is the cutoff. At $x=2$ this reaches one, and it stays one afterward. Therefore

$$
\boxed{F(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x<0,\\
0.4+0.15x^2,&0\le x\le2,\\
1,&x>2.
\end{cases}}
$$

The jump at zero has height $0.4$. There is no jump at two: a positive density value at an endpoint does not create a positive point probability there.

## Part (b): spending at least 100 dollars

The requested event is $\{X\ge1\}$. Every qualifying outcome lies in the continuous part, so

$$
\boxed{P(X\ge1)=\int_1^2 0.3x\,dx
=0.15(4-1)=0.45.}
$$

The CDF gives the same answer. Let $F(a-)=P(X<a)$ denote the left limit at $a$. In general,

$$
P(X\ge a)=1-F(a-),
\qquad P(X>a)=1-F(a).
$$

## Why does the left limit equal the CDF value here?

**The only difference between \(F(a^-)\) and \(F(a)\) is the probability of \(X\) being exactly \(a\).** They are equal whenever that probability is zero. Let’s unpack that using this example.

Here, \(X\) measures spending in hundreds of dollars, so \(X=1\) means spending exactly \$100.

**First, what does \(F(1^-)\) mean?**

The superscript “\(-\)” means **approach 1 from below**:

\[
F(1^-)=\lim_{x\to1,\;x<1}F(x).
\]

Imagine moving the spending cutoff through \$99, \$99.90, \$99.99, and closer and closer to \$100, while keeping it below \$100. In the limit, we include every spending amount **strictly below \$100**, but exclude exactly \$100.

Therefore:

| Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| \(F(1^-)=P(X<1)\) | Probability of spending **less than \$100** |
| \(F(1)=P(X\le1)\) | Probability of spending **at most \$100** |

Notice that \(1^-\) does **not** mean a particular number such as \(0.999\). It denotes this limiting process.

**Now separate “at most \$100” into two disjoint possibilities:**

- Spending less than \$100.
- Spending exactly \$100.

By the addition rule,

\[
P(X\le1)=P(X<1)+P(X=1).
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{F(1)=F(1^-)+P(X=1).}
\]

That identity explains the entire distinction.

**Why is \(P(X=1)=0\) in this model?**

The model puts positive spending into a continuous density:

\[
f(x)=0.3x,\qquad 0<x\le2.
\]

Probability comes from **area over an interval**. Exactly one value has zero width, hence zero area:

\[
P(X=1)=\int_1^1 0.3x\,dx=0.
\]

Although \(f(1)=0.3\), that is a density height—not the probability of spending exactly \$100.

An **atom** simply means a point assigned positive probability. So “no atom at one” meant precisely:

\[
P(X=1)=0.
\]

Consequently,

\[
F(1)=F(1^-)+0.
\]

Using the CDF from the slide,

\[
F(1)=0.4+0.15(1)^2=0.55,
\]

so \(F(1^-)=0.55\) too. Therefore,

\[
\boxed{P(X\ge1)=P(X>1)=1-0.55=0.45.}
\]

Including exactly \$100 adds zero probability **under this continuous model**.

At zero, however, the model explicitly assigns \(P(X=0)=0.4\). There,

\[
F(0^-)=0,\qquad F(0)=0.4.
\]

**A point with positive probability creates a jump in the CDF. At a point with zero probability, including that point causes no jump.**

## Read the answer within each population

Among paying users alone,

$$
P(X\ge1\mid X>0)
=\frac{P(X\ge1)}{P(X>0)}
=\frac{0.45}{0.6}=0.75.
$$

The numerator needs no extra intersection because spending at least 100 dollars already implies positive spending. Equivalently, $0.6\times0.75=0.45$: 60 percent pay, and 75 percent of those pay at least 100 dollars.
