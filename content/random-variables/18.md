# Read a shaded density plot as an interval probability

The source diagram shades the region between 50 and 70 under a density curve. That shaded area represents $P(50\le X\le70)$. The curve's height at 50 or 70, and the highest point of the curve, answer different questions.

## Identify the horizontal interval first

The horizontal axis lists possible values of the random variable. The two vertical boundaries mark the event

$$
A=\{50\le X\le70\}.
$$

Every narrow slice of the shaded region contributes a little probability. Adding all the slices is integration:

$$
\boxed{P(A)=\int_{50}^{70}f(t)\,dt}.
$$

We use $t$ as the integration variable, but another letter would give the same result. The limits 50 and 70 specify which values count toward the event.

Because the variable has a density, including or excluding either endpoint does not change this probability. The vertical boundary lines themselves have zero width and therefore zero area.

## Why a density graph can rise and then fall

The source curve has a higher peak toward the left and another smaller rise farther right. A PDF need not increase. Taller regions indicate more probability per unit of horizontal length; lower regions indicate less.

For two sufficiently narrow intervals of the same width, the interval near the larger density tends to carry more probability. But comparing intervals of different widths requires comparing their areas. A broad low region can have more probability than a narrow high region.

A mode is a location where the density peaks. It is not a point with positive probability: $P(X=c)=0$ still holds at a peak $c$.

## Approximate an area with narrow rectangles

Divide $[50,70]$ into $n$ strips, each of width

$$
\Delta t=\frac{70-50}{n}=\frac{20}{n}.
$$

Choose a representative point $t_i$ in strip $i$. The rectangle over that strip has area $f(t_i)\Delta t$, so

$$
P(50\le X\le70)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(t_i)\Delta t.
$$

For a regular curve, making the strips finer improves this approximation and leads to the integral. This explains why probability is tied to area: the density contributes its local height multiplied by a small range of possible values.

The source gives a schematic curve rather than a formula or exact measurements. It identifies the correct integral, but it does not supply enough information for an exact numerical probability.

## A separate example with an exact calculation

To see how the calculation works when a formula is known, consider the different density

$$
g(t)=
\begin{cases}
t/5000,&0\le t\le100,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

It is normalized because

$$
\int_0^{100}\frac{t}{5000}\,dt
=\left[\frac{t^2}{10000}\right]_0^{100}=1.
$$

Under this example model,

$$
P(50\le X\le70)
=\left[\frac{t^2}{10000}\right]_{50}^{70}
=\frac{4900-2500}{10000}=0.24.
$$

This number belongs to the explicitly defined $g$, not to the source's unspecified curve. Its role is to show the area calculation with all quantities available.

<summary>Check: can we use f(70) − f(50)?</summary>

No. That subtracts two density heights. Interval probability is the integral of the density or, equivalently, the difference of CDF values $F(70)-F(50)$. For the example $g$, the height difference is $0.014-0.010=0.004$, while the required area is $0.24$.
