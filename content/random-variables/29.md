# A CDF starts at zero and eventually collects all probability

The cumulative distribution function works for discrete, continuous, and mixed random variables. It measures how much probability lies at or below a moving cutoff, so its far-left limit is zero and its far-right limit is one.

## One definition covers every distribution

For a real-valued random variable $X$, define

$$
F(x)=P(X\le x),\qquad x\in\mathbb R.
$$

The input $x$ is a chosen cutoff. The output $F(x)$ is a probability and must lie between zero and one. Computing it may require adding point masses, integrating a density, or combining both operations, but the event being measured is always the same.

For example, in the four-value table with masses $0.3,0.2,0.3,0.2$ at $3,5,8,10$,

$$
F(2)=0,\qquad F(6)=0.3+0.2=0.5,\qquad F(10)=1.
$$

The cutoff six does not need to be a possible value of $X$. It simply separates values included in the cumulative probability from those excluded.

## Interpret infinity as a limit

The slide writes $F(-\infty)=0$ and $F(\infty)=1$. Precisely, these abbreviate

$$
\boxed{\lim_{x\to-\infty}F(x)=0,
\qquad \lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)=1.}
$$

Infinity is not an ordinary real input, and a real-valued random variable takes finite values. The statements say that moving the cutoff sufficiently far left captures arbitrarily little probability, while moving it sufficiently far right leaves arbitrarily little probability behind.

The CDF need not actually reach one at any finite cutoff. That depends on whether the distribution has an upper bound.

## Why probabilities follow increasing events

We use a consequence of countable additivity. If

$$
A_1\subseteq A_2\subseteq\cdots,
$$

split their union into the disjoint pieces

$$
A_1,\quad A_2\setminus A_1,\quad A_3\setminus A_2,\quad\ldots.
$$

The probability of $A_n$ is the sum of the first $n$ pieces. As $n$ grows, these partial sums approach the probability of the whole union. Consequently,

$$
P\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\right)
=\lim_{n\to\infty}P(A_n).
$$

For decreasing events, apply the same result to their increasing complements. This gives

$$
P\left(\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}B_n\right)
=\lim_{n\to\infty}P(B_n)
\quad\text{when }B_1\supseteq B_2\supseteq\cdots.
$$

These are often called continuity of probability from below and above. They concern sequences of events, even when the CDF itself has jumps.

## Apply those rules to the two tails

The events $A_n=\{X\le n\}$ increase. Every finite value of $X$ is eventually below a sufficiently large integer $n$, so their union is the whole sample space. Hence

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}F(n)
=P\left(\bigcup_n\{X\le n\}\right)=1.
$$

The events $B_n=\{X\le-n\}$ decrease. No finite value can remain below $-n$ for every positive integer $n$, so their intersection is empty. Therefore

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}F(-n)
=P\left(\bigcap_n\{X\le-n\}\right)=0.
$$

The same limits hold for real cutoffs: increasing a cutoff can only include more outcomes, and every real cutoff lies between neighboring integers.

## A limit can be reached or merely approached

The four-value example reaches $F(x)=1$ at ten and stays there. In contrast, suppose $P(X=j)=2^{-j}$ for $j=1,2,\ldots$. For every positive integer $m$,

$$
F(m)=\sum_{j=1}^m2^{-j}=1-2^{-m}<1.
$$

There is always some probability above a finite cutoff, but $2^{-m}$ tends to zero. Thus $F(m)$ tends to one, exactly as the general rule requires.
