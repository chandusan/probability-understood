# A uniformly chosen point turns geometric area into probability

Choose a point uniformly from the unit square, and let $X$ be its distance from the lower-left corner. The source slide now adds the probability model to this mapping: a region's probability equals its area divided by the square's area. Since the unit square has area 1, the probability of a region is numerically equal to its area.

## Define the sample space and uniform sampling

Use coordinates

$$S=[0,1]\times[0,1],\qquad X(u,v)=\sqrt{u^2+v^2}.$$

Uniform sampling means that equally sized areas inside the square have equal probability. One way to implement this model is to choose horizontal and vertical coordinates independently, each uniformly from $[0,1]$.

For an event represented by a measurable region $E\subseteq S$,

$$\boxed{P(E)=\frac{\operatorname{area}(E)}{\operatorname{area}(S)}=\operatorname{area}(E).}$$

The source says “any subset.” In this continuous setting, the precise condition is that the region be measurable, so its area is defined. All rectangles, disks, arcs, and other ordinary geometric regions used here satisfy that condition.

## Start with regions whose areas are familiar

For the left half of the square,

$$E_1=\{(u,v):0\le u\le1/2,\ 0\le v\le1\},$$

the width is $1/2$ and the height is 1, so $P(E_1)=1/2$.

For the lower-left square with side length $1/2$, the area is $(1/2)^2=1/4$. These are events about coordinates rather than distance, but they establish how the uniform model converts geometry into probability.

The irregular shaded region on the source slide illustrates the same rule. We cannot infer its exact area just from the drawing; a numerical answer would require its dimensions or a precise mathematical description.

## Translate a distance threshold into a quarter-disk

Consider $E_r=\{X\le r\}$. In coordinates,

$$E_r=\{(u,v)\in S:u^2+v^2\le r^2\}.$$

For $0\le r\le1$, this is a quarter-disk of radius $r$ lying entirely inside the square. A full disk has area $\pi r^2$, so

$$P(X\le r)=\frac{\pi r^2}{4},\qquad 0\le r\le1.$$

For example,

$$P(X\le1/2)=\frac{\pi(1/2)^2}{4}=\frac{\pi}{16}\approx0.19635.$$

To find the probability that the distance is above $1/2$ but at most 1, subtract the smaller quarter-disk from the larger:

$$P(1/2<X\le1)=\frac\pi4-\frac\pi{16}=\frac{3\pi}{16}\approx0.58905.$$

This is the geometric version of a difference of CDF values.

## A curve can contain possible outcomes but have probability zero

The event $X=1/2$ is a quarter-circle arc, which has area zero. Consequently,

$$P(X=1/2)=0.$$

Yet points on that arc belong to the sample space, and the mapping genuinely gives them distance $1/2$. Probability zero does not mean the set is empty in a continuous model.

Removing or adding such an arc to a region does not change its area, so $P(X<1/2)=P(X\le1/2)$ here. This contrasts with a discrete dice sum, where adding an endpoint can add positive probability mass.

## Know when the simple area formula stops applying

For $1<r<\sqrt2$, the radius-$r$ quarter-disk extends outside the square. We must keep only its intersection with the square; using $\pi r^2/4$ without clipping would count impossible point locations. The next slides derive the complete distance CDF, including that range.

## Check your understanding

Would a model that chooses points more densely near the lower-left corner still satisfy $P(E)=\operatorname{area}(E)$?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

No. The distance mapping would remain $X(u,v)=\sqrt{u^2+v^2}$, but the point probabilities would no longer be uniform over area. Equal-area regions could then have different probabilities. The area-ratio rule is a consequence of the uniform sampling assumption, not of the square's shape alone.
