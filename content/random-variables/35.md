# Complete the unusual-dice PMF and connect it to the CDF

The handwritten solution starts the sum table with probabilities at two and three and ends at twelve. Filling its middle requires keeping the repeated faces' weights; the resulting PMF also lets us compute cumulative probabilities and compare these dice with ordinary ones.

## Keep the original experiment explicit

The first fair die has labels $1,2,2,3,3,4$; the second has labels $1,3,4,5,6,8$. The rolls are independent. Write $A$ and $B$ for their displayed numbers and $X=A+B$ for the sum.

There are 36 equally likely **physical face pairs**, each with probability $1/36$. The full six-by-six sum table in the preceding lesson lists every pair. If we instead describe the first die by its four distinct numerical values, their probabilities are

| $a$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $P(A=a)$ | $1/6$ | $2/6$ | $2/6$ | $1/6$ |

Every label on die two is distinct, so $P(B=b)=1/6$ for $b\in\{1,3,4,5,6,8\}$ and zero otherwise.

## Derive a sum probability from disjoint routes

For a fixed sum $x$, partition the event by the value of $A$:

$$
\{X=x\}=\bigcup_{a\in\{1,2,3,4\}}
\{A=a,\ B=x-a\}.
$$

These routes are disjoint because they require different values of $A$. Independence allows each joint probability to factor, giving

$$
\boxed{p_X(x)=\sum_{a\in\{1,2,3,4\}}
P(A=a)P(B=x-a).}
$$

This weighted sum is often called a convolution. The formula still counts physical faces, but it groups identical labels before doing so.

For a sum of seven, all four routes are possible:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_X(7)
&=P(A=1)P(B=6)+P(A=2)P(B=5)\\
&\quad+P(A=3)P(B=4)+P(A=4)P(B=3)\\
&=\frac16\frac16+\frac26\frac16
+\frac26\frac16+\frac16\frac16\\
&=\frac{1+2+2+1}{36}=\frac16.
\end{aligned}
$$

For a sum of eleven, only $(A,B)=(3,8)$ is possible. Its probability is $(2/6)(1/6)=2/36$, because die one has two faces labeled three.

## Finish every entry, then accumulate

The completed table gives the PMF and its cumulative sum at each supported value:

| $x$ | $p_X(x)$ | $F_X(x)=P(X\le x)$ |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2 | $1/36$ | $1/36$ |
| 3 | $2/36$ | $3/36$ |
| 4 | $3/36$ | $6/36$ |
| 5 | $4/36$ | $10/36$ |
| 6 | $5/36$ | $15/36$ |
| 7 | $6/36$ | $21/36$ |
| 8 | $5/36$ | $26/36$ |
| 9 | $4/36$ | $30/36$ |
| 10 | $3/36$ | $33/36$ |
| 11 | $2/36$ | $35/36$ |
| 12 | $1/36$ | $1$ |

The CDF is zero below two, constant between successive integers, and one from twelve onward. For example,

$$
P(X\ge8)=1-F_X(7)=1-\frac{21}{36}=\frac5{12},
$$

and

$$
P(5<X\le8)=F_X(8)-F_X(5)
=\frac{26-10}{36}=\frac49.
$$

Both answers can also be checked by adding the corresponding PMF entries.

## Explore the identical sum distributions

<div class="interactive-slot" data-interactive="rv-dice"></div>

Select the nontraditional-dice model, then compare it with standard dice. Both models give the same probabilities for every event defined solely by the sum, since every sum has the same mass.

They do not give the same probabilities for every event about the individual dice. For these unusual dice, $X=3$ forces the numerical pair $(2,1)$. For standard dice, sum three has two equally likely routes, $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$. Consequently,

$$
P(A=2\mid X=3)=1
\quad\text{for the unusual dice},
$$

but the corresponding probability is $1/2$ for standard dice. A random variable can merge different underlying outcomes into the same recorded value.

## Check your understanding

How can you recover $P(X=7)$ from the CDF table?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Measure the jump at seven</summary>

$F_X(7-)=F_X(6)=15/36$, because no supported values lie strictly between six and seven. Thus $P(X=7)=F_X(7)-F_X(7-)=21/36-15/36=6/36$.
