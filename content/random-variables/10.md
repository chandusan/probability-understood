# The CDF accumulates probability up to a threshold

The cumulative distribution function, or CDF, answers one question for every real threshold $x$: what is the probability that the random variable is at most $x$? Its definition is $F_X(x)=P(X\le x)$. Unlike a PMF or a density, the CDF is available for every real-valued random variable, whether discrete, continuous, or a mixture.

## The threshold need not be a possible value

For a dice sum $X$, the possible values are the integers 2 through 12, but $F_X(7.5)$ is perfectly meaningful. It asks for the probability that the sum does not exceed 7.5. Since no sum lies between 7 and 7.5,

$$F_X(7.5)=P(X\le7)=F_X(7).$$

For a discrete variable with PMF $p$, we accumulate its masses:

$$\boxed{F_X(x)=\sum_{t\le x}p(t),}$$

where the sum runs over its possible values $t$. Notice the different roles: $x$ is the threshold; $t$ indexes the values being added.

For the dice sum,

$$F_X(4)=p(2)+p(3)+p(4)=\frac{1+2+3}{36}=\frac16.$$

This differs from $p(4)=3/36$, which counts only equality to 4.

## Why every CDF stays between zero and one

For each $x$, the event $\{X\le x\}$ is an ordinary event. Therefore

$$0\le F_X(x)\le1.$$

A CDF value is itself a probability. This will distinguish it from a density, whose height can exceed one without violating any probability rule.

## Why a CDF cannot decrease

If $a\le b$, any outcome with $X\le a$ also has $X\le b$. Thus

$$\{X\le a\}\subseteq\{X\le b\},$$

which implies

$$F_X(a)\le F_X(b).$$

Moving the threshold to the right can add outcomes but cannot remove them. This is the meaning of **non-decreasing**. Flat portions are allowed when the intervening values carry no probability.

## Explain the two limits

For a real-valued $X$,

$$\lim_{x\to-\infty}F_X(x)=0,\qquad
\lim_{x\to\infty}F_X(x)=1.$$

A threshold far to the left eventually excludes almost all probability; one far to the right includes almost all of it. With bounded dice sums, the CDF is already zero below 2 and one from 12 onward. For an unbounded distribution, the limiting values need not be attained at any finite threshold.

## Right-continuity decides what happens at a jump

The definition uses “less than **or equal to**,” so any probability at the threshold itself is included. At a value where there is positive mass, the CDF takes the higher value after that mass is added.

Formally,

$$\lim_{t\downarrow x}F_X(t)=F_X(x).$$

Here $t\downarrow x$ means approaching $x$ from above. The extra event $\{x<X\le t\}$ shrinks away as $t$ approaches $x$, leaving precisely $\{X\le x\}$. A CDF may jump when approached from the left; it cannot omit its own jump at $x$.

For the dice sum, the value just below 7 is $15/36$, while $F_X(7)=21/36$. Values just above 7 are also $21/36$.

## Distinguish density from exact-value probability

The source compares the CDF with a probability density function. A density describes probability per unit of the numerical axis; its value $f(x)$ is **not** $P(X=x)$. In a continuous model with a density, every specified point has probability zero, while an interval can have positive probability obtained by integrating the density.

The CDF remains an accumulated probability in either setting. In particular,

$$\boxed{P(a<X\le b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a),\qquad a<b.}$$

Subtracting removes the outcomes at or below $a$ from those at or below $b$, explaining the strict lower and inclusive upper endpoint.
