# Build a discrete CDF by accumulating its masses

A discrete CDF is a running total of point probabilities. Each atom produces an upward jump, and the CDF value at that point is the height after the jump because the definition includes equality.

## Turn a PMF into a cumulative sum

If $X$ has countable support $S_X$ and PMF $p(u)$, the event $\{X\le x\}$ is the disjoint union of the events $\{X=u\}$ for supported values $u\le x$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{F(x)=P(X\le x)
=\sum_{\substack{u\in S_X\\u\le x}}p(u).}
$$

The variable $u$ runs through the possible outcomes; $x$ is the cutoff. Even when $x$ is not a possible outcome, this definition still tells us which masses to include.

Use the earlier table as a complete numerical example:

| Value $u$ | Mass $p(u)$ | Total just before $u$: $F(u-)$ | Total including $u$: $F(u)$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | $0.3$ | $0$ | $0.3$ |
| 5 | $0.2$ | $0.3$ | $0.5$ |
| 8 | $0.3$ | $0.5$ | $0.8$ |
| 10 | $0.2$ | $0.8$ | $1$ |

## Specify every interval and boundary

Below three, no mass has been included. At three, add $0.3$; that total stays unchanged until five. Continue in the same way:

$$
\boxed{F(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x<3,\\
0.3,&3\le x<5,\\
0.5,&5\le x<8,\\
0.8,&8\le x<10,\\
1,&x\ge10.
\end{cases}}
$$

The inequalities determine the graph's endpoints. At $x=5$, the lower plateau ends with an open circle at height $0.3$, while the next plateau starts with a filled circle at height $0.5$. The filled point is the actual CDF value $F(5)$.

That is also how to read the original slide's schematic staircase. Its arrow labeled $p(x)$ points to a **vertical jump**, measured from the open lower endpoint to the filled upper endpoint. The picture illustrates the rule; the table above supplies exact numerical values for our worked example.

## Recover the PMF from the staircase

Subtract consecutive cumulative levels:

$$
p(3)=0.3-0=0.3,\qquad p(5)=0.5-0.3=0.2,
$$

$$
p(8)=0.8-0.5=0.3,\qquad p(10)=1-0.8=0.2.
$$

At six there is no jump, so $P(X=6)=F(6)-F(6-)=0$. Yet $F(6)=0.5$: zero probability **at** a value does not imply zero cumulative probability **up to** it.

The jumps sum to one, and all are nonnegative. Thus the graph is nondecreasing, starts with limiting height zero, and finishes with limiting height one. Its filled upper endpoints ensure right-continuity.

Select the four-value table model to match this example. Compare the event $X\le5$ with $X<5$: the first includes the jump at five and the second stops below it. Their probabilities differ by $p(5)=0.2$.

For finite support, and for familiar integer-valued distributions, “staircase” is a literal description with horizontal gaps between possible values. A general countably infinite support can have accumulation points or be dense, so horizontal gaps need not exist everywhere. The cumulative-sum formula and jump formula remain valid in every discrete case.
