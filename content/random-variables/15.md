# Derive the overlap area using two triangles and a sector

Let $1<x<\sqrt2$. A disk of radius $x$ centered at the lower-left corner crosses the top and right sides of the unit square. We will derive the shaded overlap area $A(x)$ by identifying every length and angle in the source diagram.

## Find where the circle crosses the square

Write $O=(0,0)$. At the top edge, the vertical coordinate is 1, so the circle equation becomes

$$
u^2+1=x^2.
$$

The intersection has nonnegative horizontal coordinate

$$
a=\sqrt{x^2-1}.
$$

Thus the top intersection is $P=(a,1)$. The same calculation at the right edge gives $Q=(1,a)$. Since $1<x<\sqrt2$, we have $0<a<1$, so both intersections lie on the square's sides.

The two radii $OP$ and $OQ$ have length $x$. They divide the shaded region into three pieces with no overlapping interiors: the triangle with vertices $O,(0,1),P$; the circular sector between $OP$ and $OQ$; and the triangle with vertices $O,(1,0),Q$.

## The two triangle areas

The first triangle has a vertical side of length 1 and a perpendicular horizontal side of length $a$. Its area is $\tfrac12(1)(a)=a/2$. The other triangle has the same two perpendicular lengths and also has area $a/2$.

Together they contribute

$$
\frac a2+\frac a2=a=\sqrt{x^2-1}.
$$

Their equality follows from these explicit lengths; it does not require assuming that the entire shaded region is a triangle or a sector.

## Identify the angle $\phi$

Let $\phi$ be the angle between the vertical axis and $OP$. In the corresponding right triangle, the side opposite $\phi$ has length $a$ and the adjacent side has length 1. Hence

$$
\tan\phi=\frac a1=a,
\qquad
\phi=\arctan a=\arctan\sqrt{x^2-1}.
$$

Here $\arctan$ is the **inverse tangent function**: it returns the angle whose tangent is $a$. It does not mean $1/\tan a$. The relevant angle lies in $(0,\pi/4)$ and is measured in radians.

The right-hand triangle gives the same angle between the horizontal axis and $OQ$. The first-quadrant angle $\pi/2$ consists of these two angles plus the central sector angle $\theta$. Therefore

$$
\theta=\frac\pi2-2\phi.
$$

## Calculate the sector area and add the pieces

A sector of angle $\theta$ occupies the fraction $\theta/(2\pi)$ of a full disk. Its area is

$$
\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\,\pi x^2
=\frac{\theta x^2}{2}.
$$

Adding the triangles gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(x)
&=a+\frac{x^2}{2}\left(\frac\pi2-2\phi\right)\\
&=a+x^2\left(\frac\pi4-\phi\right)\\
&=\boxed{\sqrt{x^2-1}
+x^2\left(\frac\pi4-\arctan\sqrt{x^2-1}\right)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Because the square has area 1, this area is also $F(x)$ for this radius range.

## Check both ends of the geometry

As $x$ approaches 1, $a$ approaches 0 and $\phi$ approaches 0. The triangles disappear, while the sector becomes a quarter disk. The formula gives $A(1)=\pi/4$.

As $x$ approaches $\sqrt2$, $a$ approaches 1 and $\phi$ approaches $\pi/4$. The sector angle becomes zero, and the two triangles fill the square. The formula gives $A(\sqrt2)=1$.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Check the equivalent inverse-trigonometric forms</summary>

The same right triangle has adjacent side 1 and hypotenuse $x$, so $\cos\phi=1/x$. On $0\le\phi\le\pi/4$,

$$
\phi=\arccos(1/x)=\arctan\sqrt{x^2-1}.
$$

Also $\arcsin(1/x)=\pi/2-\phi$, giving

$$
A(x)=\sqrt{x^2-1}+x^2\bigl(\arcsin(1/x)-\pi/4\bigr).
$$

These are the same formula on $1\le x\le\sqrt2$. The angle range is what makes the inverse-function identities unambiguous.

</details>
