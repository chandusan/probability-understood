# Add alternatives and multiply stages

Counting problems become easier when you distinguish two kinds of structure: an outcome belongs to one of several alternative cases, or an outcome is assembled through several stages. Disjoint alternatives add; compatible sequential choices multiply when their branch counts are fixed.

## Rule 1: add mutually exclusive cases

If $A_1,A_2,\ldots$ are pairwise disjoint finite sets, then

$$
|A_1\cup A_2\cup\cdots|=|A_1|+|A_2|+\cdots.
$$

The slide writes the number of elements of $A$ as $n(A)$; here $|A|$ means the same thing. “Pairwise disjoint” means $A_i\cap A_j=\varnothing$ whenever $i\ne j$.

For example, suppose a shop sells four distinct hot drinks and three distinct cold drinks. If each drink is listed in exactly one category, choosing one drink has $4+3=7$ possibilities. A drink cannot be counted in both cases.

If cases overlap, simply adding their sizes double-counts the overlap. For two sets the repair is

$$
|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|.
$$

An alternative repair is to rewrite the problem using disjoint cases before counting.

## Rule 2: multiply two stages

Suppose the first operation has $n_1$ choices, and **after every first choice** the second operation has exactly $n_2$ available choices. Then

$$
N=n_1n_2.
$$

A meal with one of four mains and one of three sides has $4\times3=12$ possibilities if every main can be paired with all three sides. Each first-stage choice contributes three completed meals.

The available side identities could even differ by main; the product remains valid if each main permits exactly three sides.

## When the second count varies

If three mains each allow two sides, but a fourth main allows five, the total is

$$
2+2+2+5=11,
$$

not $4\times2$ or $4\times5$. Count the completions below each first branch and add them, or group branches with the same number of completions: $3\times2+1\times5$.

These are counting rules, not assertions that random events are independent. There need not be any random experiment at all.

## Check your understanding

A ticket can be standard, with three possible seat zones, or premium, with two possible zones and two meal choices in each zone. How many tickets are possible?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the combination of rules</summary>

There are three standard tickets. A premium ticket has $2\times2=4$ possibilities. The ticket types are disjoint, so

$$
3+(2\times2)=7.
$$

We multiply within the premium case because both a zone and a meal must be chosen; we add between ticket types because a ticket is either standard or premium.
