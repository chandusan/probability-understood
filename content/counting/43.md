# Stronger hands distinguish rank roles and suited sequences

The remaining categories reveal two different kinds of structure: repeated ranks with different roles, and consecutive ranks sharing one suit. Precise definitions keep these categories separate from the ones we have already counted.

## Full house and four of a kind

A **full house** has rank pattern

$$
\{x,x,x,y,y\},\qquad x\ne y.
$$

One rank appears three times and another appears twice. In the source image, three aces are joined by two sevens. The rank used for the triple has a different role from the rank used for the pair: three aces and two sevens is a different rank pattern from three sevens and two aces.

**Four of a kind** has pattern

$$
\{x,x,x,x,y\},\qquad x\ne y.
$$

It necessarily uses all four suits of rank x, together with one card of another rank. The source example shows four queens and a five. The unmatched fifth card is often called a kicker.

## Straight flush and royal flush

A **straight flush** has five consecutive ranks, all in one suit. For this chapter's mutually exclusive table, reserve the top sequence for a separate category. Thus “straight flush” here means a straight flush **other than a royal flush**.

A **royal flush** is

$$
\{10,J,Q,K,A\}
$$

all in the same suit. The source shows a spade royal flush and a heart straight flush with ranks 6 through 10.

Among the ten allowable straight rank patterns, nine are nonroyal and one is royal. The low-ace pattern $A,2,3,4,5$ is one of the nine nonroyal patterns. Aces cannot join sequences such as $K,A,2,3,4$.

## A category label is more specific than a property

A royal flush has the properties “all one suit” and “five consecutive ranks.” Nevertheless, the table places it only in the royal-flush category. It is excluded from both the ordinary-flush count and the ordinary-straight count.

Similarly, a full house contains a triple and a pair as subsets, but it is not placed in the “three of a kind” or “one pair” categories. Those categories require the remaining ranks to stay distinct.

Without these conventions, category counts would overlap and would not sum to the total number of hands.
