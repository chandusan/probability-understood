# See multiplication as repeated branching

The three-stage tree shows what the compact product $n_1n_2n_3$ is hiding: groups of groups of complete outcomes. Each factor records how many ways every partial path can continue at one level.

## Follow one path through the source diagram

Start at the root on the left. Choose one of the $n_1$ first branches. At that node, choose one of $n_2$ second branches. From the resulting second-stage node, choose one of $n_3$ final branches.

The red braces mark these different levels. The $n_2$ label repeats after the possible first choices, and the final fans carry the common $n_3$ structure. The general leaf count is

$$N=n_1n_2n_3.$$

Do not interpret the product as a count of edges or nodes. It counts complete root-to-leaf paths, assuming those paths correspond one-to-one with the desired outcomes.

## Count from the inside outward

Consider two first choices, three second choices, and two final choices. Fix a first choice. Its subtree has $3\cdot2=6$ leaves. There are two such first-choice subtrees, giving $6+6=12$.

Alternatively, after two stages there are $2\cdot3=6$ partial histories. Each has two continuations, so there are $6\cdot2=12$ final histories. Both views explain the same product.

## Grow and inspect the tree

In the experiment, choose three stages. Set the first count to two, the second to three, and the third to two. Use the path selector to trace a complete outcome. Then turn on unequal branches: the first first-stage choice receives one extra second-stage choice, and each new history still has the usual final continuations.

With the unequal setting, the two first-choice subtrees contain $4\cdot2=8$ and $3\cdot2=6$ leaves. The total is $8+6=14$. This particular change adds two final outcomes, because the new second-stage history branches twice at the last stage.

## Why a tree helps with more difficult restrictions

If a restriction depends on earlier choices, a tree makes that dependency visible. Count the leaves beneath each valid branch, then add across disjoint branches. You may find regular subtrees where multiplication applies, even though the whole tree does not have uniform branching.

The tree is also a check on your outcome definition: if two different paths lead to what the question regards as the same final object, the path count is too large. The diagram does not remove that modeling responsibility.
