# A lottery ticket is a set, not a sequence

Imagine writing six different numbers on a ticket. Rearranging their order does not create a new entry: the ticket containing 2, 8, 17, 25, 36, and 49 is the same set however you read it. That is why the six-from-49 lottery begins with a combination count.

## Specify the experiment before counting

The slide's simplified model has 49 numbers, $1,2,\ldots,49$. A ticket contains six distinct numbers, and six distinct main winning numbers are selected uniformly without replacement. We first ignore any bonus number or additional prize rules.

The number of possible six-number winning sets is

$$
N=\binom{49}{6}
=\frac{49!}{6!\,43!}
=\frac{49\cdot48\cdot47\cdot46\cdot45\cdot44}
{6\cdot5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1}
=13{,}983{,}816.
$$

The numerator counts ordered draws: 49 choices first, 48 next, and so on. Every fixed six-number set appears in exactly $6!$ of those orders. Dividing by $6!$ removes that repeated counting.

## Equal likelihood comes from the draw model

Under uniform drawing without replacement, each ordered draw has the same probability, and each unordered set has the same number of orders. Therefore the $13{,}983{,}816$ sets are equally likely.

For a fixed ticket, matching all six main numbers means the draw equals that one set. Its probability is $1/N$. A ticket with consecutive numbers has exactly the same jackpot probability as any other specific ticket in this model. A pattern that looks less random is not automatically less likely as one specified outcome.

There are two equivalent ways to count matches: hold the ticket fixed and vary the winning set, or hold a winning set fixed and count possible tickets. Either way, the universe splits into six numbers in the reference set and 43 outside it.

## The two-pool recipe

To match exactly k main numbers, take k from the six reference numbers and $6-k$ from the other 43:

$$
N_k=\binom6k\binom{43}{6-k},
\qquad
P(K=k)=\frac{N_k}{\binom{49}{6}},
\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,6.
$$

K denotes the number of matches. This formula will guide the next pages. Each ticket is counted once because its matched and unmatched parts are uniquely determined.

## Check your understanding

Why is $49^6$ the wrong number of tickets? Why is $49\cdot48\cdots44$ still too large?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Identify the two different counting mistakes</summary>

The count $49^6$ permits repeated numbers and distinguishes their positions. The product $49\cdot48\cdots44$ correctly prevents repetition but still treats different orders of the same six numbers as different tickets. Dividing that product by $6!$ gives the correct unordered count, $13{,}983{,}816$.

</details>
