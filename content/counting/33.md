# Stars and bars turns quantities into positions

When a purchase cares only about how many items of each type you take, record those quantities directly. Stars and bars is a way to encode that quantity list as a simple sequence whose positions we know how to count.

## First distinguish the two combination models

Without replacement, choosing $r$ distinct objects from $n$ objects gives

$$
\binom nr=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}.
$$

With replacement, a category can be chosen repeatedly. “Buy five donuts from three flavors” means you may buy several chocolate donuts, for instance. We treat donuts of the same flavor as indistinguishable for the purchase and assume each flavor has sufficient stock.

Let the flavors always be ordered chocolate, vanilla, strawberry. A purchase is a triple of nonnegative integers

$$
(c,v,s),\qquad c+v+s=5.
$$

The triple $(3,0,2)$ means three chocolate donuts, no vanilla donuts, and two strawberry donuts. It does not say which donut you chose first.

## Encode a purchase with five stars and two bars

Each star represents one donut. Two bars divide the stars into the three flavors, always in the same flavor order:

```text
Chocolate | Vanilla | Strawberry
   ***    |         |     **
```

Removing the spaces gives the slide's encoding:

```text
***||**
```

It has five stars and two bars. There are no stars between the bars because the vanilla quantity is zero.

| Encoding | Chocolate | Vanilla | Strawberry |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `***||**` | 3 | 0 | 2 |
| `**|*|**` | 2 | 1 | 2 |
| `|*****|` | 0 | 5 | 0 |
| `*****||` | 5 | 0 | 0 |
| `||*****` | 0 | 0 | 5 |

Bars can be adjacent or appear at an end. Those positions are essential: they allow zero quantities. Requiring a star between every pair of bars would accidentally require at least one donut of each flavor.

## Count bar positions, not individual donuts

There are $5+2=7$ symbol positions. Choose which two contain bars. Every unchosen position must contain a star:

$$
\boxed{\binom72=\frac{7\times6}{2\times1}=21.}
$$

Equivalently, choose the five star positions: $\binom75=21$. We do not multiply by $5!$ because the stars are identical. We do not multiply by $2!$ because the bars are identical.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Why this correspondence proves the count</summary>

Given a purchase $(c,v,s)$, write $c$ stars, a bar, $v$ stars, a bar, and $s$ stars. That produces exactly one encoding.

Given an encoding with five stars and two bars, count the stars before the first bar, between the bars, and after the second bar. That recovers exactly one purchase. These two procedures undo each other.

Therefore every purchase corresponds to exactly one selection of bar positions, and every selection of bar positions corresponds to exactly one purchase. Counting the encodings counts the purchases with no missing cases and no duplicate representations.

</details>

## Verify 21 without any formula

Fix the chocolate quantity $c$. The remaining $5-c$ donuts are split between vanilla and strawberry. Vanilla may receive any integer from zero through $5-c$, so there are $6-c$ possibilities.

| Chocolate quantity | Possible vanilla–strawberry splits |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 6 |
| 1 | 5 |
| 2 | 4 |
| 3 | 3 |
| 4 | 2 |
| 5 | 1 |

The cases are disjoint and exhaustive, giving $6+5+4+3+2+1=21$. This independent route checks the stars-and-bars answer.

## Generalize to any number of categories

To choose $r$ items from $n\geq1$ labeled categories with repetitions allowed, use $r$ identical stars and $n-1$ identical bars. There are $r+n-1$ positions. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\binom{n+r-1}{n-1}=\binom{n+r-1}{r}.}
$$

This also counts nonnegative integer solutions to

$$
x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n=r.
$$

The categories are distinguishable: three chocolate and two strawberry differs from two chocolate and three strawberry. What is ignored is the order of individual selections within the purchase.

## Why the answer is not three to the fifth power

The count $3^5=243$ describes **ordered sequences** of five flavor choices. Several such sequences lead to the same quantities. For $(3,0,2)$, the number of sequences is

$$
\frac{5!}{3!0!2!}=10.
$$

But $(5,0,0)$ has just one sequence, and $(2,1,2)$ has $5!/(2!1!2!)=30$. The overcount is not constant. There is no single factorial by which we can divide $3^5$ to get the number of purchases.

Stars and bars avoids that difficulty by giving each purchase exactly one description from the beginning.

## If every flavor must appear

If we require at least one donut of every flavor, first reserve one for each. Of five donuts, two remain to distribute freely. The answer becomes

$$
\binom{3+2-1}{2}=\binom42=6.
$$

More generally, with lower bounds $x_i\geq a_i$, put $y_i=x_i-a_i$. If $R=r-\sum_i a_i\geq0$, the remaining nonnegative counts sum to $R$ and give $\binom{n+R-1}{R}$ possibilities. If $R<0$, none are possible. Upper bounds need additional work because ordinary stars and bars imposes no capacity limit.

## Check your understanding

How many purchases of four donuts from three flavors are possible? How many if every flavor must appear? Encode the purchase with zero chocolate, one vanilla, and three strawberry donuts.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal all three answers</summary>

With zeros allowed,

$$
\binom{3+4-1}{4}=\binom64=15.
$$

With at least one of each flavor, reserve three donuts and distribute the remaining one. There are three possibilities, also given by $\binom31=3$.

The requested purchase $(0,1,3)$ is encoded as

```text
|*|***
```

The leading bar expresses zero chocolate. There are four stars and two bars, for six positions in total.

</details>
