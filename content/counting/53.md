# Two named players meet with probability two over n

A tournament can have an enormous number of possible brackets and results. Fortunately, this problem asks for something that symmetry can count without listing them. Every completed single-elimination tournament has exactly the same number of played matches: one for each eliminated player.

## Keep the source's tournament rules explicit

There are $n\ge2$ players, including two specified players A and B. In each round:

1. If the number of survivors is odd, choose one survivor uniformly at random to receive a bye into the next round.
2. Pair the remaining survivors uniformly at random.
3. Each pair plays once; the loser is eliminated and the winner advances.
4. Repeat with new random pairings until one champion remains.

All players have equal ability. We model each match as a fair choice of winner, with no player label favored by the rules or past history. A bye advances a player without a played match and eliminates nobody.

The question is: **What is the probability that A and B ever play one another, in any round?**

## Count matches before counting tournament histories

Start with n players and finish with one champion. Exactly $n-1$ players must be eliminated. Each played match eliminates one player, so every completed tournament contains

$$
n-1
$$

matches, regardless of the pattern of byes.

There are

$$
\binom n2=\frac{n(n-1)}2
$$

possible unordered pairs of players. A pair can meet at most once: after their first match, one of its members is eliminated.

Because the pairing, bye, and winning rules treat every label identically, every specified pair has the same probability p of ever meeting. Spread the fixed total of $n-1$ played pairs across the $\binom n2$ symmetrically situated possible pairs:

$$
\binom n2p=n-1,
\qquad
\boxed{p=\frac{n-1}{\binom n2}=\frac2n}.
$$

<summary>Make the symmetry argument precise with indicators</summary>

For each unordered pair $\{i,j\}$, define $I_{ij}=1$ if that pair plays during the tournament and $I_{ij}=0$ otherwise. Since no pair can play twice,

$$
\sum_{i<j}I_{ij}=n-1
$$

in every tournament realization. Take expectations. An indicator's expectation is the probability of its event, so

$$
\sum_{i<j}P(i\text{ meets }j)=n-1.
$$

Relabeling the players does not change the tournament's probability model. Therefore all those meeting probabilities are the same p, and $\binom n2p=n-1$ follows.

No independence between the indicators is needed. In fact, match possibilities strongly depend on one another. Linearity of expectation works despite that dependence.

## Check the odd-player case explicitly

With three players A, B, and C, each receives the first-round bye with probability $1/3$.

| Bye recipient | When A and B meet | Contribution to the probability |
|---|---|---:|
| C | A and B play immediately. | $1/3$ |
| A | B must beat C, then meet A in the final. | $(1/3)(1/2)=1/6$ |
| B | A must beat C, then meet B in the final. | $(1/3)(1/2)=1/6$ |

The total is $1/3+1/6+1/6=2/3$, matching $2/n$. A bye does not invalidate the match-count argument because it causes no elimination.

## Know when the symmetry argument applies

With two players, they must meet, and $2/n=1$. With eight players, any specified pair has probability $2/8=1/4$ of meeting under the stated random, symmetric model.

If A and B occupy fixed positions in a predetermined bracket, pairs may no longer have equal meeting probabilities. In a four-player fixed bracket, a pair assigned to meet in the first round meets with probability one; a pair in opposite first-round matches meets only if both advance, with probability $1/4$ under fair matches. The formula $2/n$ concerns the random symmetric model, not either fixed-position case.

Unequal abilities can also break label symmetry. The tournament still has $n-1$ matches, but an individual pair's probability need not equal the average over all possible pairs.

## Check your understanding

For ten equal-ability players under these rules, what is the probability that A meets B? How many opponents does A meet on average? Why is the second answer not necessarily an integer?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Apply the same probability to every possible opponent</summary>

The probability for A and B is $2/10=1/5$. Player A has nine possible opponents, each with meeting probability $1/5$. Adding their indicators gives an expected number of opponents of

$$
9\cdot\frac15=\frac95=1.8.
$$

A plays an integer number of matches in any one tournament. The expectation is an average over many possible tournaments, so it need not be an integer. Early elimination produces fewer matches; surviving longer produces more.

</details>
