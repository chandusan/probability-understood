# Read and use “n choose k”

The symbol $\binom nk$ packages one specific question: how many size-$k$ subsets can we select from $n$ distinct objects? Its several notations all represent the same count; understanding the question is more useful than memorizing the typography.

## Three names for one number

The slide uses

$$
\binom nk=C(n,k)={}_nC_k.
$$

Read each as **“$n$ choose $k$.”** For integers $0\leq k\leq n$,

$$
\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
=\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k(k-1)\cdots1}.
$$

The numerator first counts ordered selections of $k$ distinct objects. The denominator removes the $k!$ orderings of each selected set.

For example,

$$
\binom83=\frac{8\times7\times6}{3\times2\times1}=56.
$$

There is no need to expand all of $8!$ and $5!$ before canceling. The falling-product form shows exactly which factors survive.

## Useful meaning-based checks

Choosing zero objects has one result: the empty set. Choosing all $n$ objects also has one result. Thus

$$
\binom n0=\binom nn=1.
$$

Choosing one object has $n$ possibilities, so $\binom n1=n$. And choosing $k$ objects determines the complementary set of $n-k$ objects left out:

$$
\binom nk=\binom n{n-k}.
$$

These are checks on your reasoning as well as identities for computation. For example, $\binom85=\binom83=56$: specifying five selected people is equivalent to specifying the three omitted people.

## Why it is called a binomial coefficient

A binomial is an expression with two terms, such as $x+y$. Expanding $(x+y)^n$ means multiplying $n$ copies of that binomial. Each product term is formed by choosing either $x$ or $y$ from each copy.

To produce $x^k y^{n-k}$, choose which $k$ of the $n$ copies supply $x$. There are $\binom nk$ ways. That is why the same number appears as a coefficient in algebra; the next page develops this argument fully.

## Check your understanding

Without separately calculating two factorial quotients, explain why $\binom{12}{2}=\binom{12}{10}$ and find their value.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the correspondence and count</summary>

Every selected pair has a unique ten-element complement, and every ten-element subset leaves out a unique pair. This one-to-one correspondence makes the counts equal.

$$
\binom{12}{2}=\frac{12\times11}{2}=66.
$$

So both counts are 66. The correspondence also explains why the numerical equality must hold before we calculate anything.
