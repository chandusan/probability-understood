# A flush shares one suit but avoids consecutive ranks

Once the suit of a flush is chosen, the deck effectively becomes a smaller collection of 13 cards, one at each rank. Choose any five of them, then remove the ten choices that form a straight. This is the suit-first counterpart of the previous rank-first calculation.

## Choose the suit and five ranks

There are four possible common suits. Inside a fixed suit, selecting five distinct cards is the same as selecting five of the 13 ranks:

$$
\binom{13}{5}=1{,}287.
$$

The broad count of hands with all five cards in the same suit is therefore

$$
4\binom{13}{5}=4\cdot1{,}287=5{,}148.
$$

There is no factor $4^5$ here. The single common suit has already been chosen, so every selected rank now corresponds to one specific card.

## Remove the suited straights

The broad count includes 36 nonroyal straight flushes and four royal flushes. The ordinary-flush category excludes all of them:

$$
N_{\text{flush}}=5{,}148-36-4=5{,}108.
$$

Equivalently, each suit offers ten straight rank sets among its 1,287 five-rank sets. Thus

$$
N_{\text{flush}}
=4\left(\binom{13}{5}-10\right)
=4\cdot1{,}277=5{,}108.
$$

The probability is

$$
P(\text{ordinary flush})
=\frac{5{,}108}{2{,}598{,}960}
\approx0.00196540,
$$

or about $0.196540\%$.

## Compare the three distinct-rank constructions

All of these hands have five different ranks, but the restrictions differ:

| Category | Rank choice | Suit choice per rank set |
|---|---|---|
| High card | 1,277 nonstraight rank sets | 1,020 assignments that are not all one suit |
| Ordinary straight | 10 straight rank sets | 1,020 assignments that are not all one suit |
| Ordinary flush | 1,277 nonstraight rank sets | 4 common-suit assignments |
| Any straight flush | 10 straight rank sets | 4 common-suit assignments |

These four cases partition the distinct-rank hands. The last case is then split into nonroyal and royal categories for the final table.
