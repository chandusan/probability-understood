# The three rules every probability must obey

A probability model distributes a total weight of one across possible outcomes. Its rules prevent negative weights, ensure that something in the sample space occurs, and let us combine alternatives without counting the same outcome twice.

## Identify the objects first

The sample space $S$ contains the possible outcomes. An event $E$ is a measurable subset of $S$: it collects outcomes satisfying a question. The probability function $P$ assigns a real number to each event.

For a die, $E=\{2,4,6\}$ is a set, while $P(E)$ is its numerical probability. Keeping these roles separate makes the axioms easier to read.

## Non-negativity

For every event,

$$P(E)\ge0.$$

A probability cannot be a negative weight. An event with probability zero is allowed. In a finite uniform model it must be empty; in a continuous model an individual possible outcome can have probability zero.

## Normalization

The entire sample space is certain:

$$P(S)=1.$$

This fixes the total probability budget. A model whose event weights sum to more or less than one is not a complete valid assignment.

## Countable additivity

If $E_1,E_2,\ldots$ are **pairwise disjoint**, meaning $E_i\cap E_j=\varnothing$ whenever $i\ne j$, then

$$P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_i\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(E_i).$$

The union means at least one event happens. Disjointness means an outcome cannot belong to two of these events, so the sum contains no repeated weight. “Countable” allows a numbered infinite sequence; finite additivity is included as a special case.

For a fair die, $E_1=\{1,2\}$ and $E_2=\{5\}$ are disjoint. Their union has probability $2/6+1/6=3/6$. If we instead combine $\{1,2\}$ and $\{2,3\}$, the outcome $2$ is in both sets; simple addition would count it twice.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Why the other familiar bounds follow</summary>

Additivity applied to $S$ and the empty set gives

$$1=P(S\cup\varnothing)=P(S)+P(\varnothing)=1+P(\varnothing),$$

so $P(\varnothing)=0$. Also, $E$ and its complement $E^c$ are disjoint and cover $S$. Therefore

$$P(E)+P(E^c)=1.$$

Since $P(E^c)\ge0$, we obtain $P(E)\le1$. Thus the familiar range $0\le P(E)\le1$ follows from the three axioms; it is not an additional independent requirement.

</details>

## Check your understanding

Someone assigns probabilities $0.4,0.4,0.4$ to the three outcomes of a sample space. Each number is between zero and one. Is this valid?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

No. Distinct singleton outcomes are disjoint, so their union must have probability $0.4+0.4+0.4=1.2$. But their union is the entire sample space, whose probability must be one. Checking individual bounds is necessary but does not replace checking normalization and additivity.

</details>
