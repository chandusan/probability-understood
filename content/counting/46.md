# Two pairs choose an unordered pair of paired ranks

A two-pair hand contains two ranks that each occur twice and one different single rank. The two paired ranks have the **same structural role**. Calling one the “first pair” and the other the “second pair” would label an order the hand itself does not possess.

## Choose the paired ranks together

For rank pattern $\{x,x,y,y,z\}$, start with

$$
\binom{13}{2}=78
$$

choices for the two paired ranks. Aces-and-sevens is one choice, not two choices depending on which rank we name first.

For each chosen rank, choose two of its four suits. Once the ranks are selected, they distinguish the two suit selections:

$$
\binom42\binom42=6\cdot6=36.
$$

For example, a pair of aces in hearts and spades together with a pair of sevens in clubs and diamonds is a different hand from changing the ace suit pair. No extra factor of two is needed to assign suits to an invented first and second pair.

## Add the kicker

The fifth card's rank must avoid both paired ranks. That leaves

$$
\binom{11}{1}=11
$$

rank choices, followed by four possible suits.

The complete count is

$$
N_{\text{two pair}}
=\binom{13}{2}\binom42^2\cdot11\cdot4
=78\cdot36\cdot11\cdot4
=123{,}552.
$$

Its probability under uniform dealing is

$$
P(\text{two pairs})
=\frac{123{,}552}{2{,}598{,}960}
\approx0.04753902,
$$

or about $4.7539\%$.

## Why choose eleven ranks rather than twelve?

Two ranks have already been used, not one. A fifth card of either paired rank would produce three of one rank and two of the other: a full house. Choosing from the remaining eleven ranks guarantees the fifth card is a genuine kicker.

This illustrates a recurring strategy: build the desired category directly rather than first building a broader class and then trying to remove every unwanted outcome.

## Check your understanding

A student counts the paired ranks as $13\cdot12$ and gets twice the source's total. What precisely was counted twice? How could their method be repaired?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Exchange the two equivalent roles</summary>

Every hand appears once with x called the first paired rank and y the second, and again with those names exchanged. The two pairs have equal multiplicity, so the exchange does not change the hand. Dividing by $2!$ repairs the count:

$$
\frac{13\cdot12}{2}\binom42^2\cdot11\cdot4
=123{,}552.
$$

For a full house, the rank roles will be different—triple versus pair—so that same division would be wrong there.
