# Exactly one match uses one winning choice and five others

Build a one-match ticket in two parts: choose its single winning number, then choose its five nonwinning numbers. These decisions identify the whole ticket uniquely. No extra decision about the order of the six numbers is needed.

## Count the two parts

There are six choices for the one number that matches the fixed winning set:

$$
\binom61=6.
$$

The remaining five numbers must all come from the 43 outside that set:

$$
\binom{43}{5}=962{,}598.
$$

The multiplication principle therefore gives

$$
N_1=\binom61\binom{43}{5}
=6\cdot962{,}598
=5{,}775{,}588.
$$

For example, if the winning set is $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, a one-match ticket might be $\{4,8,17,25,36,49\}$. Its winning part is $\{4\}$ and its nonwinning part is $\{8,17,25,36,49\}$. The decomposition has no ambiguity.

## Turn the count into a probability

All six-number sets are equally likely under our model, so

$$
P(K=1)=\frac{5{,}775{,}588}{13{,}983{,}816}
\approx0.4130194505.
$$

Thus exactly one match occurs with probability about $41.3019\%$. It is common in this simplified experiment, but remains a no-prize result under the slide's illustrative payout schedule.

## Two tempting extra factors are wrong

There is no factor of six for “where the matched number goes”: the ticket is an unordered set, with no first or last position. We already used the factor six to select **which winning number** is present.

Likewise, do not choose the other five numbers from the 48 numbers other than that selected winner. Those 48 would still include the other five winning numbers, allowing additional matches and violating “exactly one.” They must come from the 43 nonwinning numbers.

The guiding question is always: does the construction generate every intended ticket once and only once?

## Check your understanding

Find the probability of either zero or one match. Why may we add the two counts directly?

<summary>Combine disjoint categories</summary>

No ticket has both zero and one match, so the events are disjoint. Their combined count is

$$
6{,}096{,}454+5{,}775{,}588=11{,}872{,}042.
$$

Therefore

$$
P(K\le1)=\frac{11{,}872{,}042}{13{,}983{,}816}
\approx0.8489844260.
$$

The complement, at least two matches, has probability approximately $0.1510155740$. That still includes the no-prize category of exactly two matches.
