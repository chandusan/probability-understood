# Choose a counting model before a formula

Two questions organize many selection problems: do positions matter, and may a category or object be selected again? A third question appears when arranging a collection: are all its objects distinguishable, or do some swaps leave the arrangement unchanged?

## The four basic selection cases

Here $n$ is the number of available distinct objects or categories, and $r$ is the number of selections.

| Does order matter? | Can selections repeat? | Count | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yes | No | $n!/(n-r)!$ | A podium with distinct winners |
| Yes | Yes | $n^r$ | A length-$r$ code using $n$ symbols |
| No | No | $\binom nr$ | An $r$-person team from $n$ people |
| No | Yes | $\binom{n+r-1}{r}$ | $r$ donuts from $n$ flavors |

The two no-replacement formulas require $0\leq r\leq n$. The repeated-selection cases assume each category remains available as often as needed. Capacity limits or extra eligibility constraints require additional reasoning.

For example, three ordered selections from five distinct objects give $5\times4\times3=60$ without replacement and $5^3=125$ with replacement. The difference is whether the pool shrinks after each choice.

## A fixed collection with repeated types

The slide also gives a different formula: the number of distinguishable arrangements of a **multiset**. Suppose there are $N$ items in total, with $m_1$ of one type, $m_2$ of another, and so on, where $m_1+\cdots+m_q=N$. Then

$$
\boxed{\frac{N!}{m_1!m_2!\cdots m_q!}.}
$$

Here the quantities of each type are fixed before arranging. This is different from choosing a repeated selection, where the quantities themselves may vary.

## Why repeated types produce factorial divisors

Consider the letters of BANANA: three A's, two N's, and one B. Temporarily label the repeated letters $A_1,A_2,A_3,N_1,N_2,B$. The six labeled objects have $6!$ arrangements.

Erase the subscripts. Each visible word has $3!$ possible assignments of the A labels and $2!$ possible assignments of the N labels. Every visible arrangement therefore has $3!2!$ labeled descriptions:

$$
\frac{6!}{3!2!}=60.
$$

Once again, equal multiplicity justifies division. We are not ignoring all order: BANANA and ANANAB are different. We are ignoring only swaps between identical letters.

## Check your understanding

How many four-character strings contain exactly two A's, one B, and one C? Why are neither $3^4$ nor $\binom{6}{4}$ the answer?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the correct model</summary>

The composition is fixed and positions matter, so use the multiset-arrangement formula:

$$
\frac{4!}{2!1!1!}=12.
$$

The expression $3^4$ allows every composition, including AAAA. The repeated-combination count $\binom{3+4-1}{4}=\binom64$ counts unordered flavor-like quantities rather than arrangements of the specified letters. Different questions can use the same three symbols and four positions while requiring different counts.
