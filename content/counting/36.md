# Prize categories partition the possible outcomes

A lottery ticket can match zero through six main winning numbers. Those seven possibilities are mutually exclusive and exhaustive: every ticket belongs to exactly one category. This makes them useful events to count before discussing prizes.

## “Exactly” and “at least” answer different questions

Let K be the number of main-number matches. The category “three matches” means $K=3$: the other three ticket numbers must be outside the winning set. The event “at least three matches” means

$$
\{K\ge3\}=\{K=3\}\cup\{K=4\}\cup\{K=5\}\cup\{K=6\}.
$$

The four events are disjoint, so their probabilities can be added. In the slide's simplified prize schedule, that union is the event of receiving one of the listed prizes.

Likewise, the no-prize event is $\{K=0,1,2\}$. Receiving no prize is not the same as matching no numbers: one or two matches also fall into the no-prize group here.

## Why the partition is a useful check

The seven counts must add to the number of all tickets:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{6}\binom6k\binom{43}{6-k}=\binom{49}{6}.
$$

This is more than an algebra identity. The left side sorts every six-number set by how many of its elements come from the reference group of six. Nothing is omitted, and no ticket belongs to two categories.

The seven probabilities must therefore total one. If they do not, either a category is missing, some outcomes were counted twice, or an arithmetic error occurred.

## Check your understanding

A ticket matches four main numbers. Does it belong to the event “exactly three matches”? Does it belong to “at least three matches”? Can both statements be true?

It does not belong to $\{K=3\}$ because its count is four. It does belong to $\{K\ge3\}$. Although a four-match ticket contains several three-number winning subsets, that does not make it a three-match ticket. Counting those subsets would answer a different question and could count the same ticket repeatedly.
