# Three cars and eight ordered stories

For three cars, a complete outcome records the turn made by each car in order. Counting those stories gives the event sizes; fair, independent turn choices are the extra assumptions that make the stories equally likely.

## Write the whole space once

Label the cars first, second, and third. With $L$ for left and $R$ for right,

$$S=\{LLL,LLR,LRL,LRR,RLL,RLR,RRL,RRR\}.$$

There are $2\cdot2\cdot2=8$ possible sequences. We are assuming each car has those two choices and every listed combination is allowed. Real traffic may have different turn rates or dependencies; the exercise is an idealized model.

## Exactly one right turn

The event is

$$A=\{LLR,LRL,RLL\}.$$

The single $R$ can occupy any one of the three positions. With independent turns and $P(R)=P(L)=1/2$, each sequence has probability $(1/2)^3=1/8$. Hence

$$\boxed{P(A)=\frac38=0.375.}$$

The order matters: $LLR$ and $RLL$ identify different cars turning right, even though both have the same total number of right turns.

## All cars turn the same way

This event has two possibilities:

$$C=\{LLL,RRR\}.$$

Therefore

$$\boxed{P(C)=\frac28=\frac14=0.25.}$$

The events $A$ and $C$ are disjoint: exactly one right turn cannot also mean all three turns match. They do not exhaust the space, because the three sequences with exactly two right turns are in neither event.

## Keep the possibilities, change the weights

In the experiment, begin at $p=0.50$ and highlight each event. Then move the right-turn probability to $0.80$. The same eight sequences remain listed, but their weights change; dividing by eight no longer gives an event's probability.

Suppose turns remain independent, with a common right-turn probability $p$. Each one-right sequence has weight $p(1-p)^2$, so

$$P(A)=3p(1-p)^2.$$

The all-right and all-left sequences have weights $p^3$ and $(1-p)^3$, giving

$$P(C)=p^3+(1-p)^3.$$

At $p=0.8$, these become $0.096$ and $0.520$. The counts are still three and two, but the relative probability of the events is quite different. At $p=0$ or $1$, some listed outcomes have zero probability.

## Check your understanding

Under the equal-turn model, what is the probability that at least two cars turn right?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

The event is $\{LRR,RLR,RRL,RRR\}$, with four equally likely sequences. Thus its probability is $4/8=1/2$. It combines exactly two right turns and exactly three right turns, which are disjoint cases. The count is not merely two because the phrases “two rights” and “three rights” summarize differently sized collections of sequences.
