# Multiply when every first choice has the same number of continuations

A two-stage count is a collection of groups: each first-stage choice starts a group containing all its possible second-stage continuations. If there are $n_1$ groups and every group has $n_2$ members, the total is $n_1n_2$.

## Derive the product instead of memorizing it

Suppose there are three shirt colors and four sizes available for every color. Fixing the color leaves four complete color-size choices. The full count is therefore

$$4+4+4=3\cdot4=12.$$

The general version is

$$\boxed{N=\underbrace{n_2+\cdots+n_2}_{n_1\text{ groups}}=n_1n_2.}$$

Each complete outcome must correspond to one construction path. A color-size pair has exactly one first-stage color and one second-stage size, so this requirement is satisfied.

## The second count must hold after every allowed first choice

Suppose red shirts come in four sizes, blue in three, and green in two. The number of second choices now depends on the first. There is no single $n_2=4$ valid for all branches:

$$N=4+3+2=9.$$

We can still count perfectly well by adding the branch sizes. The product rule is the special case in which those sizes are equal.

The source says $n_2$ is the number of ways the second step can be performed once the first is completed. The essential qualification is that this same number works **regardless of which allowed first choice was made**.

## Constant counts do not mean unchanged options

Choose gold and silver medalists from eight runners. Once a gold medalist is chosen, the available names for silver change, but their number is always seven. Therefore $8\cdot7$ is valid.

The second choice depends on the first: the gold medalist is unavailable for silver. That dependence does not obstruct the counting product. This rule is about the number of continuations, not probabilistic independence between choices.

## What does “order matters” mean here?

A sequential record distinguishes its positions or roles: first choice and second choice. Swapping gold and silver changes the result. If the question instead asks for an unordered two-person team, two selection orders may describe the same final team. Multiplying sequential choices then counts ordered records, and a further correction or a unique construction is needed for teams.

## Check your understanding

Choose a first letter and a different second letter from $\{A,B,C,D\}$. How many two-letter records are possible? Does the changing set of available letters invalidate multiplication?

<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

There are four first choices and exactly three remaining choices after each first choice, so there are $4\cdot3=12$ records. The available second letters vary in identity but not in number. For example, after $A$ they are $B,C,D$; after $B$ they are $A,C,D$. This is enough for the counting product, even though the second letter cannot be chosen independently of the restriction imposed by the first.
