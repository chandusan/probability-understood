# Add disjoint cases

If every object belongs to exactly one of several groups, count each group and add. The addition rule works because every object contributes one count, and none contributes twice.

## Translate the slide's notation

Let $A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots$ be pairwise disjoint sets:

$$A_i\cap A_j=\varnothing\qquad\text{whenever }i\ne j.$$

Let $n_i=|A_i|$ be the number of elements in the $i$th set, and let

$$A=\bigcup_i A_i.$$

Then the counting rule is

$$\boxed{|A|=\sum_i|A_i|=n_1+n_2+n_3+\cdots.}$$

For the finite counting problems here, only finitely many nonempty finite groups are involved. For a countable family, the same idea can yield an infinite total; it is not an instruction to turn infinite cardinalities into a probability ratio.

## Each outcome needs exactly one home

Suppose a bookstore has twelve history titles, eight mathematics titles, and five poetry titles, with each title assigned to exactly one category. Choosing one title gives $12+8+5=25$ possibilities.

But if a history-of-mathematics title appears in both the history and mathematics groups, the categories are no longer disjoint. The raw sum counts that title twice. We must either subtract overlaps or redesign the categories so each title has one home.

A useful check is to ask two questions: does every desired outcome enter some group, and can any desired outcome enter more than one? The first prevents omissions; the second prevents duplicate counts.

## Use disjoint cases even when the original descriptions overlap

For “ace or diamonds,” use the cases “diamond” and “ace that is not a diamond.” The counts are $13$ and $3$. Every qualifying card is in one of these cases, and no qualifying card is in both, so the answer is $16$.

For dice summing to at least ten, use exact sums $10,11,12$. Their counts are $3,2,1$. Different exact sums cannot occur for the same pair, so the total is six.

The art is often in choosing the cases, not in performing the addition.

## Check your understanding

A class has $12$ students who play piano and $8$ who play guitar. Three play both. How many play at least one instrument? Give a disjoint-case count.

<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

Take “plays piano” and “plays guitar but not piano” as the two disjoint groups. They contain $12$ and $8-3=5$ students, giving $17$. Equivalently, inclusion–exclusion gives $12+8-3=17$. The uncorrected $20$ counts the three dual-instrument players twice.
