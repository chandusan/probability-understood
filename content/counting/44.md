# See one pair as a matched rank plus three kickers

The source's infographic highlights a useful construction: isolate the two cards of the repeated rank, then inspect the other three cards one at a time. For **exactly one pair**, those three kickers must all have different ranks and must avoid the paired rank.

## Read the actual illustrated hand

The visual uses two queens as the pair, together with a seven, a three, and a ten as the kickers. One corresponding hand shown is

$$
Q\heartsuit,\ Q\spadesuit,\ 7\clubsuit,\ 3\diamondsuit,\ 10\spadesuit.
$$

Its rank pattern is

$$
\{x,x,y,z,u\}.
$$

Here $x=Q$, while y, z, and u represent the three distinct other ranks. These letters identify structural roles; they do not label five dealing positions.

The repeated suit of spades is harmless. A pair is a repetition of **rank**, not of suit. Several kickers may share a suit without changing the category, because the paired cards already have different suits and prevent a five-card flush.

## Why the kicker restrictions matter

Starting with the two queens, compare three possible completions:

| Three additional ranks | Result |
|---|---|
| 7, 3, 10 | Exactly one pair: all kicker ranks differ. |
| 7, 7, 10 | Two pairs: queens and sevens. |
| Q, 7, 10 | Three of a kind: three queens. |

Choosing “any three remaining cards” would allow all these completions. To count exactly one pair, we must choose **three distinct ranks from the other twelve**, then one suit for each selected rank.

## The picture becomes a counting plan

The construction has four decisions:

$$
\underbrace{13}_{\text{paired rank}}
\cdot
\underbrace{\binom42}_{\text{suits of the pair}}
\cdot
\underbrace{\binom{12}{3}}_{\text{three kicker ranks}}
\cdot
\underbrace{4^3}_{\text{kicker suits}}.
$$

The next slide works through their numerical values. The infographic's statement that one pair makes up approximately $42.26\%$ of all five-card hands is consistent with the exact count $1{,}098{,}240$ out of $2{,}598{,}960$.

The memory aid is the structure itself: two cards of one rank, then three distinct kicker ranks. Once that picture is clear, each factor in the count has a job.

## Check your understanding

You have $Q\heartsuit$ and $Q\spadesuit$. May all three kickers be clubs? May one of their ranks be Q? Explain which restriction matters.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Separate suit reuse from rank reuse</summary>

The three kickers may all be clubs if their ranks are distinct and none is Q, for example $3\clubsuit,7\clubsuit,10\clubsuit$. That still gives exactly one pair. A queen kicker would create at least three queens and move the hand out of the one-pair category. The relevant kicker restriction is on ranks; each permitted rank still offers four suit choices.
