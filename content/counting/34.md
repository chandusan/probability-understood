# Build a solution you can check

Counting is less about recognizing a formula at a glance than about creating a description that represents every valid outcome exactly once. Formulas are useful components; your model explains why they fit together.

## Begin with one concrete outcome

Before calculating, write an example of the finished object: a shelf, a pipeline, an executive assignment, a subset, or a donut quantity list. Then ask:

1. Which changes create a different outcome: swapping positions, changing membership, or changing quantities?
2. Can an object or category appear again?
3. Which objects are distinguishable?
4. What restrictions must every finished outcome satisfy?

This prevents applying the right formula to the wrong question. A pipeline is an ordered sequence; a donut purchase is a quantity list. They can both involve three categories and five selections while counting entirely different things.

## Choose a construction strategy

| Structure in the problem | Useful strategy |
|---|---|
| A sequence of decisions | Multiply fixed branch counts |
| Disjoint alternative cases | Count each case and add |
| A small forbidden set | Count all outcomes and subtract invalid ones |
| Objects required to stay together | Form blocks, then arrange within blocks |
| Many equivalent ordered descriptions | Divide by a proved constant multiplicity |
| Repeated category quantities | Find a stars-and-bars encoding |

These strategies can be combined. For the books, we multiply block orders by internal orders. For the pipeline, we multiply choices and subtract a forbidden set. For combinations, we count ordered descriptions and divide by the size of each group.

## Audit the result with three questions

**Coverage:** Does every valid outcome arise from the construction?

**Uniqueness:** Does each arise once, or have you proved exactly how often it is counted?

**Validity:** Can your construction accidentally produce something forbidden?

A small version of the problem often exposes mistakes. List all outcomes for three objects before trusting a formula for thirty. A second counting method provides an especially useful check when it reaches the same total through a different construction.

## Check your understanding

Four distinct books consist of two mathematics books, one chemistry book, and one history book. How many shelf orders keep the mathematics books adjacent? Solve it in two ways.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal two matching constructions</summary>

**Blocks:** Treat the mathematics pair as one block. There are three units to arrange, giving $3!$ orders, and two internal orders for the mathematics pair:

$$
3!\times2!=12.
$$

**Starting position:** The adjacent mathematics pair can start at shelf position 1, 2, or 3. For each start, its two books have two internal orders, and the other two books have two orders in the remaining positions:

$$
3\times2\times2=12.
$$

Every valid shelf has one mathematics-block starting position, so this second construction also counts each shelf once. The agreement checks the reasoning as well as the arithmetic.
