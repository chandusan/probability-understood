# From counting executives to probability

Counting possibilities gives a probability only when the possibilities being compared have equal chances. For this exercise, imagine choosing three different executives uniformly from the committee, with the roles distinguished.

## Define the random selection model

The committee contains ten people: six male and four female. We choose a chairperson, a vice-chairperson, and a treasurer, with no person holding two roles. Assume all $P(10,3)$ ordered assignments are equally likely. Sequentially selecting each role uniformly from the remaining people is one way to achieve that model.

The desired event is that all three selected executives are male.

## Count all assignments and favorable assignments

There are

$$
P(10,3)=10\times9\times8=720
$$

possible assignments. To make all executives male, fill the three positions from the six male committee members:

$$
P(6,3)=6\times5\times4=120.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{P(\text{all male})=\frac{120}{720}=\frac16.}
$$

The probabilities are not determined by the committee's composition alone. A selection procedure that favors particular candidates or uses role-specific eligibility could give a different answer.

## Why unordered counting gives the same probability

We can instead ignore the roles when asking whether the selected people are all male. There are $\binom{10}{3}=120$ three-person groups, of which $\binom63=20$ are entirely male:

$$
\frac{\binom63}{\binom{10}3}=\frac{20}{120}=\frac16.
$$

Why is this legitimate? Each group corresponds to exactly $3!=6$ ordered assignments, so under the uniform assignment model all groups are equally likely. Dividing both numerator and denominator by the same multiplicity leaves the ratio unchanged.

Do not mix representations: 120 favorable **ordered assignments** divided by 120 total **unordered groups** is meaningless.

## A sequential probability check

The same result follows by tracking what remains:

$$
\frac6{10}\times\frac5{9}\times\frac4{8}=\frac16.
$$

The factors change because selecting a male removes one male and one person from the pool. The expression $(6/10)^3$ would instead correspond to independent draws with replacement.

## Check your understanding

Under the same model, what is the probability that all three executives are female?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the answer</summary>

$$
\frac{P(4,3)}{P(10,3)}=\frac{4\times3\times2}{720}=\frac1{30}.
$$

Equivalently, $\binom43/\binom{10}3=4/120=1/30$. The two methods agree because each selected group still has six assignments to roles.
