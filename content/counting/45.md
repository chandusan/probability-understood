# Count exactly one pair without extra order factors

A one-pair hand has a single repeated rank and three distinct other ranks. Build it by selecting those rank groups, then the suits within them. Every resulting hand reveals its own paired rank and kicker ranks, so the construction counts each hand exactly once.

## Four choices specify the whole hand

**First, choose the paired rank.** There are

$$
\binom{13}{1}=13
$$

choices, from twos through aces.

**Second, choose two suits at that rank.** There are

$$
\binom42=6
$$

unordered suit pairs. For queens, hearts-and-spades is the same selection as spades-and-hearts.

**Third, choose the three kicker ranks.** They must be distinct and must avoid the paired rank, leaving

$$
\binom{12}{3}=220
$$

choices.

**Fourth, choose a suit for each kicker rank.** Each chosen rank contributes one of its four cards, giving

$$
4\cdot4\cdot4=4^3=64
$$

assignments. Once the ranks are fixed, assigning clubs to the seven and hearts to the ten is different from assigning hearts to the seven and clubs to the ten. The ranks distinguish the suit choices even though the hand is unordered.

## Multiply the compatible decisions

$$
N_{\text{one pair}}
=13\binom42\binom{12}{3}4^3
=13\cdot6\cdot220\cdot64
=1{,}098{,}240.
$$

Therefore

$$
P(\text{exactly one pair})
=\frac{1{,}098{,}240}{2{,}598{,}960}
\approx0.42256903.
$$

That is about $42.2569\%$, matching the earlier infographic's rounded figure.

## Why we do not multiply by arrangements

There is no factor of $5!$ for arranging the cards: every dealing order belongs to the same hand. There is also no factor of $3!$ for ordering the kicker ranks. The combination $\binom{12}{3}$ already chooses their unordered set.

The $4^3$ factor does not secretly order the kickers. It assigns a suit to each of three distinct selected ranks. Think of labeling the three columns with their actual ranks before making suit choices.
