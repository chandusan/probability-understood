# Replace a long list with a reliable construction

Counting becomes useful when we can describe how to build each outcome exactly once. The next rules let us count a large collection by splitting it into disjoint cases or assembling each outcome through a sequence of choices.

## Addition combines alternative cases

Suppose a meal is either one of three sandwiches or one of two soups, with exactly one item chosen. The choices are disjoint alternatives, so there are $3+2=5$ meals.

The word “or” alone is not sufficient. If two categories overlap, their common outcomes must be handled; the ace-of-diamonds example showed why. Addition applies directly when the cases do not overlap.

## Multiplication combines stages

Now suppose a meal contains a sandwich **and** a soup. There are three sandwich choices and, after each sandwich, two soup choices. Each sandwich starts a group of two complete meals, so there are $3\cdot2=6$.

Multiplication is repeated addition of equally sized groups. This is the central idea behind the product rule, and a tree diagram makes those groups visible.

## Count complete outcomes, not descriptions

If an outcome can be built in more than one way, counting construction paths may overcount it. For instance, selecting Ada then Ben and selecting Ben then Ada are two ordered records. If the final outcome is the unordered team $\{\text{Ada},\text{Ben}\}$, those records describe the same team.

Before multiplying, establish the match between the records you count and the outcomes the question distinguishes. Later formulas for combinations will account for repeated orderings, but the underlying issue is already present.

## A practical decision process

Describe one complete outcome. Separate alternative cases until they are disjoint. Within a case, choose a sequence of construction steps and count the options after each partial history. Multiply only when every history at a stage has the stated number of next choices. If the branch counts differ, add their totals or split into more uniform cases.

This is more reliable than choosing a formula based solely on whether the question contains the words “and” or “or.” The structure of the outcomes determines the rule.

## Check your understanding

A traveler can choose one of four trains or one of three buses for a single journey. Alternatively, a two-leg journey requires one of four trains followed by one of three buses. How many plans are there in each case?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

For a single journey, the alternatives are disjoint and there are $4+3=7$ plans. For the two-leg journey, assume every train can be followed by every bus; then there are $4\cdot3=12$ ordered plans. If schedules forbid some connections, the second answer must count the valid buses after each train instead of automatically multiplying by three.
