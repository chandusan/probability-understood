# Fill ordered positions without reusing objects

A permutation of size $k$ records both **which objects were selected** and **which position each object occupies**. Once an object fills one position, it is unavailable for the others. That shrinking pool is the source of the falling product.

## Count the choices position by position

Start with $n$ distinct objects and fill $k$ labeled positions without replacement. The labels might be first, second, and third place, or chairperson, vice-chairperson, and treasurer.

| Position | Available objects |
|---|---:|
| First | $n$ |
| Second | $n-1$ |
| Third | $n-2$ |
| $k$th | $n-k+1$ |

Although the identities of the available objects depend on earlier choices, their **number** is the same after every valid history of the same length. The multiplication rule therefore gives

$$
P(n,k)=n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1).
$$

The slide also writes this as $P_{n,k}$ or ${}_nP_k$. They refer to the same count.

## Why the factorial quotient stops at the right place

The factorial $n!$ continues multiplying all the way to 1. We only want the first $k$ factors, so divide away the tail:

$$
\boxed{P(n,k)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}.}
$$

For five objects filling three positions,

$$
P(5,3)=5\times4\times3=\frac{5!}{2!}=60.
$$

The sequence $A,B,C$ differs from $B,A,C$: the selected set is the same, but the first two positions contain different objects.

## Check the boundaries of the formula

The usual domain is integers $0\leq k\leq n$. The slide emphasizes $k<n$, but the same formula includes $k=n$: arranging all objects gives $n!/0!=n!$. It also includes $k=0$: there is one empty sequence, so $P(n,0)=1$.

If $k>n$, no such sequence exists without replacement. If repetition is permitted, the pool does not shrink and the count becomes $n^k$. Always decide whether an object can be used again before reaching for a formula.

## Check your understanding

Seven distinct runners enter a race. How many possible gold–silver–bronze results are there, assuming no ties? Why is $7^3$ inappropriate?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the result</summary>

$$
P(7,3)=7\times6\times5=210.
$$

Once a runner wins gold, that same runner cannot also win silver or bronze. The expression $7^3=343$ includes impossible repetitions such as the same runner occupying all three places.

</details>
