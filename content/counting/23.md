# Does changing the order change the outcome?

The difference between permutations and combinations is a decision about what an outcome records. Imagine swapping two selected objects. If that creates a different finished result, order matters. If it leaves the result unchanged, those two descriptions belong to the same combination.

## Compare two questions about the same people

Suppose the available people are $A,B,C,D$. Choosing $A$ and then $B$ might mean either of these things:

| What is being recorded? | Are $AB$ and $BA$ different? |
|---|---|
| Chairperson, then treasurer | Yes: different role assignments |
| A two-person team | No: the same two members |

There are $P(4,2)=4\times3=12$ ordered selections but only $\binom42=6$ two-person teams. Every team has two orderings. For example, the team $\{A,B\}$ corresponds to $AB$ and $BA$.

“Order” need not mean chronological order. Labeled locations, jobs, ranks, and stages also make positions different. Conversely, a selection can happen over time while the final outcome ignores that time order: picking a committee member first does not make that person the chairperson unless the rules say so.

For distinct objects selected without replacement, each $k$-object set has exactly $k!$ orderings. That fixed number will let us convert a permutation count into a combination count.

## Write one outcome before choosing a formula

An ordered outcome might be $(A,C,B)$; an unordered one might be $\{A,B,C\}$. This tiny modeling step prevents many mistakes. A question containing the word “choose” does not automatically call for combinations: choosing a password still produces an ordered sequence.

Likewise, the word “arrange” suggests order but does not settle whether repetition is allowed or whether some objects are indistinguishable. Those are separate questions.

## Check your understanding

A teacher chooses three of five students to represent the class, then names one of the three as spokesperson. How many outcomes are there?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the two-layer count</summary>

Choose the team in $\binom53=10$ ways, then choose its spokesperson in three ways:

$$
10\times3=30.
$$

Only one role is distinguished. Counting $P(5,3)=60$ would unnecessarily distinguish the other two representatives from each other. Alternatively, choose the spokesperson first in five ways and the other two from the remaining four in $\binom42=6$ ways: $5\times6=30$.
