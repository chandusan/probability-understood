# Five rules that keep probability calculations honest

The counting formula handles equally likely outcomes. The broader probability rules work even when weights differ: they tell us how to combine events while respecting the total probability budget and correcting overlaps.

## The five rules on the slide

| Rule | Formula | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Non-negativity | $P(E)\ge0$ | Check that a result is a valid weight |
| Total probability | $P(S)=1$ | Account for the whole space |
| Disjoint addition | $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$ | The events have no common outcomes |
| Complement | $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$ | “Not A” is easier to describe |
| General addition | $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$ | The events may overlap |

The notation $A^c$ and the slide's bar over $A$ both mean the complement relative to the chosen sample space.

## See why overlap must be removed once

Add $P(A)$ and $P(B)$. An outcome belonging only to $A$ contributes once; an outcome belonging only to $B$ contributes once; an outcome in both contributes twice. The union should include every outcome only once, so subtract the overlap's weight once.

For a fair die, take $A=\{2,4,6\}$ and $B=\{4,5,6\}$. The intersection is $\{4,6\}$, and

$$P(A\cup B)=\frac36+\frac36-\frac26=\frac46.$$

The union itself is $\{2,4,5,6\}$, which confirms the result by direct counting.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Derive the rules from disjoint pieces</summary>

Split the union into three disjoint regions:

$$A\cup B=(A\setminus B)\cup(A\cap B)\cup(B\setminus A).$$

Also,

$$P(A)=P(A\setminus B)+P(A\cap B),$$

$$P(B)=P(B\setminus A)+P(A\cap B).$$

Adding the last two equations and subtracting one copy of $P(A\cap B)$ gives exactly the probability of the three disjoint regions. That proves general addition. If the overlap is empty, its probability is zero, recovering the disjoint rule.

For the complement rule, $A$ and $A^c$ are disjoint and their union is $S$. Therefore $P(A)+P(A^c)=1$, so $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$.

</details>

## A useful distinction

Disjoint does not mean independent. Two positive-probability disjoint events cannot occur together, so knowing one happened rules out the other. Independence would instead preserve the other's probability. The counting rules here use set relationships; a separate argument is needed whenever probabilistic independence is claimed.

## Check your understanding

Suppose $P(A)=0.6$, $P(B)=0.5$, and $P(A\cap B)=0.2$. Find the probability of at least one event, then the probability of neither.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

“At least one” is the union, so its probability is $0.6+0.5-0.2=0.9$. “Neither” is the complement of the union, giving $1-0.9=0.1$. Adding $0.6+0.5$ without correcting the overlap would give $1.1$, an immediate sign that repeated weight has entered the calculation.

</details>
