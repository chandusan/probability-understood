# Award the medals one role at a time

Gold and silver are different roles, so an outcome specifies both who wins and which medal each person receives. Choosing those roles in sequence creates every two-medal result exactly once.

## Choose gold

There are eight finalists and no ties. Any one of the eight can be named gold medalist, so the first stage has eight possibilities.

This is a count of possible assignments, not a claim that each runner has an equal chance in a real race.

## Choose silver after gold is fixed

The gold medalist cannot also receive silver. For any chosen gold medalist, seven distinct runners remain available for silver.

Although the list of names changes with the first choice, its length is always seven. That is exactly the condition needed for the product rule:

$$\boxed{8\cdot7=56.}$$

We stop after silver because the question does not ask us to rank the other six runners. Their finishing orders would add detail that the requested outcome does not include.

## Why the tempting alternatives fail

The expression $8\cdot8=64$ allows one runner to occupy both roles. The eight invalid diagonal assignments explain the difference $64-8=56$.

The sum $8+7=15$ counts options at separate stages rather than complete two-role outcomes. Each gold choice must be combined with each permitted silver choice.

Dividing $56$ by two would treat a swap of gold and silver as the same result. That would be suitable for an unordered pair of medalists, but it would discard which runner won which medal. Ada-gold/Ben-silver and Ben-gold/Ada-silver are distinct here.

## Connect this example to the tree

Imagine eight first branches, one for each possible gold medalist. From every branch, draw seven branches for silver. The $56$ leaves are the medal assignments. The construction is unique: each leaf names one gold medalist and one different silver medalist.

This reasoning will soon become the general formula for selecting an ordered set of distinct objects. For now, the stages provide the proof.

## Check your understanding

How many assignments are possible if bronze is also awarded, with no ties? If you only care which two people receive gold or silver, without distinguishing their medals, how many pairs are possible?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

For gold, silver, and bronze there are $8\cdot7\cdot6=336$ assignments. Each chosen runner is removed before the next role is filled.

For the unordered pair of gold-or-silver recipients, each pair appears twice in the $56$ ordered assignments, once in each medal order. Thus there are $56/2=28$ pairs. The different answer comes from a different definition of what counts as the same outcome.
