# Count possibilities without losing the story

Probability asks how likely an event is. When all the underlying outcomes are equally likely, answering that question becomes a counting problem: how many outcomes make the event true, compared with how many could occur altogether?

## The bridge from the previous chapter

We already know how to describe an experiment with a sample space $S$, an event $E\subseteq S$, and a probability assignment. For a finite equally likely space,

$$P(E)=\frac{|E|}{|S|}.$$

The vertical bars mean “number of elements” here. This formula moves the difficulty into two counts. Listing all outcomes works for two dice; it becomes impractical for a shelf of ten books or a long sequence of choices.

Counting principles let us obtain the same result without writing every outcome. Their purpose is to make our list implicit, while ensuring that nothing is omitted or counted twice.

## Begin with one concrete outcome

Suppose eight runners compete for gold and silver. One result is “Ada wins gold; Ben wins silver.” Reversing the names changes the result because the medals are different roles.

There are eight choices for gold, then seven for silver. Every two-medal result appears once in that construction, so the count is $8\cdot7=56$.

This count does not say the $56$ results have equal probabilities. A stronger runner may be more likely to win. Counting possibilities and weighting possibilities are separate tasks.

## Four questions before a formula

Before counting, ask what makes two outcomes different, whether order matters, whether the same object can be used again, and whether any restrictions remove combinations.

Those questions explain why two similar-looking problems can have different answers. Choosing two students for the same job differs from choosing a president and a secretary. A four-digit code may allow repetition; arranging four distinct books does not.

The chapter first reviews the probability connection, then builds addition and multiplication rules, and finally uses those rules to count ordered arrangements. Each formula will come from a construction you can explain.

## Check your understanding

You count $56$ possible gold-and-silver results. Can you immediately conclude that a particular pair of runners has probability $1/56$ of winning those exact medals?

No. The count establishes the number of distinct outcomes. Probability $1/56$ additionally requires all those ordered medal results to be equally likely. If the experiment artificially assigns two medals uniformly to different runners, that assumption holds. In an actual race it need not. The denominator in a probability calculation must represent equally weighted outcomes, or we must add their individual probability weights instead.
