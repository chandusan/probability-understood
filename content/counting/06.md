# Why the dice exercise has denominator 36

To find the chance of a sum of at least ten, count the ordered die pairs that produce those sums. The denominator is $36$ because those pairs are equally likely; the eleven possible sums are not.

## Set up the equally likely outcomes

Label the dice first and second. A full outcome is $(i,j)$, where each coordinate is one of $1,2,3,4,5,6$.

There are $6\cdot6=36$ pairs. Assuming the dice are fair and independent, every pair has probability

$$\frac16\cdot\frac16=\frac1{36}.$$

Fairness alone does not establish independence. For example, a mechanism that always makes one die copy the other could give fair individual faces but only six doubles. The familiar $36$ equal weights use both assumptions.

## Solve the exercise by disjoint sums

Let $A$ be the event that the sum is at least $10$. The possible favorable sums are $10,11,12$.

| Sum | Ordered pairs | Count |
|---|---|---:|
| $10$ | $(4,6),(5,5),(6,4)$ | $3$ |
| $11$ | $(5,6),(6,5)$ | $2$ |
| $12$ | $(6,6)$ | $1$ |

A single pair cannot have two different sums, so these cases are disjoint. Their counts add to $3+2+1=6$:

$$\boxed{P(A)=\frac6{36}=\frac16\approx0.1667.}$$

## Why three favorable sums out of eleven is wrong

There are eleven possible sums, $2$ through $12$, but they have different numbers of routes. Sum $2$ comes only from $(1,1)$; sum $7$ comes from six pairs. A random pair does not pick its sum uniformly.

The source picture's six-by-six grid preserves exactly the information we need. Each cell has equal probability, and cells with sums $10,11,12$ cluster in one corner. Counting the highlighted cells gives the correct probability.
