# Read a counting tree from its root to its leaves

A tree diagram turns a sequence of choices into visible paths. A complete path from the starting point to a final leaf represents one complete outcome; counting leaves is therefore the goal.

## Decode the source picture

The leftmost point is the root, before any choices. Its branches lead to the possible first-stage choices. The red brace labeled $n_1$ counts these branches.

At each first-stage node, another fan of branches represents the second choices. Every fan is labeled $n_2$. That repeated label is the key information: each of the $n_1$ first choices has the same number $n_2$ of continuations.

The total number of final leaves is therefore

$$N=n_1n_2.$$

The drawing is a schematic of the rule. Its fan shapes help identify the repeated structure; the symbols carry the general counts.

## Count leaves, not all the nodes

For two first choices and three second choices after each, there are six complete paths. The two intermediate first-stage nodes are partial outcomes, not additional final outcomes. Adding the root and intermediate nodes would answer a different question: how many nodes are drawn.

Label first choices $A,B$ and second choices $1,2,3$. The leaves correspond to

$$A1,A2,A3,B1,B2,B3.$$

Each record appears once. Reading the tree in this way provides both a count and a method of listing outcomes without repetitions.

## Change the branch counts

Use two stages in the experiment. Begin with two first choices and three second choices, then trace a selected path. Turn on unequal branches: the first first-stage branch now has one extra continuation. Predict the new leaf total before reading it.

The unequal example has group sizes $4$ and $3$, so the count is $4+3=7$. The old product $2\cdot3=6$ misses the new outcome. A tree does not require equal branches; only the shortcut of multiplying a fixed count at each level does.

## A tree is a counting picture, not automatically a probability model

The edges here describe allowed choices and contain no probability weights. Equal-looking branches need not be equally likely. To turn the picture into a probability tree, we would attach appropriate conditional probabilities to the edges. That is a separate modeling step.

## Check your understanding

A root has three first branches. They lead to $2$, $4$, and $3$ terminal branches respectively. How many complete outcomes are there? Why is $3\cdot4$ wrong?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

There are $2+4+3=9$ leaves. Multiplying $3\cdot4=12$ would pretend that all three first branches have four continuations. The tree only gives four continuations after one of them. Counting each group and adding is the valid rule for these unequal branch sizes.
