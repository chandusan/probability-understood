# When probability becomes a ratio of counts

If a finite sample space contains $N$ equally likely outcomes, each receives the same share of the total probability one. An event containing $m$ of those outcomes receives $m$ of those shares.

## Derive the weight of one outcome

Write

$$S=\{o_1,o_2,\ldots,o_N\}.$$

Let every singleton outcome have probability $q$. Because the singleton events are disjoint and together fill the sample space,

$$1=P(S)=\underbrace{q+q+\cdots+q}_{N\text{ terms}}=Nq.$$

Therefore

$$P(\{o_i\})=\frac1N.$$

The braces emphasize that probability is assigned to the event containing the outcome. The slide's shorter notation $P(o_i)$ uses the same idea informally.

## Collect the favorable outcomes

If $E$ contains $m$ outcomes, add their weights:

$$\boxed{P(E)=\frac mN=\frac{n(E)}{n(S)}=\frac{|E|}{|S|}.}$$

Both $n(A)$ and $|A|$ denote the number of elements of a finite set $A$. “Favorable” means that the outcome satisfies the event; it does not necessarily mean something desirable happened.

For a uniformly selected integer from $\{1,\ldots,10\}$, the event “multiple of three” is $\{3,6,9\}$. Its probability is $3/10$.

## Why this satisfies the axioms

Counts are nonnegative. The whole space has probability $N/N=1$. For disjoint events, no outcome is repeated, so the count of their union is the sum of their counts. Dividing all those counts by the same $N$ gives additivity.

Even a countably indexed disjoint family causes no difficulty in a finite space: at most $N$ members can be nonempty. The infinite sum therefore reduces to finitely many nonzero contributions.

## The assumption you must not skip

Finite does not imply equally likely. The count of heads in two fair independent tosses has outcomes $0,1,2$, but their probabilities are $1/4,1/2,1/4$. The four underlying sequences $TT,TH,HT,HH$ are equally likely; the three counts are not.

When outcomes have different weights, the general rule is to sum the weights of the outcomes in the event. The ratio of counts is a special case justified by equal weights.

## Check your understanding

A box has nine blue tickets and one red ticket, all physically distinct and selected uniformly. Is the probability of red $1/2$ because there are two possible colors?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

No. The ten tickets are equally likely, while the two colors are not. One of the ten tickets is red, so $P(\text{red})=1/10$. Recording only the color groups nine underlying outcomes into blue and one into red; it does not give the two groups equal probability.
