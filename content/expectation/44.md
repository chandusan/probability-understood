# Recognize the binomial theorem inside the MGF

For $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$ and $q=1-p$, the MGF is a sum of $n+1$ weighted exponential values. The simplification to $(pe^t+q)^n$ follows by matching each component of the sum to the binomial theorem, not by an additional probability assumption.

## Write the two expressions in matching form

The binomial theorem says

$$
(A+B)^n=\sum_{x=0}^n\binom nx A^xB^{n-x}.
$$

The MGF calculation gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_X(t)
&=\sum_{x=0}^ne^{tx}\binom nxp^xq^{n-x}\\
&=\sum_{x=0}^n\binom nx(pe^t)^xq^{n-x}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Set $A=pe^t$ and $B=q$. The summation limits, combination coefficient, and exponents then match exactly, so

$$\boxed{M_X(t)=(pe^t+q)^n.}$$

The expression $pe^t$ is an algebraic quantity here; it does not need to be a probability. The binomial theorem applies to numbers and functions beyond probabilities.

## See the entire calculation for two trials

For $n=2$, the probabilities of $X=0,1,2$ are $q^2,2pq,p^2$. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_X(t)&=q^2e^0+2pq e^t+p^2e^{2t}\\
&=q^2+2q(pe^t)+(pe^t)^2\\
&=(q+pe^t)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The middle coefficient two represents the two success–failure orders. The general coefficient $\binom nx$ plays the same role for $n$ trials.

## A second derivation using the individual trials

Write $X=I_1+\cdots+I_n$, where $I_i$ is one on success and zero on failure. Then

$$e^{tX}=e^{tI_1}\cdots e^{tI_n}.$$

Independence lets the expectation of this product factor:

$$M_X(t)=\prod_{i=1}^nE(e^{tI_i})=(q+pe^t)^n.$$

This second proof explicitly uses independence at the factoring step. Without independence, the product of the individual MGFs need not describe the sum. The first proof uses the binomial PMF, which already incorporates the same independent-trial assumptions.
