# Average a reciprocal without finding its distribution

Let $X$ have density $f(x)=3x^2$ for $0<x<1$, and zero elsewhere. Find $E(1/X)$. Although $1/X$ becomes very large near zero, the density becomes small there; the expectation depends on the product of these two effects.

## Check the model and identify the transformed quantity

The density integrates to one:

$$\int_0^13x^2\,dx=[x^3]_0^1=1.$$

Set $g(x)=1/x$. The expectation-of-a-function rule gives

$$E(1/X)=\int_0^1\frac1x\,3x^2\,dx.$$

There is no need to derive the density of $Y=1/X$ before calculating this average.

## Simplify before integrating

On the support, $x>0$, so

$$\frac1x\,3x^2=3x.$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{E(1/X)=\int_0^13x\,dx
=\left[\frac32x^2\right]_0^1=\frac32.}
$$

More formally, because the original integrand involves $1/x$, the integral can be read as a limit from $\varepsilon$ to $1$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. After simplification it equals $\frac32(1-\varepsilon^2)$, whose limit is finite.

## Why $1/E(X)$ would answer a different question

Here

$$E(X)=\int_0^13x^3\,dx=\frac34,$$

so $1/E(X)=4/3$, not $3/2$. Averaging reciprocals gives more influence to small observations than taking the reciprocal of a single average does.

## A useful boundary check

For powers of the reciprocal,

$$E(X^{-r})=3\int_0^1x^{2-r}\,dx.$$

The integral is finite when $2-r>-1$, or $r<3$. In particular, $E(X^{-2})=3$ is finite, but $E(X^{-3})=3\int_0^1dx/x$ diverges.

This shows why “$X$ lies between zero and one” does not automatically make every transformed expectation finite. The transformation can become too large near an endpoint, and its growth must be compared with the probability density there.
