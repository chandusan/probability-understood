# Standard deviation measures spread in the original units

The population standard deviation is $\sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}=\sqrt{E[(X-\mu)^2]}$. It is the nonnegative square root of variance. Squaring made deviations add without cancellation; taking the square root gives a spread measure in the same units as the observations.

## Interpret the sequence of operations

Standard deviation is a **root mean square deviation**:

1. Measure each deviation from the mean.
2. Square it.
3. Take the probability-weighted average.
4. Take the square root.

It is not generally the mean absolute deviation $E(|X-\mu|)$. Both describe spread, but they weight large deviations differently.

For the outage distribution, $\operatorname{Var}(X)=1.41$, so

$$\sigma=\sqrt{1.41}\approx1.1874\text{ outages}.$$

This number summarizes spread; it does not say every month is exactly $1.1874$ outages away from the mean.

## Standardize values into a common unit

For $0<\sigma<\infty$, define

$$Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}.$$

Subtracting $\mu$ moves the mean to zero. Dividing by $\sigma$ measures deviations in units of the original standard deviation. Algebraically,

$$E(Z)=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Z)=1.$$

A value $z=2$ means two standard deviations above the mean. This makes scales comparable, which can matter in distance-based methods and numerical optimization.

## Standardization does not make a distribution normal

If $X$ has a long right tail, subtracting and dividing by constants preserve that relative shape. A standardized skewed variable remains skewed. Mean zero and variance one do not identify the normal distribution.

Likewise, the familiar claim that about $99.7\%$ of observations lie within three standard deviations is a normal-model result, not a rule for every distribution. Under only finite variance, Chebyshev's inequality provides the weaker guarantee that at least $1-1/9=8/9$ of the probability lies in the interval $[\mu-3\sigma,\mu+3\sigma]$.

For practical anomaly thresholds, the distribution and context matter as well as the scale. Standard deviation is a useful measuring unit, not a universal definition of what is unusual.
