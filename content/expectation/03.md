# The mean is a balance point and a long-run average

Expectation has two complementary interpretations. It is the balance point of probability placed along a number line, and, under suitable repeated-sampling assumptions, it is the value approached by the sample average. Neither interpretation says that one observation must equal the mean.

## Place probability masses on a ruler

Imagine a weight of size $p(x)$ placed at position $x$. A proposed balance point $c$ balances the ruler when the total signed turning effect is zero:

$$
\sum_x (x-c)p(x)=0.
$$

A weight to the right contributes positively; a weight to the left contributes negatively. Expand the expression:

$$
\sum_x xp(x)-c\sum_x p(x)=E(X)-c.
$$

Since probabilities sum to $1$, the turning effect vanishes exactly when $c=E(X)$. For a density, replace the sum by $\int(x-c)f(x)\,dx$ and the same reasoning works.

For example, put probability $3/4$ at $0$ and $1/4$ at $4$. The mean is $1$. About that point, the left contribution is $(-1)(3/4)$ and the right contribution is $(3)(1/4)$: they cancel.

## Repeated observations explain the weighting

If $X_1,\ldots,X_N$ are independent observations from the same distribution with a finite absolute mean, the law of large numbers says that

$$
\overline X_N=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_N}{N}
$$

approaches $E(X)$ as the number of observations grows, in the appropriate probabilistic sense. For a finite distribution, the basic intuition is that the fraction of observations equal to $x$ settles near $p(x)$. The sample average then settles near $\sum_x xp(x)$.

This is a statement about the average, not a claim that individual observations become less random or that every finite sample has exactly the expected composition.

## Balance does not imply equal probability on either side

In the example above, $75\%$ of the probability is below the mean and $25\%$ is above it. The smaller right-hand probability acts through a larger distance.

The mean balances **probability times distance**. A median instead divides the distribution by accumulated probability. The two coincide for many symmetric distributions but need not coincide in general.
