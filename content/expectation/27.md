# Read skewness from tails, then check the actual moment

A skewness diagram highlights the direction in which a distribution extends unevenly. The numerical coefficient, however, comes from averaging cubed standardized deviations. Pictures provide intuition; the formula makes the comparison precise and also explains why zero skewness alone does not prove symmetry.

## Compare the three familiar shapes

| Shape | Typical visual feature | Third central moment |
|---|---|---|
| Right skew | A longer or more influential right tail | Positive in the moment-based sense |
| Symmetric | Equal probabilities in reflected regions | Zero, when finite |
| Left skew | A longer or more influential left tail | Negative in the moment-based sense |

“Right skew” names the tail's direction, not where most observations sit. A distribution with many small values and a few very large values often has positive skewness.

The common order mode–median–mean from left to right is an intuition for many simple unimodal right-skewed distributions, not a universal theorem for all distributions.

## What symmetry guarantees

If deviations $d$ and $-d$ receive matching probabilities throughout the distribution, their cubes cancel pair by pair. Thus symmetry around a finite mean implies $E[(X-\mu)^3]=0$, when the third absolute moment exists.

This reasoning uses an exact probability-preserving pairing, not simply a graph that looks roughly balanced.

## A counterexample to the reverse implication

Let the distribution be

| $x$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $1$ | $3$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $p(x)$ | $4/20$ | $5/20$ | $10/20$ | $1/20$ |

Its mean is

$$E(X)=\frac{-8-5+10+3}{20}=0.$$

Its third central moment is therefore its third raw moment:

$$E(X^3)=\frac{-32-5+10+27}{20}=0.$$

But the distribution is not symmetric about zero: there is probability at $3$ and none at $-3$, and the probabilities at $1$ and $-1$ differ. The variance is $2>0$, so its standardized skewness is genuinely zero despite the asymmetry.

The cancellation of one weighted sum supplies only one constraint. Symmetry requires all reflected probabilities to agree, a much stronger condition. Use skewness together with the distribution itself rather than treating one number as a complete shape description.
