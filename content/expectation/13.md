# Why a uniform distribution has its mean at the midpoint

Suppose $X$ is uniformly distributed on $[a,b]$, where $a<b$. Its density is $1/(b-a)$ throughout the interval and zero outside it. We will derive $E(X)=(a+b)/2$ by integration and then explain the same answer through symmetry.

## Equal density means equal-width intervals have equal probabilities

For $a\le c<d\le b$,

$$P(c\le X\le d)=\frac{d-c}{b-a}.$$

The constant density compensates for the interval's width: multiplying $1/(b-a)$ by the full width $b-a$ gives total area $1$. Individual points still have probability zero.

## Calculate the expectation

$$
\begin{aligned}
E(X)&=\int_a^b x\frac1{b-a}\,dx\\
&=\frac1{b-a}\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_a^b\\
&=\frac{b^2-a^2}{2(b-a)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Factor the numerator as $(b-a)(b+a)$, then cancel $b-a$:

$$\boxed{E(X)=\frac{a+b}{2}.}$$

The cancellation is legitimate because $a<b$, so $b-a\ne0$.

## See why the answer had to be central

Let $m=(a+b)/2$. A narrow interval around $m-d$ has the same probability as its reflected interval around $m+d$. Their distances from $m$ are equal and opposite. Their contributions to the average deviation therefore cancel.

Pairing every interval this way gives $E(X-m)=0$, so the balance point is $m$. This is a precise meaning of symmetry: reflecting the distribution around $m$ preserves its probabilities while changing the sign of each deviation.

For example, if waiting time is uniform from $2$ to $8$ minutes, the mean is $5$ minutes. It is not $1/(8-2)=1/6$; that is the density height, measured per minute.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Does the midpoint rule hold for every distribution on $[a,b]$?</summary>

No. It holds for the uniform distribution and, more generally, for distributions symmetric about that midpoint when the mean exists. The density $3x^2$ on $[0,1]$ puts more probability near $1$ and has mean $3/4$, not $1/2$.

</details>
