# What the main MGF properties mean

A moment-generating function turns probability-weighted powers into derivatives of one function. The core properties are $M_X(0)=1$, $M_X^{(k)}(0)=E(X^k)$, and a power-series expansion whose coefficients contain the moments. These statements use an MGF finite on an open interval around zero.

## A compact translation table

| Feature of $M_X$ | Information about $X$ |
|---|---|
| Value at zero, $M_X(0)$ | Total probability, $1$ |
| Slope at zero, $M_X'(0)$ | Mean, $E(X)$ |
| Second derivative at zero, $M_X''(0)$ | Second raw moment, $E(X^2)$ |
| $M_X''(0)-[M_X'(0)]^2$ | Variance |
| $k$th derivative at zero | $k$th raw moment |

“Raw” matters: derivatives of $M_X(t)$ generate powers of $X$, not powers of $X-E(X)$.

## Generate central moments by first centering

If $Y=X-\mu$, then

$$
M_Y(t)=E(e^{t(X-\mu)})=e^{-\mu t}M_X(t).
$$

Differentiating this centered MGF at zero generates $E[(X-\mu)^k]$. The deterministic factor $e^{-\mu t}$ can be taken outside the expectation because it does not depend on the random outcome.

## Scaling and shifting have a simple expression

More generally, for constants $a,b$,

$$
\boxed{M_{aX+b}(t)=e^{bt}M_X(at),}
$$

where the right side is finite. To see it, write $e^{t(aX+b)}=e^{bt}e^{(at)X}$ and average. This is the exponential counterpart of the familiar location-and-scale rules for moments.

## MGFs can help with sums

If $X,Y$ are independent and their MGFs exist near zero, then

$$M_{X+Y}(t)=E(e^{tX}e^{tY})=M_X(t)M_Y(t).$$

Independence is required for factoring that expectation. This is why the MGF of a binomial sum of independent Bernoulli trials becomes a power of a one-trial MGF.

The source focuses on the first three basic properties. These additional identities show why learning the definition is useful beyond memorizing a way to calculate one mean.
