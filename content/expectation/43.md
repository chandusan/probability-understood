# Set up the binomial moment-generating function

Let $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$: $X$ counts successes in $n$ independent trials, each with success probability $p$. Write $q=1-p$. We want the MGF $M_X(t)=E(e^{tX})$, and will see why the binomial theorem turns its sum into $(q+pe^t)^n$.

## Recall exactly what the binomial model assumes

The number of trials $n$ is fixed. Every trial has two categories, success and failure. Trials are independent and share the same success probability. Therefore

$$P(X=x)=\binom nxp^xq^{n-x},\qquad x=0,1,\ldots,n.$$

The combination counts success positions; the remaining factors give the probability of one particular pattern.

## Replace each count with its exponential value

By the expectation definition,

$$M_X(t)=\sum_{x=0}^ne^{tx}\binom nxp^xq^{n-x}.$$

Since $e^{tx}=(e^t)^x$, the two factors involving the exponent $x$ combine:

$$e^{tx}p^x=(pe^t)^x.$$

Hence

$$M_X(t)=\sum_{x=0}^n\binom nx(pe^t)^xq^{n-x}.$$

This is the binomial expansion of a sum raised to the $n$th power:

$$\boxed{M_X(t)=(q+pe^t)^n.}$$

It is finite for every real $t$ because the count $X$ is bounded.

## Check easy cases before differentiating

At $t=0$,

$$M_X(0)=(q+p)^n=1.$$

For one trial, $n=1$, the formula becomes $q+pe^t$. This is exactly the weighted average of $e^{t\cdot0}=1$ on failure and $e^{t\cdot1}=e^t$ on success.

For $p=0$, $X=0$ certainly, so $M_X(t)=1$. For $p=1$, $X=n$ certainly, so $M_X(t)=e^{nt}$. The expression agrees with both limiting cases.

The formula has not replaced probability reasoning with a new rule: it packages the same binomial probabilities into a convenient function. Differentiating that function next will retrieve the mean without summing $x\binom nxp^xq^{n-x}$ directly.
