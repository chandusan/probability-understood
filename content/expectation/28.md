# Fourth moments, kurtosis, and excess kurtosis

The fourth central moment is $\mu_4^0=E[(X-\mu)^4]$. Fourth powers are nonnegative and give unusually large influence to observations far from the mean. Standardizing gives kurtosis; subtracting three gives excess kurtosis, which is the convention used in the source's displayed formula.

## Begin with the size of each contribution

A deviation of one standard deviation contributes $1^4=1$ before probability weighting. A deviation of three standard deviations contributes $3^4=81$. Thus even a relatively small probability far from the center can have a large effect on the average.

With $Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma$, define

$$
\boxed{\beta_2=E(Z^4)=\frac{\mu_4^0}{\sigma^4}}
$$

as ordinary kurtosis. Here $\sigma^4=(\sigma^2)^2$ is the square of the variance, not the fourth power of the variance.

## Why subtract three?

The normal distribution has standardized fourth moment $3$. Using it as a reference leads to

$$\boxed{\gamma_2=\beta_2-3=\frac{\mu_4^0}{\sigma^4}-3.}$$

This is **excess kurtosis**. Under this convention a normal distribution has value $0$, not $3$. Whenever two sources quote “kurtosis,” check which convention they use before comparing numbers.

## Compute a small example

For $X=-1$ or $1$ with equal probabilities, $\mu=0$, $\sigma^2=1$, and $\mu_4^0=1$. Therefore

$$\beta_2=1,\qquad \gamma_2=1-3=-2.$$

There are no values many standard deviations from the mean: every standardized observation has magnitude exactly one.

## Interpret the number carefully

Kurtosis describes the standardized fourth-power average and is useful for understanding the influence of extreme deviations. It should not be interpreted solely as peak height. Different distributions can have similar peaks but very different tails, or the same kurtosis with different detailed shapes.

The fourth moment must be finite, and variance must be positive. If extremely heavy tails make the fourth moment infinite, a finite kurtosis coefficient is unavailable. Subtracting three does not fix that issue; it only changes the reference point of a defined finite ratio.
