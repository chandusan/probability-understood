# Expectation turns a distribution into an average

A probability distribution tells us every possible value of a random variable and how much probability each receives. Its expectation summarizes those possibilities by a probability-weighted average. The word “expected” does not mean that this value must occur on the next trial.

## Begin with an ordinary average

Suppose ten recorded days have sales counts $0,0,1,1,1,1,1,2,2,2$. Their average is

$$
\frac{0+0+1+1+1+1+1+2+2+2}{10}=1.1.
$$

Group the repeated values instead of writing every observation:

$$
\frac{0(2)+1(5)+2(3)}{10}
=0\left(\frac2{10}\right)+1\left(\frac5{10}\right)+2\left(\frac3{10}\right).
$$

Each value is multiplied by its fraction of the observations. Expectation uses the same calculation, replacing observed fractions with model probabilities.

If a model assigns probabilities $0.2,0.5,0.3$ to $0,1,2$, respectively, then

$$
E(X)=0(0.2)+1(0.5)+2(0.3)=1.1.
$$

## What does 1.1 mean?

One day cannot contain $1.1$ sales. But over many comparable days, averages can be close to $1.1$ sales per day. For example, $110$ sales across $100$ days gives exactly that average.

The most likely value here is $1$; the mean is $1.1$. These answer different questions. The most likely value identifies the largest probability, whereas the mean includes the magnitude and probability of every possible value.

## Why learn more than the mean?

A sure payoff of $10$ and a payoff of $0$ or $20$ with equal probabilities both have mean $10$. One has no uncertainty and the other does. We therefore develop variance, standard deviation, and higher moments after learning expectation.

Later we also average functions of a random variable. When a cost grows as $X^2$, the correct average cost uses $E(X^2)$, which generally differs from $[E(X)]^2$.

If $X$ is $0$ with probability $3/4$ and $4$ with probability $1/4$, then $E(X)=1$. This does not say that $X=1$ is possible: it summarizes the weighted average of $0$ and $4$.
