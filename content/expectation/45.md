# Differentiate the binomial MGF to recover mean and variance

For $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$, write $q=1-p$ and $M_X(t)=(pe^t+q)^n$. The first derivative at zero gives $E(X)$. The second derivative at zero gives $E(X^2)$, from which we subtract the squared mean to obtain variance.

## First derivative: identify the outer and inner functions

The outer function raises its input to power $n$. The inner function is $pe^t+q$, whose derivative is $pe^t$. Applying the chain rule,

$$M_X'(t)=n(pe^t+q)^{n-1}pe^t.$$

At $t=0$, $e^0=1$ and $p+q=1$. Thus

$$
\boxed{E(X)=M_X'(0)=n(p+q)^{n-1}p=np.}
$$

This agrees with counting each trial's expected success contribution $p$ across $n$ trials.

## Second derivative: the first derivative is a product

For $n\ge2$, write $M_X'(t)=np e^t(pe^t+q)^{n-1}$. Differentiate both changing factors:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_X''(t)
&=np e^t(pe^t+q)^{n-1}\\
&\quad+n(n-1)p^2e^{2t}(pe^t+q)^{n-2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term comes from differentiating $e^t$. The second comes from differentiating the powered inner expression using the chain rule.

Set $t=0$:

$$E(X^2)=M_X''(0)=np+n(n-1)p^2.$$

## Remove the squared mean

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X)
&=np+n(n-1)p^2-(np)^2\\
&=np+(n^2-n)p^2-n^2p^2\\
&=np-np^2\\
&=\boxed{np(1-p)=npq}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For $n=1$, direct differentiation of $q+pe^t$ gives the same moment and variance formulas. For $n=0$, the count is constantly zero. These cases avoid interpreting irrelevant negative exponents in the intermediate second-derivative expression.

## A numerical check

With ten trials and success probability $0.2$, the mean is $2$, the second raw moment is $2+10(9)(0.04)=5.6$, and variance is $5.6-4=1.6$. Reporting $5.6$ as the variance would confuse the second derivative's raw moment with spread around the mean.
