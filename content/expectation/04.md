# State the outage model before calculating its cost

Let $X$ be the number of server outages in one month. Its possible values are $0,1,2,3,4$ with probabilities $0.15,0.35,0.25,0.15,0.10$. The monthly cost, measured in dollars, is $C(X)=500X^2+1000$. We want the expected number of outages and the expected cost.

## Check what the model says

| Outages $x$ | $p(x)$ | Cost $C(x)$ in dollars |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $0$ | $0.15$ | $1000$ |
| $1$ | $0.35$ | $1500$ |
| $2$ | $0.25$ | $3000$ |
| $3$ | $0.15$ | $5500$ |
| $4$ | $0.10$ | $9000$ |

The probabilities are nonnegative and total $1$. The fixed charge is paid even in a month without an outage. The remaining charge grows with the **square** of the number of outages. This is quadratic growth; the source's description “exponentially scaling” does not match the displayed cost formula.

## Two averages, two quantities to weight

For outages, weight the outage count:

$$
E(X)=\sum_x x p(x)
=0+0.35+0.50+0.45+0.40=\boxed{1.7}.
$$

For cost, weight the cost actually incurred in each possible month:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[C(X)]
&=1000(0.15)+1500(0.35)+3000(0.25)\\
&\quad+5500(0.15)+9000(0.10)\\
&=150+525+750+825+900\\
&=\boxed{3150\text{ dollars}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Both averages use the same probabilities because cost is determined by the outage count. What changes is the value being averaged.

## Why not put the average outage count into the cost formula?

That would produce

$$
C(E(X))=500(1.7)^2+1000=2445.
$$

It treats every month as if it had exactly $1.7$ outages. But actual months fluctuate, and high-outage months receive much larger penalties. Averaging the squared counts accounts for this fluctuation; squaring the average erases it.

The difference here is $3150-2445=705$ dollars. Later, variance will quantify exactly where this extra cost comes from.
