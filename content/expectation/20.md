# Moments summarize a distribution at different powers

The mean is only one summary of a distribution. Moments generalize it by averaging powers: $E(X)$, $E(X^2)$, $E(X^3)$, and so on. Central moments instead take powers of the displacement from the mean, allowing us to study spread and shape without mixing them with the distribution's location.

## Why introduce powers at all?

Consider two distributions: one is always $0$; the other is $-2$ or $2$, each with probability $1/2$. Both have mean $0$. Their second moments differ:

$$E(X^2)=0\quad\text{versus}\quad E(X^2)=4.$$

Squaring prevents the negative and positive values from canceling, so it reveals a difference in spread that the mean misses.

Cubing retains the sign while emphasizing large magnitudes. Fourth powers remove signs again and give even more weight to observations far from the center.

## Distinguish three operations

| Summary | Formula | Reference point |
|---|---|---|
| Raw $k$th moment | $E(X^k)$ | Zero |
| Central $k$th moment | $E[(X-\mu)^k]$ | Mean $\mu=E(X)$ |
| Standardized $k$th central moment | $E[((X-\mu)/\sigma)^k]$ | Mean, with distance measured in standard deviations |

For standardization, we require $0<\sigma<\infty$. If the variable is constant, its standard deviation is zero and division by it is undefined.

## Location and spread should not be confused

If every observation increases by $100$, $E(X^2)$ changes dramatically because it measures squared distance from zero. But the observations' distances from their new mean do not change. Their central moments therefore stay the same.

That is why variance uses $E[(X-\mu)^2]$, not simply $E(X^2)$. Later we will derive the shortcut $\operatorname{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-\mu^2$.

## Higher powers come with a condition

For unbounded distributions, the relevant expectation may fail to be finite. Knowing a finite mean exists does not ensure a finite variance, and a finite variance does not ensure a finite fourth moment. Before interpreting a moment as a number describing the model, confirm that the required average exists.
