# Why the expectation-of-a-function formula works

Let $X$ have PMF $p(x)$, and set $Y=g(X)$. The formula $E[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)p(x)$ lets us average $Y$ directly using the distribution of $X$. It works even when different values of $X$ produce the same value of $Y$.

## Start with a many-to-one example

Suppose $X$ takes values $-2,-1,1,2$ with probabilities $0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4$. For $Y=X^2$, the values $-1$ and $1$ both produce $Y=1$, while $-2$ and $2$ both produce $Y=4$.

Thus

$$P(Y=1)=0.2+0.3=0.5,\qquad P(Y=4)=0.1+0.4=0.5,$$

and $E(Y)=1(0.5)+4(0.5)=2.5$.

Alternatively, preserve the original four cases:

$$
E(X^2)=4(0.1)+1(0.2)+1(0.3)+4(0.4)=2.5.
$$

The two calculations differ only in when they group equal transformed values.

## Write that grouping as a proof

For any possible $y$,

$$P(Y=y)=\sum_{x:g(x)=y}p(x).$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
E(Y)&=\sum_y yP(Y=y)\\
&=\sum_y\sum_{x:g(x)=y}y p(x)\\
&=\sum_x g(x)p(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

In the last line, each original value appears once, carrying the transformed value it creates. For infinite sums, absolute integrability is a sufficient condition for this rearrangement.

## The density version

A narrow interval near $x$ carries probability approximately $f(x)\,dx$ and a transformed value approximately $g(x)$. Adding these contributions gives

$$\boxed{E[g(X)]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)f(x)\,dx.}$$

This is often called the law of the unconscious statistician. The useful content is the rule, not its nickname: **transform the value; retain its probability weight**.

For finite expectation, check $\int |g(x)|f(x)\,dx<\infty$ or its discrete counterpart. A bounded $X$ can still produce an unbounded $g(X)$, as $g(x)=1/x$ near zero illustrates.
