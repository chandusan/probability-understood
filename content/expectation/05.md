# Calculate and interpret the mean outage count

The monthly outage count $X$ takes values $0,1,2,3,4$ with probabilities $0.15,0.35,0.25,0.15,0.10$. To find $E(X)$, multiply each outage count by its probability and add. Each product describes that category's contribution to the overall average.

## Show every contribution

| $x$ | $p(x)$ | $x p(x)$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $0$ | $0.15$ | $0$ |
| $1$ | $0.35$ | $0.35$ |
| $2$ | $0.25$ | $0.50$ |
| $3$ | $0.15$ | $0.45$ |
| $4$ | $0.10$ | $0.40$ |

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
E(X)&=0(0.15)+1(0.35)+2(0.25)+3(0.15)+4(0.10)\\
&=0+0.35+0.50+0.45+0.40\\
&=\boxed{1.70\text{ outages per month}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Four-outage months are uncommon, but their contribution to the mean is $0.4$, larger than the contribution $0.35$ from one-outage months. Expectation depends on both how often something happens and how large its value is.

## A concrete interpretation through 100 months

Imagine 100 months with exactly the model's proportions: 15 have no outages, 35 have one, 25 have two, 15 have three, and 10 have four. The total would be

$$
15(0)+35(1)+25(2)+15(3)+10(4)=170
$$

outages, yielding $170/100=1.7$ per month. These are illustrative counts, not a guarantee about the next 100 months. Real proportions fluctuate around the probabilities.

## Check the result before trusting it

The mean lies between the minimum $0$ and maximum $4$, as a weighted average must. It is greater than the most likely value $1$ because larger counts pull it upward. It need not be an integer, even though each month's count is an integer.

Do not average the five possible values equally to obtain $2$: that would silently replace the given probabilities with $1/5$ each.

<details markdown="1"><summary>If four-outage months become more common, what happens to the mean?</summary>

Moving probability $0.05$ from $X=0$ to $X=4$ raises the mean by $(4-0)(0.05)=0.2$. The new mean is $1.9$. This directly uses the weighted-sum definition.

</details>
