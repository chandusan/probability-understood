# Why the expectation of a constant is the constant

If a random variable always equals a fixed number $c$, its expectation is $c$. Every possible outcome contributes the same value, so averaging cannot change it. The formal proof uses just one probability fact: all weights sum, or integrate, to one.

## Begin with a finite weighted average

Suppose possible situations have probabilities $p_1,\ldots,p_m$, but each situation gives the same value $c$. Then

$$cp_1+cp_2+\cdots+cp_m=c(p_1+\cdots+p_m)=c.$$

The value is paid once in whichever situation occurs. We do not add $c$ once per listed possibility without probability weights.

## Discrete proof

For any discrete $X$ with PMF $p(x)$, the function $g(x)=c$ is constant. Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[c]&=\sum_xc p(x)\\
&=c\sum_xp(x)\\
&=c\cdot1=\boxed{c}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The index $x$ only enumerates possible situations; the value $c$ is unchanged across them.

## Continuous proof

If $X$ has density $f(x)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[c]&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}c f(x)\,dx\\
&=c\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx\\
&=c\cdot1=c.
\end{aligned}
$$

This uses the expectation-of-a-function formula. It does not claim that the constant random variable itself has an ordinary continuous density; it does not. We are averaging a constant function against the distribution of $X$.

## Why this small rule matters

A fixed monthly fee of $1000$ contributes exactly $1000$ to expected monthly cost, whatever random usage occurs. If $\mu=E(X)$, then $\mu$ is also a fixed number, so $E(\mu)=\mu$ and $E(\mu^2)=\mu^2$.

These observations justify steps in later variance proofs. Treating the mean as if it remained a random variable after it has been calculated would create unnecessary extra expectations.

<details markdown="1"><summary>What is the variance of a constant?</summary>

Its mean is $c$, so every deviation is $c-c=0$. Hence $\operatorname{Var}(c)=E(0^2)=0$. A fixed value has no spread.

</details>
