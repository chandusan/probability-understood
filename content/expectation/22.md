# Central moments measure deviations from the mean

Let $\mu=E(X)$. The $k$th central moment is $\mu_k^0=E[(X-\mu)^k]$, when finite. The superscript zero here labels the source's central-moment notation; it is not an instruction to raise the moment to the zeroth power.

## First move the center to zero

For each possible observation, form its signed deviation $X-\mu$. Values below the mean become negative, and values above it become positive. Then raise that deviation to the required power and average using the original probabilities:

$$
\mu_k^0=\sum_x(x-\mu)^kp(x)
$$

in the discrete case, or

$$
\mu_k^0=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x-\mu)^kf(x)\,dx
$$

when a density exists. The whole difference is raised to the power; $(x-\mu)^2$ does not equal $x^2-\mu^2$.

## A complete two-point example

Let $P(X=0)=3/4$ and $P(X=4)=1/4$. Then $\mu=1$, so the deviations are $-1$ and $3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_1^0&=(-1)\frac34+3\frac14=0,\\
\mu_2^0&=1\frac34+9\frac14=3,\\
\mu_3^0&=(-1)\frac34+27\frac14=6,\\
\mu_4^0&=1\frac34+81\frac14=21.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first central moment vanishes because the mean balances deviations. The second cannot cancel negative with positive deviations, so it measures spread. The third allows signed contributions, revealing an imbalance in large deviations. The fourth heavily weights deviations far from the mean in either direction.

## Translation does not alter central moments

If $Y=X+c$, its mean is $\mu+c$. Therefore

$$Y-E(Y)=(X+c)-(\mu+c)=X-\mu.$$

Every central moment is unchanged. A constant shift moves the distribution without changing its shape or spread.

In the mass analogy, a probability distribution has total mass one, which makes the first raw moment equal the center of mass. For an unnormalized physical mass distribution, one must divide its first moment by its total mass to obtain the center of mass.
