# Define raw moments and keep them separate from powers of the mean

For a positive integer $k$, the $k$th raw moment is $\mu_k=E(X^k)$, when finite. It averages the $k$th powers of possible values. The first moment $\mu_1=E(X)$ is the mean; higher raw moments contain additional information but depend on the position of zero.

## The formulas follow the function rule

Choose $g(x)=x^k$ in the expectation-of-a-function formula:

$$
\boxed{\mu_k=\begin{cases}
\sum_xx^kp(x),&X\text{ discrete},\\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^kf(x)\,dx,&X\text{ has density }f.
\end{cases}}
$$

The zeroth moment, interpreted as the expectation of the constant function $1$, is $E(1)=1$. Probability distributions are normalized to total mass one.

## Work a small example

If $X$ equals $1$ or $3$ with probabilities $1/2$ each, then

$$
\mu_1=\frac{1+3}{2}=2,\qquad
\mu_2=\frac{1+9}{2}=5,\qquad
\mu_3=\frac{1+27}{2}=14.
$$

By contrast, $\mu_1^2=4$ and $\mu_1^3=8$. A subscript and an exponent mean different things:

- $\mu_3$ is the third moment, $E(X^3)$.
- $\mu_1^3$ is the cube of the first moment, $[E(X)]^3$.

## Why the origin matters

For this example, the mean is $2$. Recenter to $Y=X-2$, whose values are $-1$ and $1$. Then $E(Y^2)=1$ and $E(Y^3)=0$.

The same two equally likely positions are still two units apart, but their raw moments have changed because we moved the origin. Central moments avoid this dependence by measuring every value relative to the distribution's own mean.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Does knowing the first three moments reveal every probability?</summary>

Not for an arbitrary distribution. A few moments give a few numerical constraints, and different distributions can satisfy the same constraints. Within a specified finite family, enough independent conditions may determine unknown parameters, as in the density $a+bx^2$ example.

</details>
