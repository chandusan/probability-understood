# Why a shift leaves variance unchanged and a scale is squared

For $Y=aX+b$, the variance is $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$. Adding the same constant to every observation shifts its mean by the same amount, leaving deviations unchanged. Scaling multiplies deviations by $a$, so squared deviations acquire the factor $a^2$.

## Find the new mean before measuring deviations

Let $\mu=E(X)$. By linearity,

$$E(Y)=E(aX+b)=a\mu+b.$$

The deviation of $Y$ from its own mean is

$$
Y-E(Y)=(aX+b)-(a\mu+b)=a(X-\mu).
$$

Both copies of $b$ cancel. This cancellation is why a fixed shift contributes nothing to spread.

## Square and average

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(Y)
&=E[(Y-E(Y))^2]\\
&=E[a^2(X-\mu)^2]\\
&=a^2E[(X-\mu)^2]\\
&=\boxed{a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking square roots gives

$$\boxed{\sigma_Y=|a|\sigma_X.}$$

The absolute value matters. Multiplying by a negative number reflects values, but a measure of distance remains nonnegative.

## Test the rule on simple changes

If a waiting time has variance $4$ minutes squared and standard deviation $2$ minutes:

- Adding a fixed five-minute delay leaves variance $4$ and standard deviation $2$.
- Doubling every waiting time gives variance $16$ and standard deviation $4$.
- Measuring in seconds multiplies variance by $60^2$ and standard deviation by $60$.

A fixed delay raises the average wait without making the variability larger. A random additional delay would be a different problem because its own fluctuation can contribute to spread.

## Apply the rule to a transformed variable carefully

For $Y=2/X+3$, the rule gives

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y)=4\operatorname{Var}(1/X).$$

It does **not** give $4/\operatorname{Var}(X)$. Treat $U=1/X$ as the random variable being scaled, then calculate its moments. This is exactly the structure of the next exercise.
