# Cubed deviations reveal an imbalance around the mean

The third central moment is $\mu_3^0=E[(X-\mu)^3]$. Cubing preserves the sign of deviations while giving large deviations greater influence. It therefore records whether the weighted contribution of large positive deviations outweighs that of large negative deviations, or vice versa.

## Why use an odd power?

If $d$ is positive, then $(-d)^3=-d^3$. Equally likely deviations at $-d$ and $d$ cancel after cubing. But a positive deviation of size $3$ contributes $27$, compared with only $1$ for a positive deviation of size $1$.

Consequently, probability far from the mean can have a strong effect on the third central moment, even when that probability is small.

## Work a distribution with a rare large value

Suppose $P(X=0)=3/4$ and $P(X=4)=1/4$. Its mean is $\mu=1$. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_3^0
&=(0-1)^3\frac34+(4-1)^3\frac14\\
&=-\frac34+\frac{27}{4}=\boxed{6}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Most observations are below the mean, but the fewer observations above it are much farther away. Their cubed deviations dominate, making the result positive.

## What the sign tells us

Positive third central moment is associated with right skew in the moment-based sense; negative third central moment indicates the opposite imbalance. For a symmetric distribution, paired deviations cancel and the third central moment is zero, provided the moment exists.

The converse does not follow: a zero third central moment can result from cancellation in an asymmetric distribution. A single summary cannot establish every aspect of a distribution's shape.

## Separate shape from measurement units

If $X$ is measured in seconds, $\mu_3^0$ has units of seconds cubed. Changing seconds to milliseconds multiplies it by $1000^3$. The shape has not changed, yet the number has. Dividing by $\sigma^3$ produces the dimensionless skewness coefficient introduced next.

In modeling, skewness can motivate examining transformations or residual behavior, but it does not by itself dictate a transformation. Ordinary linear regression does not require every input feature to be normally distributed; model assumptions must be checked for the quantities to which they actually apply.
