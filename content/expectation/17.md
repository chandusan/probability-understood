# Compute expected squared outages and the cost of variability

For the monthly outage distribution at $0,1,2,3,4$ with probabilities $0.15,0.35,0.25,0.15,0.10$, we know $E(X)=1.7$. The cost is $C(X)=500X^2+1000$, so we need $E(X^2)$. This means average the squared counts, not square the average count.

## Form the second moment directly

$$
\begin{aligned}
E(X^2)
&=0^2(0.15)+1^2(0.35)+2^2(0.25)\\
&\quad+3^2(0.15)+4^2(0.10)\\
&=0+0.35+1.00+1.35+1.60\\
&=\boxed{4.30}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Every possible count is squared before it is multiplied by its original probability. Thus the $X=4$ contribution is $16(0.10)=1.60$, not $[4(0.10)]^2$.

## Convert the result into dollars

Distributing the expectation over the fixed coefficients gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[C(X)]&=500E(X^2)+1000\\
&=500(4.30)+1000\\
&=\boxed{3150\text{ dollars}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The direct cost table and this shorter calculation are algebraically identical.

## Quantify exactly why using the mean underestimates cost

Variance, introduced in detail shortly, satisfies

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2.$$

Here it is $4.30-1.70^2=1.41$. Rearrange that identity inside the cost expression:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[C(X)]
&=500\big([E(X)]^2+\operatorname{Var}(X)\big)+1000\\
&=C(E(X))+500\operatorname{Var}(X)\\
&=2445+500(1.41)=3150.
\end{aligned}
$$

The extra $705$ dollars arises exactly from variability under this quadratic penalty. If the count were constant, its variance would be zero and the two calculations would agree. For this model, reducing variability while preserving the same mean count would reduce expected cost.

This conclusion uses the particular squared cost function. A different nonlinear cost requires its own calculation; “just plug in the mean” is not a general expectation rule.
