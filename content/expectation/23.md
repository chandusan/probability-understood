# Why variance averages squared deviations

The first central moment is always zero when the mean exists: positive and negative deviations cancel. To measure spread, we need a quantity that does not disappear through this cancellation. Variance uses the square of each deviation and then takes its probability-weighted average.

## Show the cancellation that motivates the square

Since $\mu=E(X)$ is a fixed number,

$$E(X-\mu)=E(X)-\mu=0.$$

This is true whether observations lie very close to the mean or very far from it. For instance, equally likely deviations $-1,+1$ average to zero, and so do $-100,+100$.

Squaring distinguishes them: their average squared deviations are $1$ and $10000$, respectively.

## Define variance as a full operation

$$\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2].}$$

Read it in order: find the mean, subtract it from each possible value, square each difference, and average those squared differences with the probabilities.

For the outage example, $\mu=1.7$. Directly,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X)
&=(0-1.7)^2(0.15)+(1-1.7)^2(0.35)\\
&\quad+(2-1.7)^2(0.25)+(3-1.7)^2(0.15)\\
&\quad+(4-1.7)^2(0.10)\\
&=0.4335+0.1715+0.0225+0.2535+0.529\\
&=\boxed{1.41}.
\end{aligned}
$$

## What variance can and cannot be

Every squared deviation is nonnegative, so variance cannot be negative. It equals zero exactly when $X=\mu$ with probability one. Larger variance means a larger average squared distance from the mean, not that every individual outcome is farther away.

Variance has squared units. If $X$ is measured in minutes, its variance is in squared minutes. Standard deviation takes the square root to return to the original units.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Why don't we divide by $n-1$ here?</summary>

We are calculating variance from the full probability model, not estimating an unknown population variance from a sample. The probabilities already specify the correct weights. The $n-1$ adjustment belongs to a particular sample estimator, not to the definition of a random variable's variance.
