# Derive the uniform distribution's central moments

For $X$ uniform on $[0,1]$, the mean is $1/2$. Its $k$th central moment is the average of $(X-1/2)^k$. Shifting the integration variable to $w=x-1/2$ centers the interval at zero, where the distinction between odd and even powers becomes transparent.

## State the complete integral before substituting

$$\mu_k^0=E[(X-1/2)^k]=\int_0^1(x-1/2)^k\,dx.$$

Set $w=x-1/2$, so $dw=dx$. Transform both limits:

- At $x=0$, $w=-1/2$.
- At $x=1$, $w=1/2$.

Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_k^0
&=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}w^k\,dw\\
&=\left[\frac{w^{k+1}}{k+1}\right]_{-1/2}^{1/2}\\
&=\frac{(1/2)^{k+1}-(-1/2)^{k+1}}{k+1}\\
&=\frac{1-(-1)^{k+1}}{2^{k+1}(k+1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

## Separate odd and even orders

If $k$ is odd, $k+1$ is even, so $(-1)^{k+1}=1$. The numerator is zero.

If $k$ is even, $k+1$ is odd, so $(-1)^{k+1}=-1$. The numerator is two. Therefore

$$
\boxed{\mu_k^0=\begin{cases}
0,&k\text{ odd},\\
\dfrac1{2^k(k+1)},&k\text{ even}.
\end{cases}}
$$

## Understand the geometry behind the algebra

For odd $k$, the function $w^k$ has equal positive and negative signed areas over the symmetric interval. For even $k$, both sides are positive and equal, so the total is twice the area on $[0,1/2]$.

This is symmetry applied to actual weighted contributions: each interval around $w$ has a matching interval around $-w$ with the same density.

Substituting $k=2,3,4$ gives

$$\mu_2^0=\frac1{12},\qquad \mu_3^0=0,\qquad \mu_4^0=\frac1{80}.$$

These provide variance, skewness, and kurtosis after the appropriate normalization. The fourth central moment $1/80$ is distinct from the fourth raw moment $1/5$ because the powers are measured from different origins.
