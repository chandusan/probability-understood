# One changing observation, two centers

Changing the largest observation from 9 to 12 increases the total but leaves the middle position alone. The mean therefore moves from 6 to 7, while the median stays at 6. This small example separates the two jobs clearly.

## Watch what changes—and what does not

This example isolates the effect of making one large observation even larger.

| Dataset | Mean | Median |
|---|---:|---:|
| $6,3,9$ | $6$ | $6$ |
| $6,3,12$ | $7$ | $6$ |

The total rises by $3$, so the mean rises by $3/3=1$. The middle position does not change, so the median remains $6$.

Return to the café interpretation: if the last customer waits longer, the total customer-minutes increase. The mean registers that extra total. But the middle customer's wait has not changed, so the median does not change.

If the largest wait becomes $M\ge6$, then

$$
\text{mean}=\frac{3+6+M}{3},
\qquad
\text{median}=6.
$$

This makes the outlier contrast visible without needing a large dataset.

The two losses also explain the choice:

- With squared errors, large misses receive stronger penalties, so the optimal constant moves toward the increased observation.
- With absolute errors, the count of observations on either side matters; the increased largest value remains on the same side of $6$.

## Read the arithmetic as a story

The original sum is $18$, so equal sharing gives $6$. The new sum is $21$, so equal sharing gives $7$. Sorting supplies a different piece of information: the second value is $6$ in both lists. Keeping these two operations separate is more reliable than trying to memorize a rule that “outliers change one average but not the other.”

## Important distinctions

The median remaining unchanged is a property of **this particular change**. It is not a promise that any single modification will leave any median unchanged. Robustness limits extreme influence; it does not prohibit all movement.

## Check your understanding

Start with $3,6,12$, but this time replace the smallest value 3 by 30. Does the median still stay at 6?

No. The new sorted data are $6,12,30$, so the median becomes $12$. The mean becomes $(6+12+30)/3=16$. Median robustness does not mean that one changed observation can never move it. In the original demonstration, the changed largest value stayed on the same side of the middle; this change crosses that middle.
