# See what changed before calculating SD

Ask three questions: did the center move, did distances stretch, and did the sample size change? These structural questions explain the four comparisons in the slide more clearly than memorizing separate answers.

## The four choices

The slide asks for comparisons **without calculations** using these statements:

| Statement | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **I** | Column A is greater. |
| **II** | Column B is greater. |
| **III** | The two quantities are equal. |
| **IV** | The relationship cannot be determined from the information given. |

Each entry below asks for the dataset's standard deviation:

| Row | Column A | Column B |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\{0.2,0.4,0.6,0.8\}$ | $\{2,4,6,8\}$ |
| 2 | $\{1,3,5,7,9\}$ | $\{3,5,7,9,1\}$ |
| 3 | $\{1,3,5,7,9\}$ | $\{1,3,5,7,9,9\}$ |
| 4 | $\{1,3,5,7,9\}$ | $\{1,3,5,5,7,9\}$ |

Try a prediction first. The answers are **II, III, II, I**. The calculations below use sample variance; using population variance consistently would give the same four comparisons here.

## 1. Multiply every distance by ten

Column B is column A multiplied by 10. Its SD is therefore ten times as large:

$$
s_B=10s_A.
$$

**Answer: II.** Variance is multiplied by $10^2=100$, while SD is multiplied by 10.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Check the exact numbers</summary>

Column A has mean $0.5$ and squared-deviation total

$$
SS_A=(-0.3)^2+(-0.1)^2+(0.1)^2+(0.3)^2=0.2=\frac15.
$$

Therefore

$$
s_A^2=\frac1{15},
\qquad s_A=\frac1{\sqrt{15}}\approx0.2582.
$$

Column B has mean 5 and squared-deviation total $9+1+1+9=20$, giving

$$
s_B^2=\frac{20}{3}=100s_A^2,
\qquad s_B=\sqrt{\frac{20}{3}}\approx2.5820=10s_A.
$$

</details>

## 2. Change the order, keep the observations

The columns contain the same five values in different orders. Their means and squared-deviation totals are unchanged.

**Answer: III.** Order is irrelevant to this collection's SD. Order could matter for a time-series question, but that is a different question.

For either column,

$$
\bar x=5,\qquad
SS=(-4)^2+(-2)^2+0^2+2^2+4^2=40,
$$

so

$$
s^2=\frac{40}{4}=10,
\qquad s=\sqrt{10}\approx3.1623.
$$

## 3. Add another upper-end observation

Column B adds another 9. Its mean moves upward, and more weight sits at the upper end. For these specific values, the added spread outweighs the larger variance divisor.

**Answer: II.** The new sample variance is $32/3\approx10.6667$, compared with the original 10.

Do not turn this into “adding a maximum always increases SD.” The center, squared-deviation total, and divisor all change. The exact comparison verifies the answer for this dataset.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Calculate the shifted mean and new variance</summary>

The new mean is

$$
\bar x_B=\frac{1+3+5+7+9+9}{6}=\frac{17}{3}.
$$

The squared-deviation total is

$$
\begin{aligned}
SS_B
&=\left(-\frac{14}{3}\right)^2
+\left(-\frac8{3}\right)^2
+\left(-\frac2{3}\right)^2
+\left(\frac4{3}\right)^2
+\left(\frac{10}{3}\right)^2
+\left(\frac{10}{3}\right)^2\\
&=\frac{196+64+4+16+100+100}{9}
=\frac{160}{3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
s_B^2=\frac{160/3}{5}=\frac{32}{3}>10,
\qquad
s_B=\sqrt{\frac{32}{3}}\approx3.2660>\sqrt{10}\approx3.1623.
$$

Square root preserves the order of nonnegative numbers, so comparing variances is enough to compare SDs.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>A shortcut for appending one observation</summary>

Appending a value $y$ to a sample of size $n$ gives

$$
SS_{\mathrm{new}}
=SS_{\mathrm{old}}+\frac{n}{n+1}(y-\bar x_{\mathrm{old}})^2.
$$

To understand the correction, the new mean shifts by $(y-\bar x_{\mathrm{old}})/(n+1)$. Re-centering the old observations adds $n$ times this squared shift; the new observation's squared residual adds the remaining contribution. Combining them gives the displayed formula.

Here,

$$
SS_{\mathrm{new}}=40+\frac56(9-5)^2=\frac{160}{3}.
$$

Then divide by the new sample divisor 5. The squared-deviation total grows enough to outweigh that divisor's increase from 4 to 5.

</details>

## 4. Add an observation exactly at the mean

Column B adds another 5. The mean stays at 5, the new squared deviation is zero, and the existing squared deviations do not change. The total stays 40 while the divisor rises from 4 to 5.

**Answer: I.**

$$
s_A^2=\frac{40}{4}=10,
\qquad s_B^2=\frac{40}{5}=8.
$$

Thus $s_A=\sqrt{10}\approx3.1623$ exceeds $s_B=\sqrt8\approx2.8284$. The same total squared spread has a larger divisor.

## Keep the patterns together

| Row | Change | Sample variance in A | Sample variance in B | Answer |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | Scale every value by 10 | $1/15$ | $20/3$ | **II** |
| 2 | Reorder the same values | 10 | 10 | **III** |
| 3 | Add another 9 | 10 | $32/3$ | **II** |
| 4 | Add another 5 | 10 | 8 | **I** |

## Check your understanding

You append the old mean to any sample with $n\ge2$ and positive sample variance. Does the new sample mean change? Does the sample variance rise or fall?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

The mean stays unchanged. The appended observation has zero deviation, so the squared-deviation total also stays unchanged. The sample-variance divisor grows from $n-1$ to $n$, giving

$$
s_{\mathrm{new}}^2=\frac{n-1}{n}s_{\mathrm{old}}^2.
$$

It decreases when the original variance is positive. If the original variance is zero, it remains zero.

</details>
