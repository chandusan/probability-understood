# The mean is the closest constant point

The whole dataset can be represented as one point. Every prediction that gives all observations the same value lies on a particular line. The mean appears where that line comes closest to the data point under ordinary straight-line distance.

## The crucial change of viewpoint

We usually picture observations as separate dots on a number line. The diagram uses a different construction:

> **A dataset with $n$ observations becomes one point with $n$ coordinates.**

For data $2,8$, the whole dataset becomes the single point

$$
\mathbf{x}=(2,8).
$$

The horizontal coordinate records the first observation. The vertical coordinate records the second observation. We are not plotting a relationship between two measured variables here; we are packaging the two entries of one dataset into a geometric object.

For three observations, the dataset is one point in three-dimensional space. For $n$ observations, it is a point in $n$-dimensional space.

## 1. What does one constant prediction look like?

If we use the same prediction $c$ for both observations, its geometric point is

$$
\mathbf{q}(c)=(c,c).
$$

Examples are $(0,0)$, $(3,3)$, $(5,5)$, and $(8,8)$. All of these points lie on the diagonal line $y=x$.

That diagonal is the **constant line**. Its defining feature is not a particular slope in a drawing; it is that **all coordinates are equal**.

## 2. Connect geometric distance to prediction error

The horizontal difference between $(2,8)$ and $(c,c)$ is $2-c$. The vertical difference is $8-c$.

Pythagoras gives the squared distance:

$$
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}(c)\|_2^2
=(2-c)^2+(8-c)^2.
$$

That is exactly the sum of squared prediction errors. Therefore:

> Finding the best constant under squared error is the same problem as finding the nearest point on the diagonal.

## 3. Find the nearest point in the two-observation example

The mean of $2$ and $8$ is $5$, so the nearest constant point is

$$
(5,5).
$$

The error vector is

$$
(2,8)-(5,5)=(-3,3).
$$

The squared distance is

$$
(-3)^2+3^2=18.
$$

For every other candidate:

$$
(2-c)^2+(8-c)^2=18+2(c-5)^2.
$$

The second term measures the additional cost of choosing a constant other than $5$.

For example, choosing $c=4$ gives a squared distance of $4+16=20$, larger than $18$.

## 4. Why is the shortest segment perpendicular?

Let $P=(5,5)$ be the perpendicular foot from the data point to the diagonal. Any other point $Q$ on the diagonal forms a right triangle with $P$ and the data point.

Its squared distance satisfies

$$
\|\mathbf{x}-Q\|_2^2
=\|\mathbf{x}-P\|_2^2+\|P-Q\|_2^2.
$$

Moving away from $P$ adds a nonnegative squared length. That makes $P$ the closest point.

The diagonal has direction $(1,1)$. Its dot product with the residual is

$$
(-3,3)\cdot(1,1)=-3+3=0.
$$

A zero dot product means the directions are perpendicular.

## 5. Carry the same idea into three dimensions

For observations $3,6,12$, the data point is

$$
\mathbf{x}=(3,6,12).
$$

A constant prediction becomes

$$
\mathbf{q}(c)=(c,c,c).
$$

All such points lie on the line through the origin in direction

$$
\mathbf{1}_3=(1,1,1).
$$

The mean is $7$, so its closest point is

$$
P_L(\mathbf{x})=(7,7,7).
$$

Its residual is

$$
(3,6,12)-(7,7,7)=(-4,-1,5).
$$

Again,

$$
(-4,-1,5)\cdot(1,1,1)=-4-1+5=0.
$$

The residual is perpendicular to the constant line. The geometry is the same as the two-dimensional example; only the number of coordinates has changed.

### Step 6: derive the mean directly from perpendicularity

For general $n$, a point on the constant line has the form $c\mathbf{1}_n$. At the Euclidean projection, the residual is perpendicular to the line:

$$
(\mathbf{x}-c\mathbf{1}_n)\cdot\mathbf{1}_n=0.
$$

Expanding:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i-cn=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
c=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i=\bar{x}.
$$

This is the arithmetic-mean formula obtained from a geometric condition.

### Step 7: understand the general projection formula

For any nonzero direction vector $\mathbf{u}$, the Euclidean projection onto its span is

$$
P_{\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{u})}(\mathbf{x})
=\frac{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{u}.
$$

Here $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{1}_n$, so

$$
\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{1}_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i,
\qquad
\mathbf{1}_n\cdot\mathbf{1}_n=n.
$$

The coefficient multiplying $\mathbf{1}_n$ is therefore $\bar{x}$.

**The scalar mean and the projected point are not the same type of object:** for the two-observation example, the mean is the number $5$, while the projected point is $(5,5)$.

## Important distinctions

- The constant line contains every real $c$, including negative values, even if the illustration shows only part of it.
- The direction $\mathbf{1}_n$ is not a unit-length vector: its Euclidean length is $\sqrt{n}$. That is why the projection denominator is $n$.
- This denominator $n$ comes from $\mathbf{1}_n\cdot\mathbf{1}_n$. It is separate from the later $n-1$ in sample variance.
- The drawn segment's actual Euclidean length is $\|\mathbf{x}-c\mathbf{1}_n\|_2$. The slide labels its squared error, $\|\mathbf{x}-c\mathbf{1}_n\|_2^2$. Both are minimized at the same point.
- Orthogonality is part of the **Euclidean** projection result. When we change the distance rule to $L_1$, the best point need not be reached by a perpendicular center-to-point segment.

A constant prediction must use the same value in both coordinates, so it must have the form $(c,c)$. The coordinates of $(4,6)$ differ, so it is outside the allowed line. The best allowed point is $(5,5)$. Its residual $(-3,3)$ is perpendicular to the line direction $(1,1)$, and its squared length is $18$. The mean is the scalar $5$, while the fitted point is the vector $(5,5)$.
