# The median finds the middle

The median is a statement about order: sort the observations and find the middle. A very distant value matters because of where it sits in that order, rather than receiving unlimited influence because of its size.

## Sort first, then locate the middle

Write the ordered observations as $x_{(1)}\le\cdots\le x_{(n)}$. The usual sample median is

$$
\widetilde{x}=\begin{cases}
x_{((n+1)/2)},&n\text{ odd},\\[4pt]
\dfrac{x_{(n/2)}+x_{(n/2+1)}}2,&n\text{ even}.
\end{cases}
$$

A median is also called a 50th percentile or 0.5-quantile. It is often written $\operatorname{Med}(X)$.

## Order comes before arithmetic

The subscript in parentheses indicates **position after sorting**, not the observation's original label.

For the original sequence $6,3,9$, the sorted sequence is $3,6,9$. Its middle observation is $6$.

For an odd number of observations, the median is the single middle entry. For example:

$$
1,3,5,7,9 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \widetilde{x}=5.
$$

For an even number, there are two middle entries. Conventionally, we average them:

$$
2,4,8,10 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \widetilde{x}=\frac{4+8}{2}=6.
$$

The reported median need not be an observed value. In this example, no observation equals $6$.

## What does “middle” mean when values repeat?

A median has at least half the observations at or below it and at least half at or above it. These groups may overlap because observations can equal the median.

For $2,2,2,8,9$, the median is $2$. It is not true that exactly half the observations lie strictly below $2$ and exactly half strictly above it.

## Definition versus optimization

The usual even-sample formula picks the midpoint of the two middle observations. However, as we will see next, **every point between the two middle observations minimizes total absolute error**.

For $2,4,8,10$, any $c\in[4,8]$ minimizes that loss. The standard sample median $6$ is one particular choice from this entire optimal interval.

## Important distinctions

- The formula assumes a nonempty numerical sample.
- “The 50th percentile” needs a convention when calculating empirical quantiles. The usual median formula is clear, but some generic percentile algorithms use different interpolation rules; we will revisit this in the percentile slides.
- For a general population distribution, medians need not be unique. The sample formula above is a conventional way to choose one representative median.

For $1,2,10,20$, find the usual median. Is it the only constant minimizing total absolute error?

The usual median is $(2+10)/2=6$. It is not the only absolute-error minimizer: every $c$ in $[2,10]$ is optimal. The outer pair contributes at least $20-1=19$, and the inner pair contributes at least $10-2=8$. Any point in $[2,10]$ attains both bounds, giving the same minimum total of $27$. The midpoint convention selects $6$ from that interval.
