# Why one number is not enough

A useful summary keeps the part of the data that matters to your question. A center tells you where observations gather; a measure of spread tells you how different their individual experiences can be. You usually need both.

## Start with the question you care about

Imagine choosing a café before class. It advertises an average waiting time of six minutes. That could mean almost everyone waits about six minutes. It could also mean some people are served immediately while others wait a long time. One number cannot tell you both the center and the variation around it.

A statistical summary is a deliberate compression. We trade a long list of observations for a smaller description. The useful question is: **what should that smaller description preserve?**

## Two questions guide the whole chapter

1. **Where is the data centered?** The mean and median answer this in different ways.
2. **How much do observations differ?** The range, interquartile range, variance, and standard deviation describe different aspects of that spread.

Neither question replaces the other. If two cafés have the same average, you may still prefer the one with more predictable waits. If two classes have the same spread of scores, you may still want to know which class scored higher overall.

## Three pictures to keep in mind

- **A balancing ruler:** the mean preserves the total and responds to every observation's distance.
- **The middle person in a queue:** the median depends on ordered position.
- **The width of the crowd:** measures of spread describe how much individual experiences differ from a central summary.

We will begin with small datasets so you can check every step by hand. Then we will connect the arithmetic to geometry and to the questions these summaries answer. The goal is to know why a formula is useful, when its assumptions matter, and what information it leaves out.
