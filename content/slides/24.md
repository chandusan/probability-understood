# Give features a common ruler, then find their main direction

Scaling and PCA solve different problems. **Scaling changes the ruler used for each feature. PCA changes the directions in which we describe the data.** Variance connects them, but a large variance is not automatically useful predictive information.

## Scaling: compare differences in standard-deviation units

Suppose age has mean 30 years and SD 10 years, while annual income has mean 50,000 rupees and SD 10,000 rupees. A person aged 40 earning 60,000 rupees is equally far above the two means when measured using each feature's own spread:

$$
z_{\text{age}}=\frac{40-30}{10}=1,
\qquad
z_{\text{income}}=\frac{60000-50000}{10000}=1.
$$

Each value is **one standard deviation above its mean**. Standardization converts years and rupees to the same dimensionless language: “how many SDs from the center?”

Without scaling, the raw differences are 10 and 10,000. In a Euclidean squared-distance calculation their contributions are $10^2=100$ and $10000^2=100{,}000{,}000$. Income dominates because of its numerical scale, not because we established that it matters more.

For sample observations, the rule is

$$
z_i=\frac{x_i-\bar x}{s}.
$$

For the same sample used to calculate $\bar x$ and $s$, standardized values have sample mean 0 and sample SD 1, provided $n\ge2$ and $s>0$. A constant feature has $s=0$, so this formula is undefined.

Standardization can help distance-based methods, gradient optimization, and scale-sensitive penalties. It is not mandatory for every algorithm, nor is min–max scaling the only alternative. The choice depends on what the features mean and how the method uses them.

## PCA: turn a two-coordinate story into one score

Consider four students with scores in two subjects:

| Student | Math | Physics |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 40 | 40 |
| B | 55 | 65 |
| C | 65 | 55 |
| D | 80 | 80 |

Both subject means are 60. Centering therefore gives the points

$$
(-20,-20),\quad(-5,5),\quad(5,-5),\quad(20,20).
$$

The main pattern is that students who score higher in one subject generally score higher in the other. A line at 45° runs along that pattern. A perpendicular line at 135° describes the smaller differences between the two subjects.

**Projection is a shadow.** Drop each point perpendicularly onto a candidate line and keep its signed coordinate along that line. You now have one number per student instead of two. PCA chooses the first direction whose projected coordinates retain the most variance.

## Measure how much spread each direction keeps

The total squared distance from the center is

$$
\underbrace{850}_{\text{Math}}+
\underbrace{850}_{\text{Physics}}=1700.
$$

The two perpendicular unit directions are

$$
u_{45}=\frac{(1,1)}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
u_{135}=\frac{(-1,1)}{\sqrt2}.
$$

Dotting a centered point with each unit direction gives its projected score:

| Student | Centered point | 45° score | Squared 45° score | 135° score | Squared 135° score |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | $(-20,-20)$ | $-20\sqrt2$ | 800 | 0 | 0 |
| B | $(-5,5)$ | 0 | 0 | $5\sqrt2$ | 50 |
| C | $(5,-5)$ | 0 | 0 | $-5\sqrt2$ | 50 |
| D | $(20,20)$ | $20\sqrt2$ | 800 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total** | — | — | **1600** | — | **100** |

Thus the first direction keeps

$$
\frac{1600}{1700}=\frac{16}{17}\approx94.1\%
$$

of the total squared spread. Its perpendicular direction keeps

$$
\frac{100}{1700}=\frac1{17}\approx5.9\%.
$$

These shares total 100%. The 45° direction preserves much more of this cloud's variation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Why dividing variances gives the same percentages</summary>

Each original subject has sample variance $850/(4-1)=850/3$. Their sum is $1700/3$. The first projected coordinate has sample variance $1600/3$; the second has $100/3$.

The fraction explained by the first component is therefore

$$
\frac{\text{variance of first projected coordinate}}
{\text{sum of original feature variances}}
=\frac{1600/3}{850/3+850/3}
=\frac{1600}{1700}.
$$

The common divisor cancels. If we describe these four points using population divisors, the numerator and denominator both divide by 4 instead, and the ratio stays the same.

The denominator is the **sum** of original coordinate variances, not the variance of either subject alone. A projected coordinate can have more variance than either original coordinate because it combines their shared variation.

For this symmetric example, the sample covariance matrix is

$$
\frac13
\begin{pmatrix}
850&750\\
750&850
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its eigenvectors are the 45° and 135° directions, with eigenvalues $1600/3$ and $100/3$. More generally, PCA obtains its principal directions as eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. Each eigenvalue is the variance along its direction.

</details>

## Compression preserves a pattern and loses a difference

Keep only the 45° score. A and D retain their low-versus-high overall performance. But B and C both receive score 0, even though their strengths are reversed.

Reconstructing from this one score puts both B and C at $(60,60)$. The difference between $(55,65)$ and $(65,55)$ has been discarded. Retaining 94.1% of variance therefore does **not** mean retaining every important fact about each student.

PCA's objective is geometric: retain variance, or equivalently minimize squared reconstruction error for a fixed-dimensional linear projection. It does not ask which difference predicts a target or answers your practical question. A small subject imbalance could matter greatly for choosing a specialized course even though it accounts for only 5.9% of the variation here.

## How scaling and PCA fit together

Scaling happens before PCA when a common SD ruler is appropriate. PCA then looks for high-variance directions in that chosen coordinate system. Running PCA on unscaled age and income could mostly follow income's dollar scale. Standardizing first can prevent that particular units effect, but it changes the question to variation measured in SD units.

Neither choice is automatically correct. If raw units already encode meaningful comparable differences, preserve that meaning. If units differ arbitrarily, standardization may make the comparison more useful. PCA does not remove the need to choose a sensible measurement scale.

## Two other variance applications

**Feature selection:** a feature constant across the observed rows cannot distinguish those rows. A nearly constant feature, however, can still identify a rare important case. Low variance is a reason to inspect it, not automatic proof of irrelevance. High variance may just be noise; multiplying a column by 1,000 multiplies its variance by 1,000,000 without adding information.

**Bias–variance tradeoff:** model variance concerns how fitted predictions change when the training sample changes. It is different from variation within one feature:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\operatorname{Var}_{D}[\widehat f_D(x)].
$$

A high-variance learning procedure can react strongly to accidental features of its training data, which can be a sign of overfitting. It is not a complete diagnosis on its own.

The slide's applications—feature selection, PCA, model variance, and standardized values—are useful once these meanings are kept separate. “Large variance” does not universally mean “large information value,” and PCA does not guarantee that predictive signal survives compression.
