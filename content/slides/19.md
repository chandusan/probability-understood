# IQR protects the middle of the story

Range follows the two most extreme observations. IQR asks a different question: how far apart are the lower- and upper-quarter landmarks? By looking at middle ranks, it reduces the influence of how far a few tail observations wander.

## Work the ranked-number example

The illustration contains these 19 ordered values:

$$
11,13,16,19,20,21,23,25,26,29,33,34,36,38,39,46,52,55,58.
$$

It marks

$$
Q_1=20,\qquad Q_2=29,\qquad Q_3=39.
$$

These values match a **median-of-halves construction excluding the overall median**. The 10th value, 29, is the median. Removing it leaves nine values in each half. Their middle values are 20 and 39.

Therefore

$$
\operatorname{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1=39-20=19.
$$

For comparison, the whole sample's range is $58-11=47$.

The colored groups in the illustration each contain four observations: below $Q_1$, between $Q_1$ and the median, between the median and $Q_3$, and above $Q_3$. The three quartile landmarks separate those groups.

## Why an extreme value has less influence

Imagine replacing the largest value, 58, by 580. It remains the largest observation. It does not cross any of the middle ranks that determine the marked quartiles.

The range grows enormously. Under this same quartile convention, $Q_1$, $Q_2$, and $Q_3$ stay unchanged, so IQR remains 19.

This is the mechanism behind robustness. Once a value lies well out in a tail, its exact distance need not determine the middle ranks. **Robust does not mean frozen:** adding observations changes the sample size and may move the quartile positions, as the diamonds will demonstrate.

## Keep the convention attached to the answer

The default linear interpolation method gives different quartiles for the same 19 observations:

$$
Q_1=\frac{20+21}{2}=20.5,
\qquad
Q_3=\frac{38+39}{2}=38.5.
$$

That gives IQR $=18$. The pictured answer 19 is not a subtraction error; it comes from another quartile convention.

A second useful comparison is the sample $1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8$:

| Convention | $Q_1$ | $Q_3$ | IQR |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Default linear interpolation | 2.75 | 6.25 | 3.5 |
| Median of each half | 2.5 | 6.5 | 4 |

Always establish the convention before comparing numerical answers.

## What IQR preserves—and what it loses

IQR has the original data units. Under standard symmetric sample interpolation conventions,

$$
\operatorname{IQR}(X+b)=\operatorname{IQR}(X),
$$

$$
\operatorname{IQR}(aX)=|a|\operatorname{IQR}(X).
$$

Translation moves both quartiles equally. Scaling stretches their separation; a negative multiplier reverses order while distances remain nonnegative. Quantile choices at discontinuities or ties can require care when applying such identities outside these conventions.

IQR deliberately leaves out some tail information. Two datasets may share an IQR but have very different extremes. It can even be zero while some observations differ: with default linear quartiles, $0,0,0,0,100$ has $Q_1=Q_3=0$. Enough contamination can also move the quartiles drastically.

Thus “the middle 50%” is the guiding interpretation, not a guarantee that every small or tied sample splits into exact quarters.

## Check your understanding

In the 19-value example, replace 58 by 5,800. Keeping the pictured quartile convention, what happens to the range and IQR?
