# Two distances, two shapes

“Distance” depends on the rule you use to measure it. Straight-line distance gives round balls; adding coordinate-by-coordinate distances gives diamond-shaped or octahedral balls. Growing these shapes around the data reveals why different error rules choose different centers.

## The same endpoints can have different distances

If you can travel straight through space from the origin to $(3,2,4)$, Pythagoras gives distance $\sqrt{3^2+2^2+4^2}=\sqrt{29}\approx5.39$.

If movement must follow the coordinate directions, you need three units along one axis, two along another, and four along the third. The shortest total is $3+2+4=9$. You can perform these movements in different orders without changing their total.

This is not a disagreement about where the points are. It is a change in the **rule used to measure separation**.

For a vector $\mathbf{v}=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$:

$$
\|\mathbf{v}\|_2=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_i^2},
\qquad
\|\mathbf{v}\|_1=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|v_i|.
$$

When $\mathbf{v}$ is the vector of prediction errors $\mathbf{x}-c\mathbf{1}_n$, these become the losses from slides 4 and 7:

$$
\|\mathbf{x}-c\mathbf{1}_n\|_2^2
=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-c)^2,
$$

$$
\|\mathbf{x}-c\mathbf{1}_n\|_1
=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_i-c|.
$$

## Why a circle becomes a diamond

A distance ball is the set of points within a chosen distance budget $r$ from its center.

In two dimensions, centered at the origin:

$$
B_2(r)=\{(u,v):u^2+v^2\le r^2\},
$$

which is a filled circle, while

$$
B_1(r)=\{(u,v):|u|+|v|\le r\},
$$

which is a filled diamond with vertices $(r,0),(0,r),(-r,0),(0,-r)$.

In three dimensions, the Euclidean ball is a filled sphere and the Manhattan ball is a filled octahedron. The octahedron has six vertices, one in each positive or negative coordinate-axis direction. The **unit** shapes in the original picture are the special case $r=1$.

A **sphere** strictly means the boundary at distance exactly $r$; a **ball** includes the interior at distances at most $r$. Illustrations often use “sphere” loosely for both.

## Grow an octahedron around the data

To connect this shape to the median, use

$$
\mathbf{x}=(3,6,12)
$$

as the center, and keep the candidate constant line

$$
L=\{(c,c,c):c\in\mathbb{R}\}.
$$

The growing $L_1$ ball is

$$
B_1(\mathbf{x},r)
=\{(u,v,w):|u-3|+|v-6|+|w-12|\le r\}.
$$

Its six vertices are

$$
(3\pm r,6,12),\quad
(3,6\pm r,12),\quad
(3,6,12\pm r).
$$

Here $r$ means **total absolute-error budget**. It is not the Euclidean length of a straight line drawn from the center to every point on the octahedron.

A candidate point $(c,c,c)$ is inside the ball exactly when

$$
|3-c|+|6-c|+|12-c|\le r.
$$

Thus growing the ball asks: “How large must the allowed total error become before at least one constant prediction is affordable?”

## Why first contact means minimum error

These balls are nested: increasing $r$ retains all previously affordable points and adds more.

- Before the ball meets $L$, no constant has an error that small.
- At the first contact, at least one constant becomes affordable.
- A smaller error cannot be possible, or the line would have met an earlier ball.

Therefore, **the first-contact radius is the minimum error, and the contact point identifies a minimizing constant**. This logic applies to either distance rule. The shape of the ball determines which constant is reached first.

## Why the first contact occurs at radius $9$

From the preceding slide:

$$
|3-c|+|6-c|+|12-c|\ge9+|6-c|\ge9.
$$

No ball of radius less than $9$ can contain any constant point. At $c=6$:

$$
|3-6|+|6-6|+|12-6|=3+0+6=9.
$$

So the radius-$9$ octahedron first touches the constant line at

$$
(6,6,6).
$$

The median appears because this point has the smallest total absolute deviation—not because we assumed the answer in advance.

## Where on the octahedron does the contact occur?

At radius $9$, two vertices are

$$
(12,6,12)
\quad\text{and}\quad
(3,6,3).
$$

Their connecting edge is

$$
\{(t,6,t):3\le t\le12\}.
$$

The point $(6,6,6)$ lies on this edge. It is **not** a vertex of the octahedron.

The constant line requires all three coordinates to be equal. On this edge, the second coordinate is already fixed at $6$, so the only point satisfying that requirement is $(6,6,6)$.

This is a concrete way to see why an absolute-error optimum can have a coordinate error equal to zero: the middle observation is matched exactly. It does not mean every $L_1$ optimization must touch a vertex.

### A supporting-plane proof of the same contact

A supporting plane touches a convex shape while leaving the entire shape on one side. In this example, use the plane

$$
u=w.
$$

Every point on the constant line lies in this plane.

For a point $(u,v,w)$ in the radius-$r$ octahedron,

$$
\begin{aligned}
u-w
&=(u-3)-(w-12)-9\\
&\le |u-3|+|w-12|-9\\
&\le r-|v-6|-9\\
&\le r-9.
\end{aligned}
$$

If $r<9$, then $u-w<0$ for every point in the ball. It cannot meet the plane $u=w$, and therefore cannot meet the constant line either.

At $r=9$, equality $u=w$ requires:

- $v=6$;
- $u\ge3$ and $w\le12$;
- $u=w$.

Those conditions describe exactly the edge $(t,6,t)$ for $3\le t\le12$. Its unique intersection with the constant line is $(6,6,6)$.

</details>

### What happens after first contact?

For radii from zero through twelve:

| Radius | Intersection with the constant line |
|---|---|
| $0\le r<9$ | None |
| $r=9$ | The single point $(6,6,6)$ |
| $9\le r\le12$ | All $(c,c,c)$ with $15-r\le c\le r-3$ |

For example, at $r=12$, the affordable constants are $3\le c\le9$.

The interval comes from the two relevant branches of the piecewise absolute-error function: $15-c\le r$ to the left of $6$, and $c+3\le r$ to its right.

## Contrast this with a growing Euclidean sphere

For the same data point, the Euclidean sphere first meets the constant line at the mean:

$$
(7,7,7).
$$

Its radius there is

$$
\sqrt{(3-7)^2+(6-7)^2+(12-7)^2}
=\sqrt{42}\approx6.48.
$$

At the median point, the Euclidean distance is $\sqrt{45}$, which is larger. But the mean point has Manhattan distance

$$
|3-7|+|6-7|+|12-7|=10,
$$

which is larger than the median's Manhattan distance $9$.

| Distance/cost rule | First constant point reached | Minimum |
|---|---|---:|
| Euclidean distance | $(7,7,7)$ | $\sqrt{42}$ |
| Squared Euclidean distance / SSE | $(7,7,7)$ | $42$ |
| Manhattan distance / total absolute error | $(6,6,6)$ | $9$ |

The numerical radii $\sqrt{42}$ and $9$ use different distance rules. Their magnitudes are not a contest between “better” and “worse” fits across losses.

## Why the Euclidean perpendicular rule does not transfer to $L_1$

For a sphere, the center-to-contact direction is normal to its tangent plane. At first contact with a line, the line lies in that tangent plane, so the radius is perpendicular to the line.

For the octahedron, the first-contact point can lie on an edge. A supporting plane still exists, but the center-to-contact vector need not be perpendicular to it or to the candidate line.

At the median point, the residual is

$$
(3,6,12)-(6,6,6)=(-3,0,6).
$$

Its dot product with $(1,1,1)$ is $3$, not zero. Nevertheless, it is the correct $L_1$ minimizer. We have changed the geometry of the error budget, so Euclidean perpendicularity is no longer the optimality test.

## A caution when viewing a 3D object on a 2D screen

The screen shows a projection of the three-dimensional scene. Two objects may appear to overlap on the screen while lying at different depths.

Actual contact must be determined by the distance equation, not merely by whether the projected silhouettes overlap. In our example, the mathematical test is $|3-c|+|6-c|+|12-c|\le r$.

## Important distinctions

- The original inset's “L2 (Octahedron)” label is a typo. In three dimensions, the $L_2$ ball is spherical and the $L_1$ ball is an octahedron.
- “All grid-aligned paths are equal length” needs a qualification: **shortest monotone grid-aligned paths without backtracking** have the same length. Taking a detour increases the length.
- Euclidean distance treats directions equally under rotations; Manhattan distance depends on the chosen coordinate axes.
- The squared $L_2$ loss is smooth everywhere. The raw Euclidean norm is not differentiable at the origin. Ridge regression normally uses a **squared $L_2$ penalty**, so that context matters for the slide's “smooth gradient” claim. That penalty typically shrinks coefficients continuously; it does not have the same mechanism for selecting many exact zeros as an $L_1$ penalty.
- An $L_1$ **penalty on model coefficients** can encourage zero coefficients, as in lasso regression. This does not mean that every absolute-error loss produces sparse coefficients, or that all residuals automatically become zero. The vector to which the norm is applied matters.
- The median example concerns $L_1$ loss on residuals. The regression sparsity comparison is a related application, not the explanation required to establish the median's optimality.
