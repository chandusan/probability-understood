# Equal average ratings, very different agreement

All four professors have average rating 3. That shared center hides a crucial difference: some students agree, while others are divided. Standard deviation distinguishes those patterns by measuring distances from the common mean.

## Read the dot plots as counts

The slide describes end-of-semester Student-Instructional Rating System responses on a 1–5 scale. It asks you to arrange four hypothetical professors from **smallest to largest variability**.

Counting the source dots gives:

| Professor | Rating 1 | Rating 2 | Rating 3 | Rating 4 | Rating 5 | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1 | 2 | 4 | 2 | 1 | 10 |
| B | 0 | 0 | 6 | 0 | 0 | 6 |
| C | 5 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 5 | 10 |
| D | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 10 |

B has **six** dots at 3; A, C, and D each have ten observations. Every distribution is centered at 3.

## Rank the pictures before calculating

**B:** everyone gives 3. There is no variation.

**A:** four ratings are exactly at the mean, four are one point away, and only two are two points away. Most of the mass is near the center.

**D:** ratings are spread evenly across all five values. Compared with A, more are at the extremes and fewer at the center.

**C:** every rating is two points away from the mean. Half give 1 and half give 5: complete polarization.

Therefore the source's ranking is

$$
\boxed{B<A<D<C},
$$

or, in variance notation,

$$
0=\operatorname{Var}(B)<\operatorname{Var}(A)
<\operatorname{Var}(D)<\operatorname{Var}(C).
$$

This describes spread, not which professor has the highest average. Their averages are equal.

## Verify the ranking with exact sample variances

| Professor | Mean | Sum of squared deviations | Sample variance | Sample SD |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| B | 3 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| A | 3 | 12 | $4/3$ | $\approx1.1547$ |
| D | 3 | 20 | $20/9$ | $\approx1.4907$ |
| C | 3 | 40 | $40/9$ | $\approx2.1082$ |

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through every squared-deviation total</summary>

For A,

$$
SS_A=1(1-3)^2+2(2-3)^2+4(3-3)^2
+2(4-3)^2+1(5-3)^2
=4+2+0+2+4=12.
$$

Thus $s_A^2=12/9=4/3$ and $s_A=2/\sqrt3\approx1.1547$.

For B,

$$
SS_B=6(3-3)^2=0,
\qquad s_B^2=\frac0{6-1}=0,
\qquad s_B=0.
$$

For C,

$$
SS_C=5(1-3)^2+5(5-3)^2=20+20=40.
$$

Thus $s_C^2=40/9$ and $s_C=\sqrt{40}/3\approx2.1082$.

For D,

$$
SS_D=2\big[(1-3)^2+(2-3)^2+(3-3)^2+(4-3)^2+(5-3)^2\big]=20.
$$

Thus $s_D^2=20/9$ and $s_D=\sqrt{20}/3\approx1.4907$.

A, C, and D share the divisor 9, so their squared-deviation totals already establish their order. B uses divisor 5 because its sample size is six, but zero divided by either positive divisor remains zero.

If these ratings are instead treated as each professor's entire finite population, divide A, C, and D by 10 and B by 6. The population variances are $1.2$, $4$, $2$, and $0$, respectively. The same ranking holds.

</details>

**Memory image:** B is agreement at the middle; C is disagreement between two extremes. A single average cannot distinguish them.

## Check your understanding

A and C have the same mean, minimum, and maximum. Why do their standard deviations differ?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

Those three summaries do not say where the remaining observations lie. A puts most ratings near the mean. C puts every rating at an extreme. SD uses all squared distances, so C's total is 40 while A's is only 12.

</details>
