# Why absolute errors choose the median

The median minimizes total walking distance to the observations. Moving the proposed center a little helps everyone on one side and hurts everyone on the other by the same amount, so the balance of counts determines where to stop.

## The help-desk picture

Imagine people living at different points along a straight road. You need to place one help desk at $c$. Each person's cost is the distance they must walk:

$$
|x_i-c|.
$$

The total walking cost is

$$
A(c)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_i-c|.
$$

A compact statement of the result is

$$
\widetilde{x}\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_i-c|.
$$

The symbol $\in$ matters: the median is one minimizing value, and an even-sized sample can have a whole interval of minimizers. Dividing this total by $n$ gives mean absolute error; it selects exactly the same constants.

Move the desk slightly to the right without passing anyone:

- Every person to the left must walk that extra distance.
- Every person to the right walks that much less.

If more people are to the right than to the left, moving right improves the total. If more are to the left, moving left improves it. The improvement stops when the counts balance around the middle.

**A faraway person does not get an extra vote because of how far away they are.** Their distance still contributes to the total cost, but moving the desk one small unit changes that cost by only one unit. This is different from squared error, whose sensitivity grows with the size of the error.

<summary>Why pairing the outer observations proves the result</summary>

### Proof 1: pair the outer observations

For any two values $a\le b$,

$$
|a-c|+|b-c|\ge b-a.
$$

The least total distance to both endpoints is their separation. Equality holds whenever $c\in[a,b]$.

Now sort the data and pair the smallest with the largest, the second-smallest with the second-largest, and so on.

For an odd sample, every pair achieves its minimum at the middle observation, and the unpaired middle observation contributes zero there. Hence the middle observation minimizes total absolute error.

For an even sample, all the paired intervals overlap on the interval between the two middle observations. Every point in that interval minimizes the sum. This explains the nonuniqueness noted on slide 6.

</details>

<summary>A calculus view: count the observations on each side</summary>

### Proof 2: the slope changes according to counts

Away from an observation value, the derivative of $|x_i-c|$ with respect to $c$ is:

$$
\frac{d}{dc}|x_i-c|=
\begin{cases}
-1,&c<x_i,\\
+1,&c>x_i.
\end{cases}
$$

Therefore,

$$
A'(c)=\#\{x_i<c\}-\#\{x_i>c\}
$$

whenever $c$ is not equal to an observation.

The loss decreases while more observations lie to the right, and increases once more lie to the left. At an observation, the absolute-value function has a corner, so an ordinary derivative may not exist there. A minimum can occur at that corner; it does not require an ordinary derivative of zero.

</details>

## Exact worked proof for $3,6,12$

Let

$$
A(c)=|3-c|+|6-c|+|12-c|.
$$

The outer pair gives

$$
|3-c|+|12-c|\ge 9.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{A(c)\ge 9+|6-c|\ge 9.}
$$

At $c=6$:

$$
A(6)=3+0+6=9.
$$

The lower bound is achieved, so $6$ is optimal. Achieving the overall minimum $9$ also requires $|6-c|=0$, making $c=6$ the unique minimizer.

The entire piecewise function is:

$$
A(c)=
\begin{cases}
21-3c,&c\le3,\\
15-c,&3\le c\le6,\\
c+3,&6\le c\le12,\\
3c-21,&c\ge12.
\end{cases}
$$

Its successive slopes are $-3,-1,+1,+3$. The change from a negative slope to a positive slope occurs at $6$.

The first inequality $|3-c|+|12-c|\ge9$ is an equality throughout $[3,12]$. It is the additional middle term $|6-c|$ that selects $6$.

## Compare the two losses without changing the data

| Candidate $c$ | Total absolute error | Sum of squared errors |
|---|---:|---:|
| $6$ | $3+0+6=9$ | $9+0+36=45$ |
| $7$ | $4+1+5=10$ | $16+1+25=42$ |

The median wins under absolute error. The mean wins under squared error. They optimize different costs.

## What robustness does—and does not—say

For $3,6,9$, replace the largest observation by a much larger value $M\ge6$:

$$
3,6,M.
$$

The median remains $6$, but the mean is $(9+M)/3$ and can become arbitrarily large.

This does **not** mean the median never moves when one observation changes. For example, changing $3$ to $100$ in $3,6,9$ gives $6,9,100$, whose median is $9$.

Robustness means resistance to being driven arbitrarily far by a small fraction of replacements. It does not mean complete insensitivity.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>What “50% breakdown” really guarantees</summary>

### The precise “50% breakdown” qualification

For the usual sample median, the smallest number of arbitrary replacements that can send it without bound is

$$
\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil.
$$

The corresponding finite-sample replacement-breakdown fraction is

$$
\frac{\lceil n/2\rceil}{n}.
$$

It approaches $1/2$ as the sample grows. For even $n$, exactly half can already be enough to break the usual midpoint median.

Example: start with $1,2,3,4$ and replace two observations to obtain $1,2,M,M$. Its median is

$$
\frac{2+M}{2},
$$

which grows without bound as $M$ grows. Thus the source's wording “up to half ... without shifting ... to infinity” is too strong at the boundary.

For three observations, one replacement cannot drive the median without bound while the other two remain fixed, but two replacements can. The breaking fraction is $2/3$ for this small sample; the familiar 50% is the limiting description, not an exact safe-fraction guarantee for every $n$.

The mean can be driven arbitrarily far by one replacement, so its replacement-breakdown fraction is $1/n$.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Do other losses choose other summaries?</summary>

### Answers to the slide's “other losses” prompt

The selected summary depends on the cost assigned to mistakes.

| Loss for choosing $c$ | A minimizing summary | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $\sum_i(x_i-c)^2$ | Mean | Balances all squared deviations |
| $\sum_i\lvert x_i-c\rvert$ | Median(s) | Balances counts on either side |
| $\max_i\lvert x_i-c\rvert$ | Midrange: $(\min x_i+\max x_i)/2$ | Balances the two worst endpoint errors |
| $\sum_i\mathbf{1}\{x_i\ne c\}$ | Mode(s) | Matches as many observations as possible |

For the midrange, moving toward one endpoint makes the distance to the opposite endpoint worse; centering between the endpoints equalizes the two largest misses.

For the exact-match loss, each nonmatching observation costs one, so any most frequent value minimizes the count of mismatches. If several values share the largest frequency, there are multiple modes.

</details>

## Important distinctions

- MAE ordinarily means **mean** absolute error, $n^{-1}\sum|x_i-c|$. The expression on the slide is the sum. They differ by a positive constant factor and have the same minimizers.
- With even samples, say the usual median **belongs to** the arg-min set, because that set can be an interval.
- Prefer the median when a middle experience or resistance to extremes is the goal. Skewness alone does not make the median universally preferable; the mean may be the relevant quantity when totals or expected cost matter.
- Robustness does not establish that an unusual observation is erroneous or should be removed.
