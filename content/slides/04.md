# Why squared errors choose the mean

If one number must stand in for every observation, you need a rule for judging its mistakes. When that rule adds squared errors, the mean is the unique best choice. The formula is the result of this optimization problem, not an arbitrary convention.

## Give every candidate a score

Choose one constant $c$ and measure its total squared error:

$$
J(c)=\operatorname{SSE}(c)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-c)^2.
$$

The winner is the value of $c$ with the smallest score. The claim we will establish is

$$
\bar{x}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{c\in\mathbb{R}}J(c).
$$

## What are we optimizing?

Suppose we must replace every observation with the same prediction $c$.

The error for observation $i$ is $x_i-c$. Adding signed errors would let positive and negative misses cancel. We therefore need a way to measure their sizes.

Squaring does two things:

1. It makes the contribution nonnegative.
2. It penalizes a large miss more strongly than a small one.

A miss of $10$ contributes $100$; a miss of $5$ contributes $25$. Doubling a miss quadruples its squared cost.

The phrase **arg min** means “the input value where the function is smallest.” The minimum cost is a number; the arg min is the choice of $c$ that achieves that cost.

<summary>Derive the exact squared-error identity</summary>

### Proof 1: split the error into two pieces

Write

$$
x_i-c=(x_i-\bar{x})+(\bar{x}-c).
$$

Square and add across the observations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-c)^2
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^2
+2(\bar{x}-c)\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})
+n(\bar{x}-c)^2.\end{aligned}
$$

The middle term is zero because deviations from the sample mean sum to zero. Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{SSE}(c)
=\operatorname{SSE}(\bar{x})+n(c-\bar{x})^2.}
$$

Read the result as:

> Error at any other constant = error at the mean + an extra nonnegative penalty for moving away from the mean.

The first term does not depend on our new choice of $c$. The second term is at least zero, and it is zero only when $c=\bar{x}$. The mean is therefore the unique least-squares constant for a nonempty dataset.

This identity gives more than a recipe. It tells us **how much worse** another constant is:

$$
\operatorname{SSE}(c)-\operatorname{SSE}(\bar{x})=n(c-\bar{x})^2.
$$

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>A second proof using a derivative</summary>

### Proof 2: the derivative

For readers comfortable with calculus:

$$
\begin{aligned}
J'(c)
&=-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-c)\\
&=2nc-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i.
\end{aligned}
$$

Set the derivative to zero:

$$
2nc-2\sum x_i=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
c=\frac{\sum x_i}{n}=\bar{x}.
$$

Since

$$
J''(c)=2n>0,
$$

this point is a minimum.

</details>

## Worked example: $3,6,12$

The mean is $7$.

At $c=7$:

$$
\operatorname{SSE}(7)
=(3-7)^2+(6-7)^2+(12-7)^2
=16+1+25=42.
$$

At $c=6$:

$$
\operatorname{SSE}(6)=9+0+36=45.
$$

The general identity predicts exactly this difference:

$$
45=42+3(6-7)^2.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
J(c)=42+3(c-7)^2.
$$

## Why SSE and MSE choose the same center

$$
\operatorname{MSE}(c)=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{SSE}(c).
$$

Dividing every candidate cost by the same positive number changes the scale of the vertical axis, not the position of its minimum.

## Important distinctions

- The Euclidean norm is $\sqrt{\sum(x_i-c)^2}$. SSE is its **square**. They are different numerical quantities but have the same minimizing $c$ because squaring or taking a square root preserves order for nonnegative values.
- The mean is “best” here only for the stated squared-error objective. A different loss can select a different center.
- If observations have units of minutes, SSE has units of minutes squared. We return to this units issue when discussing variance and standard deviation.

## Check your understanding

A dataset has four observations, mean 5, and squared-error total 8 at that mean. What is its squared-error total if you use 4 instead?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Work through the answer</summary>

Use the error decomposition:

$$
\operatorname{SSE}(4)=\operatorname{SSE}(5)+4(4-5)^2=8+4=12.
$$

The extra error is $4$, not $1$: shifting the common prediction affects all four coordinates. No alternative constant can beat the total $8$ achieved by the mean.

</details>
