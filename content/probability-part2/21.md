# Solve the city example and recover every region

The chance that at least one of the two cities reaches the final five is 47%. The calculation is short, but a complete region table shows exactly what that answer includes and lets us answer several related questions without rebuilding the model.

## Apply the addition rule with the supplied overlap

Using $P(A)=0.20$, $P(B)=0.35$, and $P(A\cap B)=0.08$,

$$\begin{aligned}
P(A\cup B)
&=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\\
&=0.20+0.35-0.08\\
&=\boxed{0.47}.
\end{aligned}$$

The answer is 47%, not 0.47%. To express a probability as a percentage, multiply its decimal form by 100.

The initial sum 0.55 counted the 0.08 both-selected probability twice. Subtracting 0.08 leaves one copy, as required by inclusive “or.”

## Construct the full table

Subtract the overlap from each marginal event probability, then use the complement of the union for neither:

| Outcome for these two cities | Probability |
|---|---:|
| A selected, B not selected | $0.20-0.08=0.12$ |
| B selected, A not selected | $0.35-0.08=0.27$ |
| Both selected | $0.08$ |
| Neither selected | $1-0.47=0.53$ |
| Total | $1.00$ |

The rows describe mutually exclusive events and cover every possible finalist list. This is why their probabilities must sum to one.

Check the input probabilities by recombining rows: A has probability $0.12+0.08=0.20$, while B has probability $0.27+0.08=0.35$. These checks catch many errors involving an overlap counted too many or too few times.

## Answer nearby questions carefully

Exactly one of the two cities is selected with probability $0.12+0.27=0.39$. At most one is selected with probability $1-0.08=0.92$, because “at most one” includes neither. At least one is selected with probability 0.47.

These phrases correspond to different collections of table rows. Drawing or tabulating the regions before calculating is often more reliable than choosing a formula by recognizing a keyword.

The union also passes its natural bounds:

$$\max(0.20,0.35)\leq0.47\leq\min(1,0.20+0.35).$$

It must be at least as probable as either event alone, since it contains each event, and cannot exceed their uncorrected sum.

## What learning one selection tells us about the other

Among the outcomes where A is selected, the both-selected outcomes occupy probability share

$$P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}=\frac{0.08}{0.20}=0.40.$$

Thus learning that A is selected changes B's probability from 35% to 40%. Reversing the condition gives

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{0.08}{0.35}\approx0.2286.$$

The two conditional probabilities differ because their reference groups differ. These calculations preview conditional probability: restrict attention to one event, then measure the fraction within it belonging to the other. They also confirm that the selections are not independent.
