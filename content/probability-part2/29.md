# Seven disjoint regions explain the three-event picture

Three events can overlap, but their membership patterns form disjoint regions. The slide names the seven regions inside the union $E_1$ through $E_7$. Once those labels are understood, a complicated overlap calculation becomes ordinary addition of separate pieces.

## Decode the original diagram carefully

In the source picture, A is the left circle, B is the upper circle, and C is the right circle. The labels describe the following events:

| Region | Meaning | Set expression |
|---|---|---|
| $E_1$ | All three events | $A\cap B\cap C$ |
| $E_2$ | A and B, but not C | $A\cap B\cap C^c$ |
| $E_3$ | B and C, but not A | $A^c\cap B\cap C$ |
| $E_4$ | A and C, but not B | $A\cap B^c\cap C$ |
| $E_5$ | A only | $A\cap B^c\cap C^c$ |
| $E_6$ | B only | $A^c\cap B\cap C^c$ |
| $E_7$ | C only | $A^c\cap B^c\cap C$ |

The rectangle represents the sample space. Its remaining region, outside all three circles, is

$$
E_0=A^c\cap B^c\cap C^c=(A\cup B\cup C)^c.
$$

There are therefore eight possible membership patterns in total: each of A, B, and C can be true or false, giving $2^3=8$ combinations. Some regions can be empty in a particular application; drawing them does not assert positive probability.

## Distinguish an intersection from an exclusive overlap

An outcome in $E_1$ belongs to both A and B. Consequently,

$$
A\cap B=E_1\cup E_2,
$$

not merely $E_2$. Similarly,

$$
A\cap C=E_1\cup E_4,\qquad
B\cap C=E_1\cup E_3.
$$

“A and B” imposes no restriction on C. To exclude C, write “A and B but not C” or explicitly intersect with $C^c$. This distinction is the main source of mistakes when translating a verbal description into a diagram.

## Reconstruct every event from its pieces

The three circles are the disjoint unions

$$
\begin{aligned}
A&=E_1\cup E_2\cup E_4\cup E_5,\\
B&=E_1\cup E_2\cup E_3\cup E_6,\\
C&=E_1\cup E_3\cup E_4\cup E_7.
\end{aligned}
$$

Because the $E_i$ do not overlap, their probabilities add directly. If $p_i=P(E_i)$, for example, then $P(A)=p_1+p_2+p_4+p_5$. The union has probability

$$
P(A\cup B\cup C)=p_1+p_2+p_3+p_4+p_5+p_6+p_7.
$$

The circle areas in this illustrative drawing are not numerical probabilities. To calculate, we need stated region probabilities, outcome counts, or a model that supplies them.

## Translate “exactly,” “at least,” and “none”

The region language makes several related questions precise:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{exactly one})&=p_5+p_6+p_7,\\
P(\text{exactly two})&=p_2+p_3+p_4,\\
P(\text{all three})&=p_1,\\
P(\text{at least two})&=p_1+p_2+p_3+p_4,\\
P(\text{none})&=p_0.
\end{aligned}
$$

For the club example on the preceding page, the seven interior probabilities are

$$
(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4,p_5,p_6,p_7)
=(0.05,0.10,0.07,0.05,0.25,0.13,0.13).
$$

Thus exactly one club has probability $0.51$, exactly two has probability $0.22$, all three has probability $0.05$, and none has probability $0.22$. These four disjoint categories sum to one.

## Check your understanding

Why doesn't $P(A\cap B)+P(A\cap C)+P(B\cap C)$ equal the probability that at least two events occur?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Count the central region's appearances</summary>

The pair sum is $3p_1+p_2+p_3+p_4$. The target is $p_1+p_2+p_3+p_4$. The central region appears in three intersections, but should appear only once. Therefore

$$
P(\text{at least two})=
P(A\cap B)+P(A\cap C)+P(B\cap C)-2P(A\cap B\cap C).
$$

For exactly two, subtract three copies of the triple intersection instead. Building these formulas from region membership is more reliable than memorizing separate rules for every wording.
