# Crossing chords depend on the order of their endpoints

Two chords cross inside a circle exactly when their endpoints alternate around the circumference. That geometric observation turns the question into a small counting problem. Under the slide's intended model—four independent, uniformly chosen points $A,B,C,D$ on the circle, joined as $AB$ and $CD$—the probability of a crossing is $1/3$.

## Specify what “random chord” means

A chord can be generated in several ways: choose its endpoints, choose a midpoint and a direction, or choose a random line using some specified distribution. These mechanisms need not produce the same distribution of chords. The answer on this slide uses the endpoint model.

We choose each endpoint uniformly with respect to arc length, independently of the others. Coincident endpoints have probability zero, so we can reason using four distinct points. “Inside” excludes a shared endpoint on the circumference, but that exclusion does not change the probability in this continuous model.

## Fix a starting point and read clockwise

Rotating the whole picture cannot change whether the chords cross. We may therefore start our clockwise reading at $A$ and ask in what order $B,C,D$ appear. Their labels are interchangeable under the sampling mechanism, so the six orders are equally likely:

| Order after $A$ | Do $AB$ and $CD$ cross? |
|---|---|
| $B,C,D$ | No |
| $B,D,C$ | No |
| $C,B,D$ | Yes |
| $C,D,B$ | No |
| $D,B,C$ | Yes |
| $D,C,B$ | No |

To understand the criterion, chord $AB$ divides the disk into two regions and the circumference into two arcs. The segment $CD$ crosses $AB$ inside the disk precisely when $C$ and $D$ lie on opposite arcs. Reading from $A$, this means one of $C,D$ appears before $B$ and the other after it.

Thus the alternating arrangements are $A,C,B,D$ and $A,D,B,C$, giving

$$\boxed{P(AB\text{ crosses }CD)=\frac{2}{6}=\frac13.}$$

The six orders are equiprobable even though the actual spacings between the points vary continuously. We are grouping infinitely many geometric configurations into six events with equal probability.

## Check the result by conditioning on the first chord

Let $x$ be the fraction of the circumference covered by the clockwise arc from $A$ to $B$. Conditional on this chord, $C$ and $D$ must fall on opposite arcs. There are two disjoint possibilities, so

$$P(\text{crossing}\mid x)=x(1-x)+(1-x)x=2x(1-x).$$

When the endpoints of $AB$ are independently uniform, $x$ is uniform on $[0,1]$. Averaging over the possible first chords gives

$$
P(\text{crossing})=\int_0^1 2x(1-x)\,dx
=\left[x^2-\frac{2x^3}{3}\right]_0^1=\frac13.
$$

This second proof reveals something the order count hides. A chord with nearby endpoints is difficult to cross, since one of its arcs is tiny. A diameter is easiest to cross: $x=1/2$ gives crossing probability $1/2$. Averaging across all endpoint-generated chords reduces this to $1/3$.

## Check your understanding

If $AB$ is fixed to be a diameter and $C,D$ are independent uniform endpoints, what is the crossing probability? Does that contradict $1/3$?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

Each semicircle has probability $1/2$ for each endpoint. Opposite semicircles occur with probability $2(1/2)(1/2)=1/2$. There is no contradiction: fixing the first chord to a diameter is a different experiment from sampling both of its endpoints uniformly. The original $1/3$ averages over all the resulting first-chord lengths.
