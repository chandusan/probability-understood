# Six robberies in six districts: collisions are overwhelmingly likely

**Problem.** A city has six districts, and six robberies occur during a particular week. Assume that each robbery is equally likely to occur in any district, independently of the other robberies. **What is the probability that at least one district has two or more robberies?**

The event in the question seems to require many cases: one repeated district, several repeated districts, triples, and larger clusters. Its complement is remarkably simple: every district received exactly one robbery.

## Turn the wording into a precise model

Label the robberies $1,\ldots,6$, perhaps by their times, and the districts $1,\ldots,6$. An outcome is a six-entry list $(d_1,\ldots,d_6)$, where $d_i$ identifies the district of robbery $i$.

The assumption that all assignments are equally likely gives

$$|S|=6^6=46{,}656.$$

Equivalently, each robbery independently chooses a district with probability $1/6$. This is a mathematical model for the exercise; it does not claim real districts have equal exposure or that actual robberies are independent.

Let

$$E=\{\text{some district has at least two robberies}\}.$$

Then $E^c$ says every district has at most one. Because six robberies must fit into six districts, “at most one each” forces **exactly one each**. This last step would change if there were fewer robberies than districts.

## Count the collision-free assignments

To avoid repetition, robbery 1 can go to any of six districts. Robbery 2 has five unused choices, robbery 3 has four, and so on. Hence

$$|E^c|=6\cdot5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1=6!=720.$$

Divide by all assignments and use the complement rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(E)&=1-P(E^c)\\
&=1-\frac{6!}{6^6}\\
&=1-\frac5{324}
=\frac{319}{324}
\approx0.984568.
\end{aligned}
$$

So the probability that **at least one of the six districts** receives multiple robberies is about **98.46%** under the stated model. Equal numbers of robberies and districts do not make an even spread likely. A perfectly balanced spread is just 720 of the 46,656 equally likely assignments.

## The same calculation as sequential probabilities

The first robbery cannot collide with an earlier one. If the first two are to occupy different districts, robbery 2 must avoid one used district, with probability $5/6$. Given that the first two differ, robbery 3 must avoid two used districts, with probability $4/6$. Continuing,

$$P(E^c)=1\cdot\frac56\cdot\frac46\cdot\frac36\cdot\frac26\cdot\frac16.$$

These factors are conditional probabilities: each uses the information that no earlier collision has occurred. We are not asserting that the successive “no collision so far” events are independent.

## Avoid counting occupancy patterns equally

A list of district totals records how many robberies occurred in District 1, then District 2, and so on. For example, the assignment $(1,1,2,3,4,5)$ gives district totals $(2,1,1,1,1,0)$: two robberies in District 1, one each in Districts 2 through 5, and none in District 6.

The totals $(1,1,1,1,1,1)$ hide which robbery went where. They correspond to $6!=720$ labeled assignments. The totals $(6,0,0,0,0,0)$ correspond to only one. Therefore district-total patterns are not equally likely under independent assignments.

Nor can we simply add six probabilities of “District $j$ receives at least two.” Two or three districts might each receive multiple robberies, so those events overlap. The complement handles all overlapping possibilities in one calculation.

## Generalize the idea

For $m\le n$ independent uniform assignments to $n$ bins,

$$
P(\text{at least one collision})
=1-\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-m+1)}{n^m}.
$$

If $m>n$, a collision is certain by the pigeonhole principle. This is also the structure of the birthday problem: people are the distinguishable objects and days are the bins.

## Check your understanding

If there are two robberies and six districts instead, what is the probability that some district receives more than one robbery?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

The second robbery must choose the first robbery's district, so the probability is $1/6$. Using the complement gives $1-(6\cdot5)/6^2=1/6$. The no-collision complement now means two occupied districts and four empty ones, rather than exactly one robbery in every district.
