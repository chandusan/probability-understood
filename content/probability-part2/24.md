# Count exactly five spades by filling two categories

A 13-card hand with exactly five spades must contain eight non-spades. Choosing those two groups constructs every qualifying hand once. Dividing by the number of equally likely bridge hands then gives a probability of about 12.4692%.

## Read every factor as a concrete choice

There are 13 spades available, so the five spades can be selected in

$$\binom{13}{5}=1{,}287$$

ways. The other three suits contain 39 cards, so the eight non-spades can be selected in

$$\binom{39}{8}=61{,}523{,}748$$

ways. Every spade selection can be combined with every non-spade selection. Therefore,

$$\begin{aligned}
|A|&=\binom{13}{5}\binom{39}{8}\\
&=1{,}287\cdot61{,}523{,}748\\
&=79{,}181{,}063{,}676.
\end{aligned}$$

The multiplication principle applies because the two selections together specify one complete hand, and every qualifying hand can be separated into one unique spade subset and one unique non-spade subset.

## Divide by all thirteen-card hands

Under the uniform dealing model,

$$\begin{aligned}
P(A)&=\frac{\binom{13}{5}\binom{39}{8}}{\binom{52}{13}}\\
&=\frac{79{,}181{,}063{,}676}{635{,}013{,}559{,}600}\\
&\approx\boxed{0.1246919258}.
\end{aligned}$$

As a percentage, this is approximately 12.4692%. Retaining the exact ratio until the final step avoids accumulating rounding error when this probability is later combined with other quantities.

## Why there is no factor for choosing positions

The combinations already count unordered hands. Multiplying by $\binom{13}{5}$ again to decide which dealing positions contain spades would switch part of the calculation to ordered sequences while keeping an unordered denominator.

An ordered approach is possible, but it requires an ordered count throughout. Choose the five spade positions, arrange five distinct spades in them, and arrange eight distinct non-spades in the other positions. Its ratio is

$$\frac{\binom{13}{5}\frac{13!}{8!}\frac{39!}{31!}}{\frac{52!}{39!}}.$$

Simplifying gives exactly the same probability as the combination formula. Both the favorable ordered count and the total ordered count are $13!$ times their unordered counterparts.

## Recognize the general pattern

If $X_S$ is the number of spades in the hand, then for $k=0,1,\ldots,13$,

$$P(X_S=k)=\frac{\binom{13}{k}\binom{39}{13-k}}{\binom{52}{13}}.$$

This is a hypergeometric probability: sampling without replacement from a population with two categories. Every hand has exactly one spade count, so these 14 probabilities sum to one.

It differs from a binomial model of 13 independent draws with spade probability $1/4$. Real cards are drawn without replacement: after drawing a spade, the next spade probability becomes $12/51$, not $1/4$.

## Exactly five and at least five use different sums

The event $X_S\geq5$ includes six-spade, seven-spade, and longer spade hands. Its probability is

$$P(X_S\geq5)=\sum_{k=5}^{13}\frac{\binom{13}{k}\binom{39}{13-k}}{\binom{52}{13}}.$$

These terms can be added directly because the events $X_S=k$ are mutually exclusive for different k.

## Check your understanding

Write the probability that the hand has no spades. Explain why it is not $(3/4)^{13}$.

The answer is $\binom{39}{13}/\binom{52}{13}$, since all 13 cards must come from the 39 non-spades. Sequentially it is $(39/52)(38/51)\cdots(27/40)$. Each successful non-spade draw changes the next probability. The power $(3/4)^{13}$ would apply to independent draws with replacement, which is a different experiment.
