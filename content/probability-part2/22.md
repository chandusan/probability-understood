# A bridge hand is an unordered set of thirteen cards

Before finding the probability of a particular suit pattern, establish the sample space. For one specified bridge player receiving a uniformly shuffled standard deck, every set of 13 distinct cards is equally likely. The order in which those cards arrive does not change the hand.

## Specify the deck and the random experiment

A standard deck has 52 distinct cards: 13 ranks in each of four suits, spades, hearts, diamonds, and clubs. There are no jokers. We focus on the 13 cards received by one specified player, without replacement, under a uniform shuffle.

The sample space is

$$S=\{H:H\subseteq\text{deck},\ |H|=13\}.$$

The notation $|H|=13$ says the set H contains 13 cards. A card cannot appear twice because the hand is a subset of a deck of distinct cards.

The number of hands is

$$\boxed{|S|=\binom{52}{13}=635{,}013{,}559{,}600.}$$

This denominator will be shared by all four bridge questions on the next slide.

## Why divide an ordered count by thirteen factorial?

An ordered sequence of 13 drawn cards has

$$52\cdot51\cdots40=\frac{52!}{39!}$$

possibilities. Every fixed hand can arrive in $13!$ orders. Erasing the arrival order collapses those $13!$ sequences into one hand, so

$$|S|=\frac{52!/39!}{13!}=\frac{52!}{13!39!}.$$

For example, receiving the ace of spades first and the king of hearts second produces the same final hand as receiving those two in the reverse order, provided the other 11 cards are unchanged.

If we chose to work with ordered draws instead, that would also be valid, but both numerator and denominator would need to count ordered outcomes consistently.

## Why are unordered hands equally likely?

Under a uniform shuffle, all $52!$ complete deck orders have the same probability. Fix a particular 13-card set for the chosen player's 13 dealing positions. Its cards can occupy those positions in $13!$ orders, and the other 39 cards can occupy the remaining positions in $39!$ orders.

Thus each hand is generated by exactly $13!39!$ full deck orders and has probability

$$\frac{13!39!}{52!}=\frac1{\binom{52}{13}}.$$

The argument works whether the player receives the first 13 cards or every fourth card in a deal: the specified positions are fixed, and the same number of permutations fills them.

## One player's hand versus a complete deal

A full bridge deal assigns hands to four named players. Its number of possibilities is

$$\binom{52}{13}\binom{39}{13}\binom{26}{13}\binom{13}{13}
=\frac{52!}{(13!)^4}.$$

That is a larger sample space because it records who receives the remaining cards. For a question only about one player's hand, counting those additional assignments adds work that eventually cancels. Every fixed hand for our player has the same number of completions to a full deal.

## Turn the denominator into a probability

If an event E consists of certain 13-card hands, then under this uniform model,

$$P(E)=\frac{|E|}{\binom{52}{13}}.$$

Counting alone would not justify this ratio under a biased dealing process. Equal likelihood is the bridge between the combinatorial calculation and its probability interpretation.
