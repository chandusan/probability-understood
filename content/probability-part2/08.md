# One specific bin: define the question before counting

Throw $m$ distinguishable balls into $n$ distinguishable bins, with each ball independently choosing each bin with probability $1/n$. For a bin selected in advance, what is the probability of receiving exactly $k$ balls? The words “specific” and “exactly” are doing important work: they determine both what we count and what must be excluded.

## What one outcome records

Label the balls $1,2,\ldots,m$ and the bins $1,2,\ldots,n$. An outcome is a list of destinations,

$$\omega=(b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_m),\qquad b_i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.$$

For instance, with three balls and two bins, $(1,2,1)$ means balls 1 and 3 went to Bin 1 while ball 2 went to Bin 2. The outcome $(2,1,1)$ produces the same totals but is a different assignment of labeled balls.

There are $n^m$ destination lists, each with probability $(1/n)^m$. This equiprobability is the reason favorable-count divided by total-count will work. The act of “throwing” alone does not guarantee it; uniform, independent destinations are the model assumptions.

## Focus on Bin 1

Choose Bin 1 before any balls are thrown, and let

$$X=\text{the number of balls in Bin 1}.$$

The requested event is $\{X=k\}$, with $k\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\}$. We are not asking whether *some* bin contains $k$, nor whether Bin 1 contains *at least* $k$.

For three balls and two bins, “Bin 1 has exactly two” consists of

$$\{(1,1,2),(1,2,1),(2,1,1)\}.$$

There are eight total assignments, so its probability is $3/8$. The assignment $(1,1,1)$ does not qualify: three is not exactly two.

## Decide which balls arrive, then keep all others out

The favorable assignment requires two decisions. First choose the $k$ balls that enter Bin 1: there are $\binom mk$ choices. Each of those chosen balls has its destination fixed. Every remaining ball must go to one of the other $n-1$ bins; otherwise Bin 1 would receive more than $k$.

Thus, for $n\ge2$,

$$
\boxed{P(X=k)=\frac{\binom mk(n-1)^{m-k}}{n^m}.}
$$

The next slides examine the numerator and translate this answer into a binomial probability. Here, focus on the meaning of each factor: which balls enter, where the others go, and how many total assignments were possible.

## “Specific bin” is different from “some bin”

With two balls and two bins, a specific bin has exactly one ball with probability $1/2$. Some bin has exactly one ball also with probability $1/2$, because whenever one bin has one, the other does too. Multiplying the specific-bin probability by two would count the same qualifying outcome twice.

By contrast, the *expected number* of bins containing exactly $k$ balls is

$$nP(X=k).$$

Expectation counts qualifying bins and can exceed one. It is not generally the probability that at least one such bin exists. Keeping counts and event probabilities separate prevents a common misuse of symmetry.

## Check your understanding

Four balls independently choose among three bins. How many assignments put exactly two balls in Bin 1? What is the probability?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

Choose the two balls in $\binom42=6$ ways. Each remaining ball has two permitted destinations, giving $2^2=4$ choices. There are $6\cdot4=24$ favorable assignments out of $3^4=81$, so the probability is $24/81=8/27$. The unused bins may receive any distribution of the remaining balls, including both in one bin.
