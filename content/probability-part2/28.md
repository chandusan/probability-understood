# Three events need one final overlap correction

Adding three event probabilities counts an outcome once for each event it belongs to. Subtracting pair overlaps repairs the outcomes in exactly two events, but removes the central triple overlap completely. Inclusion–exclusion adds that central part back once.

## Read the formula as a counting instruction

For any events $A,B,C$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A\cup B\cup C)
={}&P(A)+P(B)+P(C)\\
&-P(A\cap B)-P(A\cap C)-P(B\cap C)\\
&+P(A\cap B\cap C).
\end{aligned}
$$

The union means **at least one of the three events occurs**. An outcome in two or three events still belongs to that union only once. The purpose of the alternating signs is to give every outcome in the union a final weight of one.

No independence assumption appears here. The identity works whether the events reinforce one another, exclude one another, or have any other relationship. What changes with the model is the value of each intersection probability.

## Follow one outcome through the calculation

Imagine a student who belongs to some of three clubs. Adding the three membership lists creates duplicate entries for students in several clubs. The correction depends only on how many clubs the student belongs to:

| Events containing the outcome | In the single-event sum | In the pair subtraction | In the triple addition | Final weight |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| 2 | 2 | $-1$ | 0 | 1 |
| 3 | 3 | $-3$ | $+1$ | 1 |

The last row is the reason for the final plus sign. A student in all three clubs appears in all three pairwise intersections: $A\cap B$, $A\cap C$, and $B\cap C$. Subtracting the pairs therefore subtracts that student three times, not twice.

## Work through a numerical example

Suppose the probabilities of belonging to three clubs are

$$
P(A)=0.45,\quad P(B)=0.35,\quad P(C)=0.30,
$$

with pair intersections

$$
P(A\cap B)=0.15,\quad P(A\cap C)=0.10,\quad P(B\cap C)=0.12,
$$

and triple intersection $P(A\cap B\cap C)=0.05$. Then

$$
P(A\cup B\cup C)=1.10-0.37+0.05=0.78.
$$

The sum $1.10$ exceeds one because memberships can overlap. It is a sum of three probabilities, not yet the probability of the union. After correcting the repeated contributions, $78\%$ belong to at least one club and $22\%$ belong to none.

Also notice that $P(A\cap B)=0.15$ includes the $0.05$ in all three clubs. The probability of belonging to A and B **but not C** is $0.15-0.05=0.10$.

## When does ordinary addition suffice?

If every pair of events is disjoint, every pair intersection is empty. The triple intersection must then be empty as well, and the formula reduces to

$$
P(A\cup B\cup C)=P(A)+P(B)+P(C).
$$

Merely knowing that the triple intersection is empty is insufficient. Events can overlap in pairs without anything belonging to all three. In that case the pair subtractions are still needed.

## Check your understanding

If each individual probability is $0.4$, each pair intersection has probability $0.1$, and the triple intersection is empty, what is the probability that at least one event occurs?

The answer is $3(0.4)-3(0.1)=0.9$. The triple term vanishes, but the three pair terms do not. The probability that none occurs is $0.1$. These numbers are consistent: each single-only region has probability $0.2$, each pair-only region has probability $0.1$, and the outside region has probability $0.1$.
