# Will two particular tennis players ever meet?

The tournament looks complicated because pairings change after every round, and odd numbers of players create byes. Yet the question asks for one simple event: do two specified players, $A$ and $B$, meet at any stage? The right preparation is to identify what stays unchanged across all the possible tournament histories.

## Read the random mechanism carefully

There are $n\ge2$ players. In each round, the remaining players are paired uniformly at random. If their number is odd, a uniformly chosen player receives a bye and advances without a match. Each match eliminates exactly one player. Under the equal-ability model, either participant has probability $1/2$ of winning, regardless of their label and previous results.

The symmetry matters: relabeling the players does not change the distribution of the tournament. There is no privileged player, protected seed, or skill advantage. The event is

$$
E_{AB}=\{A\text{ and }B\text{ play one another at some point}\}.
$$

They need not meet in the first round or in the final. Any encounter counts. Once they meet, one is eliminated, so a second encounter is impossible in this tournament.

## Distinguish the first round from the whole tournament

If $n$ is even, $A$ has $n-1$ possible first-round opponents, each equally likely. Thus

$$
P(\text{$A,B$ meet in round 1})=\frac1{n-1}.
$$

This is only one route to $E_{AB}$. When $A$ and $B$ receive different opponents and both survive, they may meet later.

For odd $n\ge3$, **both $A$ and $B$ must avoid the first-round bye** to meet in that round. Account for this in two steps:

1. **The bye goes to someone other than $A$ or $B$.** There are $n-2$ such players among the $n$ equally likely recipients, so this has probability $(n-2)/n$.
2. **Given that both are playing, they are paired together.** After the bye recipient is removed, $n-1$ players remain to be paired. Player $A$ has $n-2$ possible opponents, one of whom is $B$. The conditional probability is therefore $1/(n-2)$.

Multiplying these conditional steps gives

$$
P(\text{$A,B$ meet in round 1})
=\frac{n-2}{n}\cdot\frac1{n-2}
=\frac1n.
$$

For example, with five players $A,B,C,D,E$, the bye must go to $C$, $D$, or $E$, with probability $3/5$. Four players then play, and $A$ has three possible opponents, giving probability $1/3$ of drawing $B$. The result is $(3/5)(1/3)=1/5$.

We can also start by checking $A$ alone, but then we must account for $B$ in the conditional calculation. Given that $A$ avoids the bye, its recipient is uniform among the other $n-1$ players. Thus $B$ also avoids it with probability $(n-2)/(n-1)$. This gives the equivalent three-step calculation

$$
\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\frac{n-2}{n-1}\cdot\frac1{n-2}
=\frac1n.
$$

The three factors mean: $A$ avoids the bye; $B$ avoids it given that $A$ does; and they are paired given that both avoid it. The last two factors combine to $1/(n-1)$. That is why the shorter product $((n-1)/n)(1/(n-1))$ also works: $B$'s eligibility is already included in its second factor. The two avoid-bye events are not independent, because there is exactly one bye.

## A first-round bye still allows a second-round meeting

To meet in any particular round, both players must still be in the tournament and neither can receive **that round's** bye. A bye in an earlier round advances its recipient automatically.

For example, start with three players $A,B,C$. If $A$ receives the first-round bye, $B$ plays $C$. If $B$ wins, $A$ and $B$ meet in round 2, which is the final. So a first-round bye prevents a first-round meeting but can help a player reach a later meeting. The probability $1/n$ above concerns **round 1 only**; the question asks whether the pair ever meets.

## Work through four players

With four players, there are two routes to an $A$–$B$ meeting. They meet immediately with probability $1/3$. Otherwise, which happens with probability $2/3$, they have different opponents. Both must win their first match, an event with probability $1/4$ in the usual independent fair-match model. If both win, they meet in the final.

The two routes are disjoint, so

$$
P(E_{AB})=\frac13+\frac23\cdot\frac14=\frac12.
$$

This small case is worth doing: it shows why “$A$ has $n-1$ possible opponents” is insufficient for the full question. A player may face more than one opponent during a tournament.

## Find the quantity the bracket cannot change

Every match eliminates one player. To finish with one champion, the tournament must eliminate $n-1$ players, so it always contains exactly $n-1$ matches. A bye eliminates nobody and therefore adds no match to this count.

There are $\binom n2$ possible unordered player pairs. Every pair has the same probability of appearing, and no pair can appear twice. Sharing the fixed total of $n-1$ appearances equally across these pairs gives

$$
\boxed{P(E_{AB})=\frac{n-1}{\binom n2}=\frac2n.}
$$

## Check your understanding

In a tournament with ten equal-ability players, is the probability that $A$ meets $B$ equal to $1/9$, $1/10$, or $1/5$?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

The probability of meeting at some point is $2/10=1/5$. The value $1/9$ is only the probability of meeting in the first round. The value $1/10$ is each player's probability of becoming champion under the symmetric model; that is a different event.
