# Will two particular tennis players ever meet?

The tournament looks complicated because pairings change after every round, and odd numbers of players create byes. Yet the question asks for one simple event: do two specified players, $A$ and $B$, meet at any stage? The right preparation is to identify what stays unchanged across all the possible tournament histories.

## Read the random mechanism carefully

There are $n\ge2$ players. In each round, the remaining players are paired uniformly at random. If their number is odd, a uniformly chosen player receives a bye and advances without a match. Each match eliminates exactly one player. Under the equal-ability model, either participant has probability $1/2$ of winning, regardless of their label and previous results. We use independent fair match results.

The symmetry matters: relabeling the players does not change the distribution of the tournament. There is no privileged player, protected seed, or skill advantage. The event is

$$
E_{AB}=\{A\text{ and }B\text{ play one another at some point}\}.
$$

They need not meet in the first round or in the final. Any encounter counts. Once they meet, one is eliminated, so a second encounter is impossible in this tournament.

## Distinguish the first round from the whole tournament

Let $E$ be the event **“$A$ and $B$ play each other in round 1.”** This is one route to the larger event $E_{AB}$ of meeting at some point in the tournament. Assume pairings are uniformly random and, when $n$ is odd, the bye recipient is also chosen uniformly.

### If the number of players is even

Everyone plays. Player $A$ has $n-1$ possible opponents, each equally likely. Exactly one of them is $B$, so

$$
\boxed{P(E)=\frac1{n-1}.}
$$

For example, with six players, $A$ has five possible opponents, giving $P(E)=1/5$. When $A$ and $B$ receive different opponents and both survive, they may meet later, but that is outside the first-round event $E$.

### If the number of players is odd

For odd $n\ge3$, two things must happen. Here, **“both play” means neither $A$ nor $B$ receives the first-round bye**.

**Step 1: neither $A$ nor $B$ receives the bye.** The bye must go to one of the other $n-2$ players. Each of the $n$ players is equally likely to receive it, so

$$
P(\text{both play})=\frac{n-2}{n}.
$$

**Step 2: given that both play, $A$ is paired with $B$.** Removing the bye recipient leaves $n-1$ players to be paired. Excluding $A$ itself leaves $n-2$ possible opponents, including $B$. Therefore,

$$
P(E\mid\text{both play})=\frac1{n-2}.
$$

The vertical bar $\mid$ means **“given that.”** In this probability, we are restricting attention to draws in which both players avoid the bye, and asking what fraction of those draws pair them together.

Since meeting requires both to play, we can now multiply the probability of reaching Step 2 by the conditional probability of meeting once there:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(E)
&=P(\text{both play})\,P(E\mid\text{both play})\\
&=\frac{n-2}{n}\cdot\frac1{n-2}\\
&=\boxed{\frac1n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For example, with five players, the bye must go to one of the other three players: probability $3/5$. Among the four players who then play, $A$ has three possible opponents: probability $1/3$ of drawing $B$. Thus $P(E)=(3/5)(1/3)=1/5$.

Combining the even and odd cases, the **first-round meeting probability** is

$$
\boxed{
P(E)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac1{n-1},&n\text{ even},\\[6pt]
\dfrac1n,&n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}}
$$

### The same calculation starting with $A$

We can also start by checking $A$ alone, but then we must account for $B$ in the conditional calculation. Given that $A$ avoids the bye, its recipient is uniform among the other $n-1$ players. Thus $B$ also avoids it with probability $(n-2)/(n-1)$. This gives the equivalent three-step calculation

$$
\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\frac{n-2}{n-1}\cdot\frac1{n-2}
=\frac1n.
$$

The three factors mean: $A$ avoids the bye; $B$ avoids it given that $A$ does; and they are paired given that both avoid it. The last two factors combine to $1/(n-1)$. That is why the shorter product $((n-1)/n)(1/(n-1))$ also works: $B$'s eligibility is already included in its second factor. The two avoid-bye events are not independent, because there is exactly one bye.

## A first-round bye still allows a second-round meeting

To meet in any particular round, both players must still be in the tournament and neither can receive **that round's** bye. A bye in an earlier round advances its recipient automatically.

For example, start with three players $A,B,C$. If $A$ receives the first-round bye, $B$ plays $C$. If $B$ wins, $A$ and $B$ meet in round 2, which is the final. So a first-round bye prevents a first-round meeting but can help a player reach a later meeting. The probability $1/n$ above concerns **round 1 only**; the question asks whether the pair ever meets.

Let $E_2$ mean **“$A$ and $B$ play each other in round 2.”** We want its probability **before the tournament begins**, for $n\ge3$. Two things must happen: both players reach round 2, and then they are paired together.

Let $S$ mean **“$A$ and $B$ both reach round 2.”** Since meeting in that round requires reaching it,

$$\boxed{P(E_2)=P(S)\,P(E_2\mid S).}$$

We will derive each factor, including the effect of a round-2 bye.

### Step 1: count the players who reach round 2

Each first-round match produces one survivor, and a bye also produces one survivor. Therefore the number remaining is

$$
m=\begin{cases}
n/2,&n\text{ even},\\[4pt]
(n+1)/2,&n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
$$

For example, five starting players produce two match winners and one bye recipient, so $m=3$ players reach round 2.

### Step 2: calculate the probability that both reach round 2

Exactly $m$ of the original $n$ players advance. Every player has the same chance of occupying one of those survivor places, so

$$P(A\text{ reaches round 2})=\frac mn.$$

Given that $A$ advances, it occupies one of the $m$ survivor places. There are $m-1$ places left among the other $n-1$ players, whose labels are treated equally. Thus

$$
P(B\text{ reaches round 2}\mid A\text{ reaches round 2})
=\frac{m-1}{n-1}.
$$

Multiplying these conditional steps gives

$$\boxed{P(S)=\frac mn\cdot\frac{m-1}{n-1}.}$$

This includes advancement through a first-round bye. It also excludes $A$ and $B$ meeting in round 1, because that would eliminate one of them. We have not assumed that their survival events are independent.

The same factor has a direct counting form. There are $\binom nm$ equally likely survivor sets. Fixing $A$ and $B$ in the set leaves $m-2$ survivors to choose from the other $n-2$ players:

$$P(S)=\frac{\binom{n-2}{m-2}}{\binom nm}=\frac{m(m-1)}{n(n-1)}.$$

Why are those survivor sets equally likely? For any proposed set, each loser must be paired with a survivor who defeats them. With even $n$, there are $m!$ ways to make those pairings. With odd $n$, choose the bye from the $m$ survivors and pair the remaining $m-1$ survivors with the losers, giving $m(m-1)!=m!$ ways. Every proposed survivor set therefore has the same number of equally likely first-round draws and results.

### Step 3: pair them, given that both reached round 2

There are now $m$ players, and the draw is made afresh. The relevant parity is the parity of $m$, the number entering **round 2**.

**If $m$ is even**, everyone plays. Given $S$, player $A$ has $m-1$ equally likely opponents, one of whom is $B$:

$$P(E_2\mid S)=\frac1{m-1}.$$

**If $m$ is odd**, both must avoid the **round-2 bye**. Let $T$ mean **“$A$ and $B$ both play a match in round 2.”** This requires both to have reached that round and neither to receive its bye.

Given $S$, the bye must go to one of the other $m-2$ players, so

$$P(T\mid S)=\frac{m-2}{m}.$$

Given $T$, removing the bye recipient leaves $m-1$ players to be paired. Excluding $A$ leaves $m-2$ possible opponents, including $B$. Therefore

$$P(E_2\mid T)=\frac1{m-2}.$$

Meeting requires $T$, and $T$ already includes reaching round 2. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(E_2\mid S)
&=P(T\mid S)\,P(E_2\mid T)\\
&=\frac{m-2}{m}\cdot\frac1{m-2}\\
&=\frac1m.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is where **both players avoiding the bye** enters the round-2 calculation. A first-round bye, by contrast, was one way to reach round 2 in Step 2.

### Step 4: combine survival and pairing

Substituting the two factors into $P(E_2)=P(S)P(E_2\mid S)$ gives

$$
\boxed{
P(E_2)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac mn\dfrac{m-1}{n-1}\dfrac1{m-1}
=\dfrac{m}{n(n-1)},&m\text{ even},\\[12pt]
\dfrac mn\dfrac{m-1}{n-1}\dfrac1m
=\dfrac{m-1}{n(n-1)},&m\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}}
$$

For **eight starting players**, $m=4$. Both reach round 2 with probability $(4/8)(3/7)$, and their conditional chance of being paired is $1/3$:

$$P(E_2)=\left(\frac48\cdot\frac37\right)\cdot\frac13=\boxed{\frac1{14}}.$$

For **five starting players**, $m=3$. Both reach round 2 with probability $(3/5)(2/4)$. To meet, the third survivor must receive the round-2 bye, with probability $1/3$; $A$ and $B$ then have to play each other:

$$P(E_2)=\left(\frac35\cdot\frac24\right)\cdot\left(\frac13\cdot1\right)=\boxed{\frac1{10}}.$$

With $n=2$, there is no second round, so its meeting probability is zero. Handle that case separately: conditioning on both players reaching a nonexistent round would be undefined.

## Work through four players

With four players, there are two routes to an $A$–$B$ meeting. They meet immediately with probability $1/3$. Otherwise, which happens with probability $2/3$, they have different opponents. Both must win their first match, an event with probability $1/4$ in the usual independent fair-match model. If both win, they meet in the final.

The two routes are disjoint, so

$$
P(E_{AB})=\frac13+\frac23\cdot\frac14=\frac12.
$$

This small case is worth doing: it shows why “$A$ has $n-1$ possible opponents” is insufficient for the full question. A player may face more than one opponent during a tournament.

## Derive the whole-tournament probability by counting

Every match eliminates one player. To finish with one champion, the tournament must eliminate $n-1$ players, so it always contains exactly $n-1$ matches. A bye eliminates nobody and therefore adds no match to this count.

There are $M=\binom n2$ possible unordered player pairs. To understand why the final answer involves $(n-1)/M$, start with a familiar selection problem and then check the tournament's pairing restrictions.

### Count selections containing a specified pair

Suppose we select $k$ distinct pairs from the $M$ possibilities, **with every group of $k$ pairs equally likely**. There are $\binom Mk$ total selections. To include the specified pair $AB$, fix it as one member and choose the remaining $k-1$ pairs from the other $M-1$ possibilities. That gives $\binom{M-1}{k-1}$ favourable selections.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(AB\text{ is included})
&=\frac{\binom{M-1}{k-1}}{\binom Mk}\\[4pt]
&=\frac{(M-1)!}{(k-1)!(M-k)!}\,
\frac{k!(M-k)!}{M!}\\[4pt]
&=\frac{k}{M}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For example, selecting three pairs uniformly from six possibilities gives $\binom63=20$ total selections. Exactly $\binom52=10$ contain $AB$, so its inclusion probability is $10/20=1/2$.

### Count valid tournament draws before applying the ratio

A tournament imposes restrictions: $AB$ and $AC$ cannot be matches in the same round, and the complete encounter history must respect elimination. Therefore we must justify the calculation using valid draws; arbitrary groups of $n-1$ pairs are not an equally likely sample space for the tournament.

For a round with $r$ players and $k=\lfloor r/2\rfloor$ matches, count valid draws by listing the players and pairing consecutive positions. If $r$ is odd, the last player receives the bye. Reversing a pair or reordering matches does not change the draw, so divide the $r!$ lists by $2^k k!$. Fixing $AB$ as one match leaves $r-2$ players and $k-1$ matches to arrange. Thus, given that both players reached this round,

$$
P(AB\text{ meet}\mid\text{both reached the round})
=\frac{(r-2)!/[2^{k-1}(k-1)!]}{r!/[2^k k!]}
=\frac{k}{\binom r2}.
$$

Before the tournament begins, the probability that both belong to that round's $r$ survivors is the same selection count used in the second-round derivation:

$$
P(\text{both reach the round})
=\frac{\binom{n-2}{r-2}}{\binom nr}
=\frac{\binom r2}{\binom n2}.
$$

The survivor sets are equally likely under uniform draws and fair results. Any two proposed sets of $r$ survivors can be exchanged by relabeling the original players. This gives a one-to-one correspondence between their valid tournament histories, preserving every draw and match-result probability. Multiplying survival and conditional pairing probabilities gives

$$
P(AB\text{ meet in this round})
=\frac{\binom r2}{\binom n2}\cdot\frac{k}{\binom r2}
=\frac{k}{\binom n2}.
$$

### Add the disjoint possibilities for when they meet

Let $k_1,k_2,\ldots,k_R$ be the numbers of matches in the successive rounds. The events “$AB$ meet in round 1,” “$AB$ meet in round 2,” and so on are **mutually exclusive**: after one meeting, one player is eliminated. We can therefore use the addition rule for disjoint events:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(E_{AB})
&=\frac{k_1}{\binom n2}+\frac{k_2}{\binom n2}
+\cdots+\frac{k_R}{\binom n2}\\[4pt]
&=\frac{k_1+k_2+\cdots+k_R}{\binom n2}\\[4pt]
&=\frac{n-1}{\binom n2}\\[4pt]
&=\boxed{\frac2n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For five players, the round sizes are $5,3,2$, giving $2,1,1$ matches. Their meeting probability is $2/10+1/10+1/10=4/10=2/5$.

This supplies the missing justification for the ratio: count favourable valid draws, include survival through earlier rounds, and add the disjoint meeting events. [The next slide](03.html) develops that counting argument in full.

In a tournament with ten equal-ability players, is the probability that $A$ meets $B$ equal to $1/9$, $1/10$, or $1/5$?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

The probability of meeting at some point is $2/10=1/5$. The value $1/9$ is only the probability of meeting in the first round. The value $1/10$ is each player's probability of becoming champion under the symmetric model; that is a different event.
