# Two extensions reveal why the sampling model matters

The final exercises reuse factorial counting in two different ways. Restricting only some people's name draws requires a partial inclusion–exclusion sum. Choosing uniformly among all words with distinct letters produces a reciprocal exponential sum, with a probability astonishingly close to one over e.

## Exercise 1: only a specified subset must avoid itself

As on the preceding slide, draw n names without replacement so all $n!$ permutations are equally likely. Fix a particular subset of m people, with $0\le m\le n$. Only those m people must avoid their own names. Everyone else is unrestricted, subject to each name being drawn once.

Relabel the specified people as $1,\ldots,m$. For each of them let $A_i$ be a self-match. The target is

$$
P(A_1^c\cap\cdots\cap A_m^c)
=1-P(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_m).
$$

For any k specified forbidden self-matches, fixing those assignments leaves $(n-k)!$ choices for everyone else. There are $\binom mk$ choices of which restricted people are fixed. Thus

$$
\boxed{P(\text{all specified }m\text{ people avoid themselves})
=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^k\binom mk\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}.}
$$

The two different sizes in the expression are essential: choose the self-match restrictions from m people, but arrange the remaining names among all $n-k$ remaining people.

## Calculate an example and test the boundaries

For n=5 and a specified subset of m=3 people,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P&=1-3\frac{4!}{5!}+3\frac{3!}{5!}-\frac{2!}{5!}\\
&=1-\frac35+\frac3{20}-\frac1{60}
=\frac8{15}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Equivalently, $120-72+18-2=64$ of the 120 assignments qualify. The other two people may or may not draw themselves.

Useful checks are:

- For $m=0$, there are no restrictions and the probability is one.
- For $m=1$, the probability is $1-1/n$.
- For $m=n$, the expression becomes $\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^k/k!=D_n/n!$.

For fixed m, the probability approaches one as n grows: only m people have restrictions, and each one's self-match probability shrinks to zero. This differs from setting $m=n$ as n grows, which yields the limit $1/e$.

The phrase “a specific subset” is not “exactly m people avoid themselves.” The latter additionally forces all other people to draw their own names and defines a different event.

## Exercise 2: choose a norepeatword uniformly

A norepeatword is a nonempty ordered sequence of distinct letters from the 26-letter alphabet. It need not be an English dictionary word. For length $\ell$, choose and arrange $\ell$ distinct letters:

$$
N_\ell=26\cdot25\cdots(26-\ell+1)=\frac{26!}{(26-\ell)!},
\qquad1\le\ell\le26.
$$

Every individual norepeatword, across all allowed lengths, receives the same probability. The total sample-space size is therefore

$$
N=\sum_{\ell=1}^{26}\frac{26!}{(26-\ell)!}.
$$

Exactly $26!$ words use all 26 letters. Dividing and cancelling gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{uses all 26 letters})
&=\frac{26!}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{26}26!/(26-\ell)!}\\
&=\boxed{\frac1{\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{25}1/j!}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The substitution $j=26-\ell$ measures the number of unused letters. The upper limit is 25 because empty words are excluded.

## Explain both the approximation and its direction

Since $e=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/j!$, the finite denominator is slightly less than e. The exact probability is therefore slightly **greater** than $1/e$, approximately $0.3678794412$.

This is an exceptionally close approximation. Write $S=\sum_{j=0}^{25}1/j!$ and $R=e-S$. Starting with the term $1/26!$, subsequent ratios are at most $1/27$, so

$$
0<R<\frac1{26!}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac1{27}\right)^r
=\frac{27}{26\cdot26!}.
$$

Because $e>S>2.5$,

$$
0<\frac1S-\frac1e=\frac{R}{eS}
<\frac{27}{26\cdot26!\cdot6.25}
<4.12\times10^{-28}.
$$

## Uniform words do not imply uniform lengths

Choosing a length uniformly from 1 through 26 and then choosing a word of that length would give probability $1/26$ of using all letters. That is a different experiment: it gives the same total weight to length-one and length-26 words despite their very different counts.

Under the slide's model, lengths 26 and 25 each have $26!$ words; length 24 has $26!/2!$; length 23 has $26!/3!$. Thus short words receive little total probability even though every individual word has equal weight. The factorial counts explain why the answer resembles $1/e$: the normalization sums reciprocal factorials. In the derangement problem, $1/e$ arose instead from an alternating exclusion sum.
