# Recognize the binomial distribution inside the bin problem

The counting formula for one bin is also a binomial probability. Each ball supplies one independent yes-or-no trial: did it enter the chosen bin? Once we focus on that question, the identities of all the other bins no longer matter. Their combined probability becomes the single “no” probability $1-1/n$.

## What is a binomial distribution?

A **binomial distribution describes how many times a particular event happens in a fixed number of independent attempts**, when that event has the same probability on every attempt.

In slide 10, the event is **“this ball enters our chosen bin.”**

Let’s build the meaning from the beginning.

**First, what is a probability distribution?**

Suppose we throw **3 balls independently into 2 equally likely bins**, and watch Bin 1. Define

$$
X=\text{number of balls that enter Bin 1}.
$$

The possible values of $X$ are $0,1,2,3$. A probability distribution tells us **the probability of each of these possible counts**.

Write:

- $S$: the ball enters Bin 1.
- $F$: the ball enters the other bin.

Each ball has probability $1/2$ of either outcome. Independence makes each three-ball pattern have probability $(1/2)^3=1/8$.

| Count $X$ | Patterns producing that count | Probability |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | FFF | $1/8$ |
| $1$ | SFF, FSF, FFS | $3/8$ |
| $2$ | SSF, SFS, FSS | $3/8$ |
| $3$ | SSS | $1/8$ |

**That whole table is the distribution of $X$.** A formula for $P(X=k)$ lets us calculate any row of the table.

**Now, what makes this distribution “binomial”?**

We have:

1. A fixed number of attempts: **3 balls**.
2. A yes-or-no question for each attempt: **did it enter Bin 1?**
3. Independent attempts.
4. The same probability of “yes” on every attempt: **$1/2$**.

Whenever these conditions hold, the **number of yes outcomes** has a binomial distribution.

“Success” is just the conventional name for “yes”; it does not mean something good happened.

**Derive its general formula.**

Suppose there are $m$ attempts, each with success probability $p$. We want exactly $k$ successes.

One particular pattern containing $k$ successes and $m-k$ failures has probability

$$
p^k(1-p)^{m-k}.
$$

But the successes can occupy any $k$ of the $m$ positions. There are

$$
\binom mk
$$

such patterns. They are mutually exclusive, so we add their equal probabilities:

$$
\boxed{P(X=k)=\binom mk p^k(1-p)^{m-k}},
\qquad k=0,\ldots,m.
$$

This formula gives the **entire binomial distribution** when evaluated for every possible $k$.

**Return to slide 10.**

For $m$ balls and $n$ equally likely bins, each ball enters our chosen bin with probability

$$
p=\frac1n.
$$

Therefore,

$$
P(X=k)=\binom mk
\left(\frac1n\right)^k
\left(1-\frac1n\right)^{m-k}.
$$

We write this compactly as

$$
\boxed{X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(m,1/n).}
$$

The rearrangement revealed this familiar formula. **The underlying experiment already had the binomial structure:** count how many independent balls answer “yes” to the same question.

The name comes from the binomial expansion of $[p+(1-p)]^m$, whose terms are exactly these probabilities.

## Rearrange the count carefully

From the previous slide, for $n\ge2$ and $0\le k\le m$,

$$P(X=k)=\frac{\binom mk(n-1)^{m-k}}{n^m}.$$

Split $n^m=n^kn^{m-k}$ to obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X=k)
&=\binom mk\frac1{n^k}\frac{(n-1)^{m-k}}{n^{m-k}}\\
&=\binom mk\left(\frac1n\right)^k
\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{m-k}\\
&=\boxed{\binom mk\left(\frac1n\right)^k
\left(1-\frac1n\right)^{m-k}.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(m,1/n)$. The symbol $\sim$ means “has the distribution.” It does not say the realized count must equal its average.

## Use complements for a tail probability

Suppose ten balls independently choose among five bins. For a chosen bin, $X\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(10,0.2)$. To find the probability of at least two balls, exclude zero and one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X\ge2)
&=1-P(X=0)-P(X=1)\\
&=1-(0.8)^{10}-10(0.2)(0.8)^9\\
&\approx0.6241904.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the probability that **one preselected bin** receives at least two. It is not the probability that some bin does. With ten balls and five bins, some bin must contain at least two by the pigeonhole principle, so that different event has probability 1.

## What remains true when the bins are unequal?

If each ball independently enters the target bin with a constant probability $p$, the same binomial formula applies even when the other bins have different probabilities. We only need a common target probability and independent placements across balls.

If ball $i$ instead has its own target probability $p_i$, the count is generally not binomial. If balls travel in linked groups, independence fails and the formula can fail even when every ball individually has probability $1/n$ of entering the target.

Counts in different bins are also dependent: their sum is always $m$. Knowing one bin received all $m$ balls tells us every other bin received zero. This does not conflict with independence of the original ball destinations; these are different random quantities.

## Explore the distribution

Set the success probability to $1/n$ and the number of trials to $m$. Change either parameter and watch the possible count probabilities move. The expected count is $mp=m/n$, but outcomes fluctuate around it.
