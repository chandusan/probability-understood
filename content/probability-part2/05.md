# The complement rule turns “at least one” into “none”

An event and its complement exhaust every possibility without overlap. Their probabilities must therefore add to one. This simple fact often saves an enormous amount of counting: when an event can occur in many different ways, its failure may have a much cleaner description.

## Derive the rule from the axioms

For an event $E\subseteq S$, define its complement by

$$E^c=S\setminus E.$$

The slide writes the same set as $\overline E$. By definition,

$$E\cup E^c=S,\qquad E\cap E^c=\varnothing.$$

The first statement says every outcome is either in $E$ or outside it. The second says no outcome can satisfy both descriptions. Additivity for disjoint events therefore gives

$$1=P(S)=P(E\cup E^c)=P(E)+P(E^c).$$

Rearranging,

$$\boxed{P(E^c)=1-P(E)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\boxed{P(E)=1-P(E^c).}$$

The source title says “Compliment”; the mathematical term is **complement**. A compliment praises something; a complement completes a set to the whole sample space.

## An impossible event has probability zero

The complement of the entire sample space is the empty set: $S^c=\varnothing$. Applying the rule gives

$$P(\varnothing)=1-P(S)=0.$$

In a finite model where every individual outcome has positive probability, zero probability means the event is empty. In continuous models the converse need not hold: an individual point may have probability zero without being impossible. For example, a uniform point on $[0,1]$ has $P(X=1/2)=0$ even though $1/2$ belongs to the sample space. The rule itself works in both settings.

## Negate the whole statement correctly

The most important step is often linguistic:

| Event | Complement |
|---|---|
| At least one success | No successes |
| All trials succeed | At least one trial fails |
| At least two people share a birthday | All birthdays are distinct |
| Some district has more than one robbery | Every district has at most one robbery |
| Exactly two successes | Any number of successes except two |

The complement of “exactly two” is not “exactly one.” It includes zero and every count above two as well. Likewise, “not all” means at least one failure, not necessarily every trial failing.

## Work an example before the larger problems

Roll a fair die four times independently. What is the probability of at least one six? Direct counting must account for one, two, three, or four sixes. The complement has a single description: every roll is a non-six.

Each roll avoids six with probability $5/6$. Independence allows multiplication, so

$$
P(\text{at least one six})
=1-P(\text{no sixes})
=1-\left(\frac56\right)^4
=\frac{671}{1296}\approx0.5177.
$$

The complement rule never requires independence. Only the calculation of the no-six probability used it. If the four rolls were dependent, $P(E)=1-P(E^c)$ would still hold, but the fourth power might be wrong.

## Check your understanding

A system has three components. Let $E$ mean “at least two components work.” State $E^c$ exactly. Can you compute $P(E)$ merely from the three individual working probabilities?

$E^c$ means “at most one component works”: either zero or exactly one. Individual working probabilities alone generally do not determine the answer, because components may fail together. With independent components, we can calculate the zero-work and one-work cases and subtract their sum from one. The set identity is unconditional; the numerical calculation needs a joint model.
