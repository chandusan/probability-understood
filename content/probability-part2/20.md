# Translate the Olympic-city question into events

Two cities are competing to reach a five-city finalist list. The question asks for the chance that city A or city B appears on that list. The crucial first step is to distinguish one complete outcome, the finalist list, from events describing properties of that outcome.

## What does one outcome look like?

A complete outcome is a set of five selected cities. There are many competing cities, and the five positions are not described as ranked. If both A and B are selected, the list still has three places for other cities.

Define

$$A=\{\text{finalist lists containing city A}\},$$

$$B=\{\text{finalist lists containing city B}\}.$$

The letters A and B are doing two jobs in the story: they label cities and also label events. In probability expressions such as $P(A)$, A means the **event that the city is selected**, not the city itself.

## Translate each supplied statement

The question gives

$$P(A)=0.20,\qquad P(B)=0.35,\qquad P(A\cap B)=0.08.$$

“Both” means intersection. “A or B” means union, including finalist lists containing both cities. Therefore the requested quantity is $P(A\cup B)$.

Nothing states that all cities have equal selection chances or that all five-city subsets are equally likely. In fact, the different probabilities for A and B already rule out a model that treats every city symmetrically. We should use the supplied probabilities rather than invent a uniform selection mechanism.

The total number of competing cities is unnecessary for this calculation. It would matter if we were asked to derive the input probabilities from a particular selection process.

## Build the disjoint regions before calculating the union

The 20% chance for A includes the 8% chance that B also appears. Therefore the probability that **only A of these two cities** is selected is

$$0.20-0.08=0.12.$$

Similarly, only B has probability

$$0.35-0.08=0.27.$$

“Only A” does not mean A is the sole finalist; there are always five finalists. It means A is selected and B is not. The remaining finalist places go to other cities.

We now have three mutually exclusive ways the requested event can happen: A without B, B without A, or both. Their probabilities are 0.12, 0.27, and 0.08. Adding them gives the desired 0.47.

## Why the overlap information matters

If we knew only 0.20 and 0.35, the union would not be determined. The overlap could range from 0 to 0.20, allowing union probabilities from 0.35 to 0.55.

An overlap of zero means the two events never happen together. An overlap of 0.20 means every finalist list containing A also contains B. The supplied 0.08 locates this problem between those extremes and yields a unique answer.

## Do not replace the given overlap with an independence assumption

Independence would imply $P(A\cap B)=0.20\cdot0.35=0.07$. The actual supplied probability is 0.08. These are different, so the events in this problem are not independent.

The fact that there are multiple finalist places allows both cities to be selected. It does not make their selections independent. Shared criteria or the selection mechanism can connect their chances.

## Check your understanding

Which event corresponds to “one or both cities reaches the final five”? Which corresponds to “one city reaches the final five, but the other does not”?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>State the events and their probabilities</summary>

“One or both” is $A\cup B$, with probability $0.12+0.27+0.08=0.47$. “One but not the other” is $(A\cap B^c)\cup(A^c\cap B)$, with probability $0.12+0.27=0.39$. The difference, 0.08, is exactly the both-selected region.
