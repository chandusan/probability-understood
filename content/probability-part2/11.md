# The birthday problem asks whether any pair matches

In a room of $n$ people, a shared birthday becomes likely much sooner than most people expect. The key is that any pair may match; nobody's birthday is singled out. The event can happen through many overlapping arrangements, so we begin by counting the simpler event that all birthdays are different.

## State the idealized birthday model

Assume there are 365 possible month-and-day birthdays. Ignore leap day, give every day probability $1/365$, and assume the people's birthdays are independent. Birth years do not matter. People remain distinguishable even when their birthdays coincide.

An outcome is an ordered assignment

$$\omega=(d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n),\qquad d_i\in\{1,\ldots,365\},$$

where the $i$th entry belongs to person $i$. Each assignment has probability $1/365^n$, and there are

$$|S|=365^n$$

possible assignments. The exponent appears because every person has 365 choices, including dates already assigned to others.

This model simplifies real birthdays, which can vary by season and can be dependent in a selected group, such as a room containing twins. The calculation is exact for the stated model.

## Define the event without narrowing it

Let

$$E=\{\text{at least two people have the same birthday}\}.$$

Three people sharing a date qualifies. So do two separate matching pairs. The event does not require exactly one pair, and it does not require anyone to match a particular person's birthday.

Its complement is

$$E^c=\{\text{all $n$ birthdays are distinct}\}.$$

Therefore

$$P(E)=1-P(E^c).$$

The complement has one unambiguous construction: keep assigning unused dates. That is why it is easier than sorting through every possible arrangement of collisions.

## Count the no-match assignments

For $n\le365$, the first person has 365 permissible dates. The second has 364 if a match is to be avoided. Given distinct birthdays so far, the third has 363, and person $n$ has $365-n+1$.

Thus

$$|E^c|=365\cdot364\cdots(365-n+1),$$

and

$$
\boxed{P(E)=1-\frac{365\cdot364\cdots(365-n+1)}{365^n}.}
$$

For three people this gives

$$P(E)=1-\frac{365\cdot364\cdot363}{365^3}\approx0.008204.$$

With 366 people, a shared birthday is certain: there are more people than possible dates. For zero or one person, a matching pair is impossible.

## Connect birthdays to bins

This is an occupancy problem. Each person is a labeled ball, each date is a bin, and a shared birthday is a bin receiving at least two people. The six-district exercise had six balls and six bins; the birthday problem has $n$ balls and 365 bins.

Seeing the common structure helps in other settings. Assigning customers to servers, placing records into hash buckets, and generating identifiers from a finite set all create analogous collision questions when the assignments are independent and uniform.

## Why adding pair probabilities is not exact

There are $\binom n2$ pairs, each with match probability $1/365$. Adding those probabilities counts the expected number of matching pairs. It does not directly give the probability that at least one pair matches, because a single room can contain several matching pairs.

For instance, three people sharing one date produce three matching pairs: $(1,2)$, $(1,3)$, and $(2,3)$. The room should count once toward the event “there is a match,” but a sum over pairs counts it three times. The complement calculation avoids that overcounting.

## Check your understanding

In a room of 23 people, suppose your birthday is fixed. Is “someone else shares my birthday” the same event as the standard birthday problem?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

No. The standard event allows a match between any two of the 23 people. Matching your date restricts attention to the 22 pairs involving you. Its probability is $1-(364/365)^{22}\approx0.05857$, about 5.86%. The probability of any shared birthday in the room is about 50.73%.
