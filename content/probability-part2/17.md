# Add the events, then remove the duplicate overlap

The probability that A or B happens includes outcomes where both happen. Adding their individual probabilities counts that shared region twice. The addition rule subtracts one copy of the overlap so every successful outcome is counted exactly once.

## Read the rule as bookkeeping

For any two events in the same probability space,

$$\boxed{P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).}$$

The union $A\cup B$ means **at least one** event occurs. This is inclusive “or”: A alone, B alone, or both. The intersection $A\cap B$ means both occur.

An outcome belonging only to A is counted once in $P(A)+P(B)$. An outcome belonging only to B is also counted once. An outcome belonging to both is counted twice, so subtracting the intersection leaves one copy.

The rule does not need equally likely outcomes, independence, or a finite sample space. It follows from the probability axioms, as the next two slides show.

## Work an example with visible overlap

Roll a fair six-sided die. Let $A=\{2,4,6\}$ be “even” and $B=\{4,5,6\}$ be “at least four.” Then

$$P(A)=\frac36,\qquad P(B)=\frac36,\qquad P(A\cap B)=\frac26.$$

Therefore,

$$P(A\cup B)=\frac36+\frac36-\frac26=\frac46.$$

Indeed the successful outcomes are $\{2,4,5,6\}$. The incorrect sum $3/6+3/6=1$ would count 4 and 6 twice while ignoring the unsuccessful outcomes 1 and 3.

## Disjoint events give the simpler formula

If $A\cap B=\varnothing$, both cannot happen on the same trial. Since $P(\varnothing)=0$,

$$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B).$$

For example, a single die result cannot be both 1 and 6. Those events are disjoint. In contrast, “even” and “at least four” can both happen, so their overlap must be subtracted.

Strictly, the numerical simplification only needs $P(A\cap B)=0$. In a continuous probability model an intersection can be nonempty and still have probability zero. Disjointness is a sufficient condition with a simple set interpretation.

## Disjoint is different from independent

Independent events satisfy $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$. Two fair coin tosses give a familiar example: “first toss is heads” and “second toss is heads” are independent, but they overlap at HH. Their union has probability $1/2+1/2-1/4=3/4$.

Two disjoint events with positive probabilities cannot be independent. Their intersection probability is zero, whereas the product of their positive probabilities is positive. Independence concerns whether learning one event changes the chance of another; disjointness concerns whether they can happen together.

## Check your understanding

If $P(A)=0.6$, $P(B)=0.5$, and $P(A\cap B)=0.2$, what are the probabilities of at least one event, both events, and exactly one event?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Separate three different questions</summary>

At least one has probability $0.6+0.5-0.2=0.9$. Both has probability $0.2$. Exactly one removes the both-region from the union, giving $0.9-0.2=0.7$, or directly $0.6+0.5-2(0.2)=0.7$.
