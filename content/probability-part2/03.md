# Count eliminations, then use symmetry

The tournament solution combines a deterministic fact with a probabilistic symmetry. Every completed tournament contains $n-1$ matches, whatever the bracket or results. Before play begins, every unordered pair of players has the same chance to account for one of those matches. Together these facts force the answer $2/n$.

## Why the number of matches is exactly $n-1$

Initially there are $n$ active players. A match reduces that number by exactly one; a bye leaves it unchanged. The tournament ends when the number reaches one. Therefore exactly $n-1$ matches must have occurred.

This argument does not require $n$ to be a power of two. For seven players, the successive round sizes are $7,4,2,1$, giving $3+2+1=6$ matches. For eight players, they are $8,4,2,1$, giving $4+2+1=7$. The elimination count unifies both cases.

## Make the symmetry argument precise

For each pair of distinct players $i,j$, define the indicator

$$
I_{ij}=\begin{cases}
1,&\text{if players $i$ and $j$ ever meet},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

An indicator records whether an event happened. Its average value over repeated tournaments is the probability of that event:

$$E[I_{ij}]=P(I_{ij}=1).$$

No pair can meet twice, because its first match eliminates one member. Consequently, in **every completed tournament**,

$$\sum_{i<j} I_{ij}=n-1.$$

Taking averages preserves the sum. Uniform pairings, uniform byes, and equal ability make all pair probabilities equal to a common value $p$. There are $\binom n2$ terms, hence

$$
\binom n2 p=n-1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{p=\frac2n.}
$$

This is an early application of **linearity of expectation**: the average of a sum equals the sum of the averages. The meeting events do not have to be independent. Indeed, they are strongly related through elimination.

## How this relates to the slide's proof

The slide imagines a match specified in advance and uses symmetry to assign each player pair probability $1/\binom n2$ of occupying it. Adding across the $n-1$ match positions works because $A$ and $B$ cannot occupy two positions.

For that wording to be precise, the match positions must be indexed in a way that does not privilege a player label—for example, using randomly assigned positions within each round. Calling a position “$A$'s first match” would destroy the uniform-pair argument. The indicator proof avoids this bookkeeping by counting pair appearances directly.

## What if the players have unequal ability?

The total number of matches remains $n-1$, but a specified pair need not have probability $2/n$ of meeting. Consider four players, where $A$ and $B$ always defeat the other two players. If paired immediately, they meet; if separated, both reach the final and meet there. Their meeting probability is 1, rather than $1/2$.

However, averaging the meeting probability over all $\binom n2$ pairs still gives $2/n$. There are always $n-1$ realized pairs; unequal skill changes which pairs are more likely to be among them. This separates the structural counting fact from the equal-ability assumption needed for a particular pair.

## A useful consequence

Let $D_A$ be the number of opponents faced by player $A$. Under the symmetric model,

$$
E[D_A]=\sum_{B\ne A}P(E_{AB})
=(n-1)\frac2n=2-\frac2n.
$$

For a large tournament, a typical entrant plays about two matches on average, even though the eventual champion may play many rounds. Most entrants are eliminated early. The same result follows by sharing the $2(n-1)$ total player-appearances equally among $n$ players.
