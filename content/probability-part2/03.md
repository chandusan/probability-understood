# Count valid draws, then add across rounds

We can derive the probability that two specified players ever meet using favorable outcomes divided by total outcomes. Count valid pairings within each round, count which players can reach that round, and then add the disjoint opportunities for the pair to meet. Every step has an explicit sample space.

## Start with the familiar uniform-group calculation

Suppose there are M objects, and a group of exactly k objects is selected **uniformly from all k-element subsets**. Fix one distinguished object. There are $\binom Mk$ possible groups. A favorable group includes the distinguished object and chooses its other $k-1$ members from the remaining $M-1$ objects. Therefore

$$
P(\text{distinguished object is selected})
=\frac{\binom{M-1}{k-1}}{\binom Mk}
=\frac{k}{M}.
$$

For the tournament, there are $M=\binom n2$ possible unordered player pairs, and a completed tournament contains $k=n-1$ matches. **If** its realized pairs were a uniformly selected k-element subset of those M pairs, the calculation would give

$$
\frac{\binom{\binom n2-1}{n-2}}
{\binom{\binom n2}{n-1}}
=\frac{n-1}{\binom n2}
=\frac2n.
$$

That is a correct calculation for the stated uniform-subset model. To establish the tournament result, we must account for the restrictions imposed by elimination. For example, the three-pair group $\{AB,AC,BC\}$ cannot be a completed four-player tournament: it contains a triangle among three players and leaves the fourth player out. In a single round, even $AB$ and $AC$ cannot both occur, because A cannot play twice in that round.

We therefore count the tournament's valid outcomes below. The final probability will have the same simple form.

## Count all valid draws in one round

Suppose a round begins with $r\ge2$ players, and let $k=\lfloor r/2\rfloor$ be its number of matches. Pairings are uniform; when r is odd, the bye recipient is uniform too.

Arrange the r players in a row and pair adjacent positions: positions 1 and 2, then 3 and 4, and so on. If r is odd, the last player receives the bye. The $r!$ arrangements count each draw repeatedly:

- Swapping the two players inside any match does not change the draw, giving a factor of $2^k$.
- Reordering the k matches does not change the draw, giving a factor of $k!$.

The number of distinct valid draws is consequently

$$
D(r)=\frac{r!}{2^k k!}.
$$

For odd r, the unpaired last player already specifies the bye; no additional factor is needed. For example, five players have $5!/(2^2\cdot2!)=15$ draws: five choices of bye and three pairings of the remaining four players.

## Count the favorable draws containing A against B

Suppose A and B are among these r players. Fix their match and arrange the remaining $r-2$ players into $k-1$ matches, with a bye if r is odd. The favorable count is

$$
F(r)=\frac{(r-2)!}{2^{k-1}(k-1)!}.
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{A meets B this round}\mid\text{both are present})
&=\frac{F(r)}{D(r)}\\
&=\frac{(r-2)!}{2^{k-1}(k-1)!}
\frac{2^k k!}{r!}\\
&=\frac{2k}{r(r-1)}
=\frac{k}{\binom r2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For even r this is $1/(r-1)$. For odd r it is $1/r$, including the possibility that A or B receives the bye. With all n original players present in round 1, its meeting probability is directly $\lfloor n/2\rfloor/\binom n2$.

## Count the survivor groups for a later round

Now keep A and B fixed among the **original n players**. If a later round starts with r players, they must both belong to its survivor group before the preceding draw calculation applies.

Under uniform draws, uniform byes, and independent fair match outcomes, all r-element survivor subsets are equally likely. One way to see this is to relabel an entire tournament history. Any bijection carrying one survivor subset to another also carries every compatible history to a unique, equally likely history for the other subset. The round sizes are fixed by repeatedly replacing a size s with $\lceil s/2\rceil$, so corresponding histories contain the same numbers of draws and fair match results.

There are $\binom nr$ survivor groups in total. A favorable group contains A and B and chooses its other $r-2$ members from the remaining $n-2$ players. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{both reach this round})
&=\frac{\binom{n-2}{r-2}}{\binom nr}\\
&=\frac{r(r-1)}{n(n-1)}
=\frac{\binom r2}{\binom n2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a genuine uniform-subset calculation: its objects are the surviving players.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Verify uniform survivor groups by direct first-round counting</summary>

Let $m=\lceil n/2\rceil$ be the number of first-round survivors. Fix any particular m-element survivor group.

If n is even, match each of the m survivors to a different loser. There are $m!$ such bijections, and the required winner of each match is specified by the survivor group.

If n is odd, choose the bye recipient from the m survivors, then match the other $m-1$ survivors to the $m-1$ losers. There are $m(m-1)!=m!$ compatible outcomes again.

Every complete first-round draw together with its winners is equally likely: each draw has probability $1/D(n)$, and each of its $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ winner combinations has the same probability. Each survivor group has the same favorable count $m!$, confirming uniformity directly.

## Multiply the two favorable-count ratios

Let $E_t$ mean that the original pair A,B meets in round t. Write r for that round's number of players and $k=\lfloor r/2\rfloor$ for its number of matches. The pair must both arrive and then be matched in the fresh draw:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(E_t)
&=P(\text{both arrive})
P(E_t\mid\text{both arrive})\\
&=\frac{\binom r2}{\binom n2}
\frac{k}{\binom r2}\\
&=\boxed{\frac{k}{\binom n2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The chance of both surviving becomes smaller in later rounds, while their conditional chance of being paired becomes larger. The cancellation leaves the round's number of matches divided by the number of original pairs.

## Add across rounds to obtain the final answer

The events $E_1,E_2,\ldots$ are disjoint: once A and B meet, one is eliminated, so they cannot meet again. Therefore their probabilities add directly.

A match eliminates exactly one player; a bye eliminates nobody. Going from n players to one champion requires exactly $n-1$ matches. If round t has $k_t$ matches, then $\sum_t k_t=n-1$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{A and B ever meet})
&=\sum_t P(E_t)\\
&=\frac{\sum_t k_t}{\binom n2}\\
&=\boxed{\frac{n-1}{\binom n2}=\frac2n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For five players, the round sizes are 5, 3, and 2. All three meeting probabilities use the original denominator $\binom52=10$:

| Round | Players remaining | Matches | Probability A,B meet in this round |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 5 | 2 | $2/10$ |
| 2 | 3 | 1 | $1/10$ |
| 3 | 2 | 1 | $1/10$ |

Adding gives $4/10=2/5$. The second-round conditional probability of meeting **given both survive** is $1/3$; the unconditional second-round probability in the table also accounts for their chance of reaching it together.

## Why the fairness assumption matters

The fixed total of $n-1$ matches alone does not determine a particular pair's probability. Consider three players, where A and B both always defeat C. If A and B are paired first, they meet immediately. If either faces C first, that player wins and then meets the other in the final. Thus A and B meet with probability one, rather than $2/3$.

There are still exactly two matches, but the survivor subsets are no longer equally likely. The counting ratios above depend on the symmetric fair model as well as on the tournament's elimination rules.

## Check your understanding

Why can we add the probabilities that A and B meet in different rounds? Would the same justification let us add “A meets B” and “A meets C” to find the probability of either event?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Check whether the events can occur together</summary>

A and B cannot meet in two different rounds, because their first encounter eliminates one of them. Their round-specific events are disjoint. However, A can defeat B and later face C, so “A meets B” and “A meets C” can overlap. Computing that union by addition would require subtracting their intersection. Disjointness belongs to the specified pair's different meeting times, not to arbitrary pairs of players.

</details>
