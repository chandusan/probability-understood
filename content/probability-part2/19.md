# Finish the addition-rule proof

The union has already been partitioned into only A, only B, and both. To express its probability using the original events, split A and B around their common intersection. The algebra then records exactly how many copies of the overlap remain.

## Find the probability of “only A”

Every outcome in A either belongs to B or does not. Consequently,

$$A=(A\cap B^c)\cup(A\cap B).$$

The two pieces are disjoint, so additivity gives

$$P(A)=P(A\cap B^c)+P(A\cap B).$$

Rearranging,

$$P(A\cap B^c)=P(A)-P(A\cap B).$$

This subtraction is valid because $A\cap B\subseteq A$: we are removing a region already contained inside A. It is not a general rule that an arbitrary intersection equals a difference of probabilities.

Similarly,

$$P(A^c\cap B)=P(B)-P(A\cap B).$$

## Substitute into the disjoint-region sum

From the preceding slide,

$$\begin{aligned}
P(A\cup B)
&=P(A\cap B^c)+P(A^c\cap B)+P(A\cap B)\\
&=[P(A)-P(A\cap B)]\\
&\quad+[P(B)-P(A\cap B)]+P(A\cap B)\\
&=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).
\end{aligned}$$

There are two negative copies of the intersection and one positive copy, leaving one negative copy overall. The positive copy was required because the union includes outcomes where both events happen.

No step used a formula of the form favorable outcomes divided by total outcomes. This matters because the proof also covers weighted finite models and continuous models. Once A and B are events in a probability space, the same bookkeeping applies.

## A pointwise identity explains the same bookkeeping

Let $\mathbf1_A(\omega)$ be 1 when outcome $\omega$ lies in A and 0 otherwise. Then, for every possible outcome,

$$\mathbf1_{A\cup B}=\mathbf1_A+\mathbf1_B-\mathbf1_{A\cap B}.$$

For an outcome in neither event the right side is 0. In exactly one event it is 1. In both events it is $1+1-1=1$. Averaging this identity over the probability model produces the addition rule. This previews indicator variables, a useful way to turn set membership into arithmetic.

## Use the formula to check whether proposed numbers are possible

Because an intersection is contained in both events,

$$P(A\cap B)\leq\min(P(A),P(B)).$$

Because the union cannot exceed 1, the addition rule also implies

$$P(A\cap B)\geq P(A)+P(B)-1.$$

Together with nonnegativity, the possible overlap must satisfy

$$\max(0,P(A)+P(B)-1)\leq P(A\cap B)\leq\min(P(A),P(B)).$$

For example, if $P(A)=0.7$ and $P(B)=0.6$, their overlap must be at least 0.3. They cannot be disjoint: two disjoint regions with those probabilities would require total probability 1.3.

## Exactly one needs a different subtraction

The event “exactly one of A and B” consists only of the first two regions. Hence

$$P(\text{exactly one})=P(A)+P(B)-2P(A\cap B).$$

The overlap is removed twice because both original event probabilities included it and we now want no copy remaining. Keeping track of the desired event prevents a common confusion between inclusive and exclusive “or.”

## Check your understanding

If $P(A)=0.7$, $P(B)=0.6$, and $P(A\cup B)=0.9$, find the four disjoint region probabilities.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Recover the complete probability table</summary>

The intersection is $0.7+0.6-0.9=0.4$. Only A is $0.7-0.4=0.3$. Only B is $0.6-0.4=0.2$. Neither is $1-0.9=0.1$. All four values are nonnegative and sum to one, providing an internal check.
