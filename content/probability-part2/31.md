# At least one six: solve the same event two ways

With three independent fair dice, a six can occur on the first, second, or third die, with overlaps when several dice show six. Inclusion–exclusion handles those overlaps directly. Counting the complement gives a shorter route and a valuable independent check.

## Specify the equally likely outcomes

Label the dice 1, 2, and 3, even if they are rolled simultaneously. An elementary outcome is an ordered triple such as $(6,2,6)$. Each coordinate has six possibilities, so there are

$$
6^3=216
$$

equally likely outcomes. Fairness makes each face equiprobable on each die; independence makes every ordered triple have probability $(1/6)^3$.

Let A mean that die 1 shows six, B that die 2 shows six, and C that die 3 shows six. Our target is $P(A\cup B\cup C)$. The word “or” is inclusive: $(6,6,2)$ and $(6,6,6)$ both count as successes.

## Route 1: count the overlapping events

For A, the first coordinate is fixed at six and the other two coordinates remain free. Therefore

$$
|A|=6^2=36,\qquad P(A)=\frac{36}{216}=\frac16.
$$

The same holds for B and C. For $A\cap B$, the first two coordinates are fixed and only the third is free:

$$
|A\cap B|=6,\qquad P(A\cap B)=\frac{6}{216}=\frac1{36}.
$$

Every pair intersection has the same count. The triple intersection contains only $(6,6,6)$, so its probability is $1/216$. Inclusion–exclusion now gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A\cup B\cup C)
&=3\left(\frac16\right)-3\left(\frac1{36}\right)+\frac1{216}\\
&=\frac{108-18+1}{216}
=\frac{91}{216}\\
&\approx0.4212963.
\end{aligned}
$$

Why isn't the answer $3/6$? That sum counts outcomes with two sixes twice and the all-six outcome three times. The overlap corrections are essential.

## Route 2: exclude outcomes with no six

The complement of “at least one six” is “no six on any die.” Each die then has five allowed faces, giving $5^3=125$ outcomes. Thus

$$
P(\text{at least one six})
=1-P(\text{no six})
=1-\left(\frac56\right)^3
=\frac{216-125}{216}
=\frac{91}{216}.
$$

The complement describes one clean restriction on every coordinate, so it is especially easy to count. This is a recurring problem-solving choice: after identifying an event, compare the complexity of counting it and counting its complement.

The product $(5/6)^3$ uses independence. De Morgan's identity defining the complement is always valid; multiplying the three no-six probabilities requires the model assumption.

## A third check: partition by the number of sixes

We can divide success into exactly one, exactly two, and exactly three sixes. These categories are disjoint:

| Number of sixes | Choose their positions | Fill remaining positions | Outcome count |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $\binom31=3$ | $5^2=25$ | 75 |
| 2 | $\binom32=3$ | $5$ | 15 |
| 3 | $\binom33=1$ | 1 | 1 |

The total is $75+15+1=91$. Notice that **exactly one** six has probability $75/216$, while **at least one** has probability $91/216$. Those phrases define different events.

## Generalize before memorizing

For n independent fair dice,

$$
P(\text{at least one six})=1-\left(\frac56\right)^n.
$$

More generally, for n independent trials with success probability p on each trial, the probability of at least one success is $1-(1-p)^n$.

## Check your understanding

How many independent fair dice must be rolled for the probability of at least one six to exceed one half?

We need $(5/6)^n<1/2$, or $n>\log(1/2)/\log(5/6)\approx3.802$. The smallest integer is four. Three dice give about $0.4213$, while four give $1-625/1296=671/1296\approx0.5177$.
