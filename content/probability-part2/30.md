# Prove inclusion–exclusion by tracking each region

The proof is an accounting check: each disjoint region inside the union must finish with coefficient one. The seven-region picture shows exactly why adding singles and subtracting pairs needs one final addition of the triple intersection.

## Correct the source's union list

The first line of the original slide repeats $E_5$ and omits $E_6$. The intended statement is

$$
A\cup B\cup C=E_1\cup E_2\cup E_3\cup E_4\cup E_5\cup E_6\cup E_7.
$$

This is a small transcription error, but it matters: $E_6$ is the part of B outside both other circles. The explanatory reasoning on the rest of the slide uses the correct seven-region decomposition.

Write $p_i=P(E_i)$. Because the regions are disjoint, the probability we want is $p_1+\cdots+p_7$.

## Step 1: add the three individual probabilities

From the diagram,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A)&=p_1+p_2+p_4+p_5,\\
P(B)&=p_1+p_2+p_3+p_6,\\
P(C)&=p_1+p_3+p_4+p_7.
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding gives

$$
P(A)+P(B)+P(C)
=3p_1+2p_2+2p_3+2p_4+p_5+p_6+p_7.
$$

The single-only regions already have the desired coefficient one. The pair-only regions have coefficient two. The central region has coefficient three. We need to remove exactly the excess contributions.

## Step 2: subtract the three pair intersections

The pair intersections have probabilities

$$
P(A\cap B)=p_1+p_2,\qquad
P(A\cap C)=p_1+p_4,\qquad
P(B\cap C)=p_1+p_3.
$$

Their sum is $3p_1+p_2+p_3+p_4$. Subtracting it from the single-event sum leaves

$$
p_2+p_3+p_4+p_5+p_6+p_7.
$$

All pair-only regions are now correct. But the central region has disappeared: its coefficient changed from three to zero. This is why stopping after the pair subtraction underestimates the union whenever the triple intersection has positive probability.

Notice that no single-only region changes in this step. Such an outcome belongs to no pair intersection, so there is nothing to subtract from its contribution.

## Step 3: restore the center once

Since $A\cap B\cap C=E_1$, adding $P(A\cap B\cap C)=p_1$ gives precisely

$$
p_1+p_2+p_3+p_4+p_5+p_6+p_7=P(A\cup B\cup C).
$$

This proves the formula. The proof used only disjoint additivity and event membership. It applies to finite, countable, and continuous probability models alike; it does not require equally likely outcomes or independent events.

## A second proof builds on the two-event rule

Treat $A\cup B$ as one event and combine it with C:

$$
P((A\cup B)\cup C)=P(A\cup B)+P(C)-P((A\cup B)\cap C).
$$

Distributivity says

$$
(A\cup B)\cap C=(A\cap C)\cup(B\cap C).
$$

The intersection of these last two events is $A\cap B\cap C$. Apply the two-event formula to each union:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A\cup B)&=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B),\\
P((A\cup B)\cap C)
&=P(A\cap C)+P(B\cap C)-P(A\cap B\cap C).
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting explains the final positive sign algebraically: the negative triple term inside the subtracted expression becomes positive. The region proof explains what that sign accomplishes.

## Check the formula in an extreme case

Suppose $A=B=C$. What should the union probability be, and what happens if we omit the triple term?

The union is just A, so its probability is $P(A)$. The correct expression is $3P(A)-3P(A)+P(A)=P(A)$. Omitting the last term would give zero, even if A were certain.
