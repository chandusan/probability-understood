# Turn four bridge questions into two events

The four questions on the slide form one connected problem. First count hands with exactly five spades or exactly five hearts separately, then count their overlap, and finally combine them with the addition rule. Clear event definitions do most of the organizing work.

## Define suit counts and events

Let $X_S$ be the number of spades in the 13-card hand and $X_H$ the number of hearts. Define

$$A=\{X_S=5\},\qquad B=\{X_H=5\}.$$

The word **exactly** matters. A hand with six spades does not belong to A, even though five of its cards can be identified as spades. Membership depends on the total suit count in the entire hand.

The four questions now become:

| Question | Event | What the hand must contain |
|---|---|---|
| Exactly five spades | A | 5 spades and 8 non-spades |
| Exactly five hearts | B | 5 hearts and 8 non-hearts |
| Exactly five of each | $A\cap B$ | 5 spades, 5 hearts, 3 other cards |
| Exactly five spades or exactly five hearts | $A\cup B$ | A, B, or both |

Each row refers to the same uniform sample space of $\binom{52}{13}$ hands.

## Count by dividing the deck into categories

For A, partition the deck into 13 spades and 39 non-spades. Choose five from the first group and eight from the second:

$$|A|=\binom{13}{5}\binom{39}{8}.$$

For B, the same reasoning applies with hearts in place of spades. For the overlap, partition into three disjoint groups: 13 spades, 13 hearts, and 26 diamonds or clubs. Then

$$|A\cap B|=\binom{13}{5}\binom{13}{5}\binom{26}{3}.$$

The products count choices from disjoint card categories. They are counting arguments, not claims that suit-count events are probabilistically independent.

## Test the event definitions on concrete hands

A hand with suit counts $(5,4,2,2)$ in the order spades, hearts, diamonds, clubs is in A but not B. A hand with counts $(3,5,3,2)$ is in B but not A. A hand with counts $(5,5,2,1)$ is in both.

A hand with counts $(6,4,2,1)$ is in neither, despite containing at least five spades. This last example is a useful guard against silently changing “exactly five” into “five or more.”

The overlap is possible because $5+5\leq13$. We therefore cannot add $P(A)$ and $P(B)$ without a correction. If both events instead required exactly seven cards of their respective suits, their intersection would be impossible because $7+7>13$.

## Interpret “a five-card major” with care

Spades and hearts are the major suits. The slide's displayed equations ask about at least one major suit whose length is **exactly five**. In other contexts a phrase such as “a five-card major” may describe a minimum of five cards. These are different mathematical events.

For this sequence, retain $X_S=5$ and $X_H=5$. The final bridge slide explains how the calculation changes if the intended condition is $X_S\geq5$ or $X_H\geq5$.
