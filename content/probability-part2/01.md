# Turn probability rules into a problem-solving method

Counting gives us a way to describe possible outcomes. Probability rules tell us how to combine those outcomes into answers. This part of the course develops the habits behind that transition: choose a model, define the event precisely, look for symmetry or a useful complement, and account for overlaps before adding probabilities.

## State the problem before choosing a method

The original slide is the chapter title, “Rules of Probability.” To introduce the methods we will use, here is the full setup of the robbery exercise from [slide 7](07.html), with its probability assumptions made explicit.

**Problem.** A city has six districts, and six robberies occur during a particular week. For this exercise, assume that each robbery is equally likely to occur in any of the six districts, independently of where the other robberies occur. **What is the probability that at least one district has two or more robberies?**

These are assumptions of the exercise, not claims about crime in a real city. Equal likelihood gives each district probability $1/6$ for each robbery. Independence means that knowing one robbery's district does not change the probabilities for another robbery.

To count outcomes, label the robberies $1,\ldots,6$, for example by the order in which they occurred, and label the districts $1,\ldots,6$. One outcome is a list $(d_1,\ldots,d_6)$ recording the district of each robbery. For example, $(2,2,5,1,6,3)$ puts robberies 1 and 2 in District 2, so it satisfies the event in the question.

There are six choices for each of the six entries. Thus the number of outcomes is

$$6^6=46{,}656.$$

Every particular list has probability $(1/6)^6$ under the stated assumptions. That is why these lists can be counted as equally likely outcomes.

Now look at the **complement**: no district has two robberies. With six robberies and six districts, this means exactly one robbery in each district. There are six choices for robbery 1's district, five unused choices for robbery 2, and so on, giving $6!=720$ lists. The probability asked for is therefore

$$
1-\frac{720}{46{,}656}
=\frac{319}{324}
\approx98.46\%.
$$

Why did we keep track of individual robberies? A list of **district totals** records the number of robberies in District 1, then District 2, and so on. The totals $(1,1,1,1,1,1)$ combine all 720 ways to put one robbery in each district. The totals $(6,0,0,0,0,0)$ describe only one outcome: all six robberies in District 1. These two lists of totals therefore have different probabilities; counting each as one equally likely possibility would change the model.

The method is now visible: state the question, specify the random model, identify equally likely outcomes, and count the easier complementary event. [Slide 7](07.html) develops this example further. The same distinction between individual outcomes and grouped totals will recur in lotteries, birthdays, and balls placed in bins.

## Translate ordinary language into events

Let $A$ and $B$ be events in a sample space $S$. The key translations are:

| Everyday wording | Event notation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Not $A$ | $A^c$ | All outcomes outside $A$ |
| $A$ or $B$ | $A\cup B$ | At least one occurs, including both |
| $A$ and $B$ | $A\cap B$ | Both occur |
| $A$ but not $B$ | $A\cap B^c$ | $A$ occurs while $B$ does not |
| Exactly one of $A,B$ | $(A\cap B^c)\cup(A^c\cap B)$ | One occurs and the other does not |

“Or” is inclusive unless the question says otherwise. “At least one pair shares a birthday” includes three people sharing a birthday and several separate matching pairs. These are exactly the cases that make direct counting awkward.

## Use the structure of the event

Three rules will do much of the work:

$$
P(A^c)=1-P(A),
$$

$$
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B),
$$

and, for disjoint events,

$$
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B).
$$

The complement rule is useful when success has many forms but failure has one clean description. The union rule repairs the double counting of outcomes in both events. Symmetry helps when changing labels leaves the experiment unchanged, as in an equal-ability tennis tournament.

Notice what is absent from this list: an unconditional rule saying “and means multiply.” The identity $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$ requires independence. Counting choices with the multiplication principle and multiplying probabilities of independent events are related ideas, but they have different assumptions.

## A small example using all three habits

Roll a fair six-sided die. Let $A=\{2,4,6\}$ be “even” and $B=\{5,6\}$ be “above 4.” Since the outcomes are equally likely,

$$
P(A\cup B)=\frac36+\frac26-\frac16=\frac46.
$$

The overlap is the outcome 6. The complement is $\{1,3\}$, so the same answer is $1-2/6$. Two methods agree because they describe the same event using different pieces.

## Check your understanding

For this die, what is the probability that exactly one of “even” and “above 4” occurs? Why is it different from the probability of their union?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

Exactly one occurs for $\{2,4,5\}$, giving $3/6=1/2$. The union also includes 6, where both statements are true. “Exactly one” removes the intersection entirely; the union retains it once.
