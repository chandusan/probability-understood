# Model twenty surgery outcomes with explicit assumptions

In the source example, each operation has an 85% success probability. Suppose twenty operations have independent outcomes and share that same probability. Let $X$ count successful operations. Under those model assumptions, $X\sim\operatorname{Binom}(20,0.85)$.

## Translate the model into a probability formula

For exactly $x$ successful operations,

$$\boxed{P(X=x)=\binom{20}{x}(0.85)^x(0.15)^{20-x},\qquad x=0,\ldots,20.}$$

One specified pattern containing $x$ successes has probability $0.85^x0.15^{20-x}$. The binomial coefficient counts the ways to select which operations succeeded.

For example, exactly sixteen successes requires four failures, so

$$P(X=16)=\binom{20}{16}(0.85)^{16}(0.15)^4\approx0.182122.$$

This is the probability of **exactly** sixteen, not of sixteen or more.

## What the assumptions mean

The number 85% alone does not establish a binomial model. Independence and a common probability across the twenty operations are additional assumptions in this classroom example. If individual success probabilities differ, or outcomes share a common source of dependence, this exact PMF need not apply.

The purpose here is to understand a specified model, not to infer a clinical probability from real patient information.

## Locate the center before calculating a tail

The expected number of successes is

$$E[X]=20(0.85)=17.$$

Its variance is $20(0.85)(0.15)=2.55$, and its standard deviation is about $1.597$ operations. A threshold of sixteen successes lies slightly below the mean, so a probability above one half for “at least sixteen” is plausible.

A calculator's binomial PMF evaluates one exact-count term; a cumulative calculation adds terms over the desired range. Keeping that distinction clear prevents replacing a tail probability by a single bar.
