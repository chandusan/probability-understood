# Recognize when a count is binomial

We write $X\sim\operatorname{Binom}(n,p)$ when $X$ counts successes in $n$ independent trials, each with success probability $p$. The notation summarizes an experiment and its assumptions, not just the shape of a formula.

## What each parameter tells us

The parameter $n$ is the fixed number of trials. It is a nonnegative integer and sets the largest possible count. The parameter $p\in[0,1]$ is the probability of success on each individual trial.

The probability mass function is

$$P(X=x)=\binom nxp^x(1-p)^{n-x},\qquad x=0,1,\ldots,n.$$

To calculate a probability, first translate the words into a set of allowed counts:

| Wording | Event | Calculation |
|---|---|---|
| Exactly $k$ | $X=k$ | One PMF term |
| At most $k$ | $X\le k$ | Sum from $0$ through $k$ |
| At least $k$ | $X\ge k$ | $1-P(X\le k-1)$ |
| More than $k$ | $X>k$ | $1-P(X\le k)$ |

The difference between $k$ and $k-1$ matters because a binomial variable assigns positive probability to individual integers.

## Check the model before calculating

Independent tosses of a fixed biased coin fit. Sampling items with replacement from a population with a fixed fraction of marked objects also fits, if each draw is uniform and independent.

Drawing without replacement from a small finite population does not generally fit: removing an item changes the fraction remaining. Nor does a count across independent trials with different success probabilities have the ordinary binomial distribution.

## A useful numerical check

The mean is $np$ and the variance is $np(1-p)$. A calculated distribution should concentrate roughly around $np$, while still assigning probabilities to other possible counts. The mean need not be an integer because it is an average across repeated experiments.
