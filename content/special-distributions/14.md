# Read the five-trial table by grouping sequences

In five independent trials, each with success probability $p$, there are $2^5=32$ success/failure sequences. The slide groups them according to $X$, the number of successes. Grouping turns a distribution on sequences into a distribution on counts.

## Why a group has a common probability per sequence

Set $q=1-p$. Both $SSFFF$ and $SFFSF$ contain two successes and three failures. Their probabilities are respectively $ppqqq$ and $pqqpq$, each equal to $p^2q^3$.

There are $\binom52=10$ ways to choose the positions of the two successes. Thus the ten disjoint sequences contribute a total probability $10p^2q^3$.

Repeating this argument for every count gives:

| Successes $x$ | Number of sequences | Probability of one sequence | $P(X=x)$ |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | $q^5$ | $q^5$ |
| 1 | 5 | $pq^4$ | $5pq^4$ |
| 2 | 10 | $p^2q^3$ | $10p^2q^3$ |
| 3 | 10 | $p^3q^2$ | $10p^3q^2$ |
| 4 | 5 | $p^4q$ | $5p^4q$ |
| 5 | 1 | $p^5$ | $p^5$ |

## Check the counting and the probabilities separately

The sequence counts sum to $1+5+10+10+5+1=32$. The probabilities sum to

$$q^5+5pq^4+10p^2q^3+10p^3q^2+5p^4q+p^5=(p+q)^5=1.$$

These are different checks: one accounts for all sequences, while the other accounts for all probability.

## When can we divide by 32?

Only when $p=q=1/2$ are all 32 sequences equally likely. Then each has probability $1/32$, and a count's probability is its number of sequences divided by 32.

When $p=0.8$, for example, $SSSSS$ has probability $0.8^5$, while $FFFFF$ has probability $0.2^5$. Counting favorable sequences and dividing by 32 would then be wrong.
