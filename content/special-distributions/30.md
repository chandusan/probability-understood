# Translate waiting-time language before calculating

In the fair-die experiment, $X$ is the roll on which the first six appears, with success probability $1/6$. The source's reference to a “first head” is a wording error: this experiment concerns a die and a six.

## Build the event from the first few rolls

Let $F$ mean a non-six and $S$ mean a six, with $q=5/6$ and $p=1/6$. Then

| Statement | What must happen? | Probability |
|---|---|---|
| $X=5$ | $FFFFS$ | $q^4p$ |
| $X>5$ | First five rolls are failures | $q^5$ |
| $X\ge5$ | First four rolls are failures | $q^4$ |
| $X\le5$ | At least one success in first five | $1-q^5$ |

This table distinguishes the three different endpoints that often cause mistakes.

## Why no final p appears in a tail probability

The event $X\ge10$ specifies nine initial failures but leaves the future open. After those failures, the first six might arrive immediately or much later. The probability of eventually obtaining one is one, so no additional factor $p$ is required:

$$P(X\ge10)=q^9.$$

By contrast, $X=10$ does require a success immediately after those nine failures, so $P(X=10)=q^9p$.

## Prepare the conditional event

Define

$$A=\{X\text{ is divisible by }3\},\qquad B=\{X\text{ is even}\}.$$

The condition restricts attention to $B=\{2,4,6,8,\ldots\}$. Among those values, $A$ retains $6,12,18,\ldots$. Hence

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}
=\frac{P(X\in\{6,12,18,\ldots\})}{P(X\in\{2,4,6,\ldots\})}.$$

We must use their geometric probabilities, because these possible waiting times are not equally likely. Simply counting “one in every three even integers” would ignore their unequal weights.
