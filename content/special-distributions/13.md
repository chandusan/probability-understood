# From one trial to a count of successes

Repeat a success-or-failure experiment independently $n$ times, with the same success probability $p$ on every trial. Let $X$ be the total number of successes. We want the probability of each value $0,1,\ldots,n$.

## Separate a sequence from a count

For five trials, the sequence $SFSFF$ is one particular outcome. It gives $X=2$. But $SSFFF$, $FFSSF$, and several other sequences also give $X=2$.

Therefore $P(X=2)$ cannot be calculated by finding the probability of just one sequence. We must add the probabilities of **all** sequences containing two successes.

## Find the probability of one fixed sequence

Write $q=1-p$. Independence means probabilities multiply across trials. Any specified sequence containing $k$ successes and $n-k$ failures has probability

$$p^kq^{n-k}.$$

For instance,

$$P(SFSFF)=p\cdot q\cdot p\cdot q\cdot q=p^2q^3.$$

All sequences with the same count have equal probability, because they contain the same numbers of factors $p$ and $q$. Sequences with different counts need not have equal probability.

## Count the sequences with that count

Choose which $k$ of the $n$ positions contain successes. Once those positions are selected, every remaining position is a failure. There are $\binom nk$ choices.

The resulting events are disjoint, so addition gives

$$\boxed{P(X=k)=\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}.}$$

This is the binomial probability formula. Its factors have different jobs: the powers give the probability of one pattern, and the binomial coefficient counts the qualifying patterns.

The assumptions are fixed $n$, two categories on each trial, independent trials, and a constant success probability. Without those conditions, a success count is not automatically binomial.
