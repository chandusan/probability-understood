# Why an MGF may fail to exist

An MGF averages $e^{tX}$. Exponential growth can overwhelm a distribution's decreasing tail, so that this average becomes infinite. Having ordinary moments does not by itself guarantee a finite MGF near zero.

## Finite moments are necessary but not sufficient

If an MGF exists near zero, then every positive integer moment exists. Consequently, a distribution missing some moments cannot have an MGF in this sense. A Student $t$ distribution with finitely many degrees of freedom is an example: its polynomial tails eventually make high-order moments diverge.

The reverse implication fails. The lognormal example shows why.

## A variable with every moment but no MGF near zero

Let $Z$ be standard normal and let $X=e^Z$. Its density is

$$f_X(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\,x}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\log x)^2}{2}\right),\qquad x>0.$$

For any positive integer $k$,

$$E[X^k]=E[e^{kZ}]=e^{k^2/2}<\infty.$$

To justify that moment formula, complete the square in the normal-density integral:

$$\begin{aligned}
E[e^{kZ}]&=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{kz-z^2/2}\,dz\\
&=e^{k^2/2}\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-(z-k)^2/2}\,dz\\
&=e^{k^2/2}.
\end{aligned}$$

The remaining integral is the total area of a shifted normal density, so it is one after including its normalizing factor.

But for $t>0$, the integrand used to calculate the MGF contains

$$\exp\!\left(tx-\frac{(\log x)^2}{2}\right)\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\,x}.$$

As $x$ grows, $tx$ eventually dominates $(\log x)^2/2$ and $\log x$. The integrand grows instead of decaying sufficiently, so the integral is infinite.

For $t\le0$, $0<e^{tX}\le1$, hence the expectation is finite. Nevertheless, **there is no interval around zero on which it is finite**. Under the chapter's definition, this variable has no MGF.

## Understand the source's wording

Saying “the lognormal MGF exists only for $t\le0$” means that the exponential expectation is finite there. It does not meet the two-sided neighborhood requirement for an existing MGF.

A two-sided example is $Y=Se^Z$, where an independent sign $S$ is equally likely to be $-1$ or $1$. Every absolute moment of $Y$ is finite, but $E[e^{tY}]$ is infinite for every $t\ne0$: one of the two tails is always magnified.
