# Sum the geometric distribution and derive its CDF

Let $X$ count trials until the first success, with independent success probability $p>0$. Write $q=1-p$, so $P(X=x)=pq^{x-1}$ for $x=1,2,\ldots$. These probabilities form a geometric series.

## Why the infinite probabilities total one

For $0\le q<1$,

$$1+q+q^2+\cdots=\frac1{1-q}.$$

One way to see this is to multiply the sum by $q$ and subtract: all terms cancel except the first, leaving $(1-q)S=1$. More formally, the finite sum is $(1-q^{m+1})/(1-q)$, and the remainder tends to zero.

Therefore

$$\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}pq^{x-1}=\frac p{1-q}=\frac pp=1.$$

## Obtain the CDF using a complement

For an integer $m\ge0$, $X>m$ means the first $m$ trials all fail. Thus

$$P(X>m)=q^m,\qquad P(X\le m)=1-q^m.$$

For a real threshold $t\ge1$, $X\le t$ is equivalent to $X\le\lfloor t\rfloor$, where $\lfloor t\rfloor$ is the greatest integer no larger than $t$. The full CDF is

$$\boxed{F_X(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t<1,\\1-q^{\lfloor t\rfloor},&t\ge1.\end{cases}}$$

The first branch is necessary; extending the source's expression to negative $t$ would produce invalid negative probabilities when $0<q<1$.

## The distribution forgets how long you have already waited

For nonnegative integers $s,t$ and $0<p<1$,

$$P(X>s+t\mid X>s)=\frac{q^{s+t}}{q^s}=q^t=P(X>t).$$

After $s$ failures, the next $t$ independent trials behave like a fresh start. This is the memoryless property. It does not mean that a success becomes “due” after a long wait; the probability of success on the next trial remains $p$.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Derive the mean and variance from the geometric series</summary>

For $qe^t<1$, sum the MGF as a geometric series:

$$m_X(t)=\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}pq^{x-1}e^{tx}
=pe^t\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(qe^t)^j
=\frac{pe^t}{1-qe^t}.$$

When $0<p<1$, this condition holds for $t<-\log q$, an interval containing zero. The cumulant generating function is

$$K_X(t)=\log p+t-\log(1-qe^t).$$

Its first derivative is

$$K_X'(t)=1+\frac{qe^t}{1-qe^t}=\frac1{1-qe^t},$$

and its second derivative is

$$K_X''(t)=\frac{qe^t}{(1-qe^t)^2}.$$

At zero, $1-q=p$. Therefore $E[X]=1/p$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)=q/p^2$. For $p=1$, $X=1$ with certainty, which gives the same mean and variance directly.

</details>
