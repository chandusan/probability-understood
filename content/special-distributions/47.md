# Find the number of lottery plays needed for five wins

Suppose each lottery play independently wins some prize with probability $0.03$, and this probability stays constant across plays. Play until the fifth win. Let $X$ count all plays, including the play that produces that fifth win. Find the PMF of $X$.

## Translate the stopping condition

For $X=x$, the first $x-1$ plays must contain exactly four wins, and the final play must be a win. There are

$$\binom{x-1}{4}$$

ways to choose the four earlier winning positions. Each complete pattern has five wins and $x-5$ nonwins, giving probability $(0.03)^5(0.97)^{x-5}$.

Therefore

$$\boxed{P(X=x)=\binom{x-1}{4}(0.03)^5(0.97)^{x-5},\qquad x=5,6,7,\ldots.}$$

The PMF is zero for smaller values: five wins cannot occur in fewer than five plays.

## Check the first two possible stopping times

Stopping at five requires five consecutive wins:

$$P(X=5)=(0.03)^5=0.0000000243.$$

Stopping at six requires exactly four wins among the first five plays, then a win on play six:

$$P(X=6)=\binom54(0.03)^5(0.97).$$

The factor five chooses the location of the single early nonwin. A nonwin on the sixth play would not qualify.

## Interpret the average waiting time

The mean is

$$E[X]=\frac5{0.03}\approx166.67\text{ plays}.$$

This is a repeated-experiment average, not a deadline by which five wins are guaranteed. The variance is $5(0.97)/(0.03)^2\approx5388.89$, indicating substantial variation around the mean.

To ask whether five wins arrive by play $m$, count successes in the first $m$ independent plays:

$$P(X\le m)=P(\operatorname{Binom}(m,0.03)\ge5).$$

The lottery example supplies an assumed fixed probability for an educational calculation; the distribution follows from that assumption and the stopping rule.
