# Sample a category without replacement

A population contains $N=a+b$ distinct objects: $a$ in category A and $b$ in category B. Select a simple random sample of $n$ objects without replacement, with every size-$n$ subset equally likely. Let $X$ count the sampled category-A objects. We want its distribution.

## Why the binomial mechanism no longer applies exactly

The first draw has category-A probability $a/N$. If it selects A, the next draw's probability is $(a-1)/(N-1)$; if it selects B, the next probability is $a/(N-1)$.

Thus the outcomes of successive draws are dependent. An independent-trial binomial calculation would not account for this changing composition.

## Count complete samples instead

There are $\binom Nn$ possible unordered samples. They are equally likely by the simple-random-sampling assumption.

If $X=x$, the sample must contain $x$ objects from A and $n-x$ from B. The number of ways to choose these is

$$\binom ax\binom b{n-x}.$$

Consequently,

$$\boxed{P(X=x)=\frac{\binom ax\binom b{n-x}}{\binom Nn}.}$$

This is the hypergeometric PMF.

## Determine which counts are feasible

We need $0\le x\le a$, $x\le n$, and $0\le n-x\le b$. Combining these conditions gives

$$\boxed{\max(0,n-b)\le x\le\min(n,a),\qquad x\text{ an integer}.}$$

For example, if the population has only two B objects and the sample has five objects, at least three must be A. The lower endpoint then exceeds zero.

## Why order does not appear in the formula

Uniform sequential draws without replacement make every ordered sample equally likely. Each size-$n$ subset has exactly $n!$ orders, so every unordered subset also has equal probability. Because $X$ only records category counts, using subsets avoids carrying those common ordering factors through the calculation.
