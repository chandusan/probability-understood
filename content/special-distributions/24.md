# Derive the binomial mean and variance step by step

For $X\sim\operatorname{Binom}(n,p)$, write $q=1-p$ and start with the complete MGF $m_X(t)=(q+pe^t)^n$. The first derivative at zero gives $E[X]$; the second gives $E[X^2]$.

## Apply the chain rule for the first derivative

The outer function is an $n$th power; the inside is $q+pe^t$, whose derivative is $pe^t$. Hence

$$m_X'(t)=n(q+pe^t)^{n-1}pe^t.$$

At zero, $e^0=1$ and $q+p=1$, so

$$\boxed{E[X]=m_X'(0)=n(1)^{n-1}p=np.}$$

## Apply the product rule for the second derivative

Rewrite the first derivative as $np\,e^t(q+pe^t)^{n-1}$. Both $e^t$ and the power change with $t$, giving

$$\begin{aligned}
m_X''(t)
&=np\,e^t(q+pe^t)^{n-1}\\
&\quad+np\,e^t(n-1)(q+pe^t)^{n-2}pe^t.
\end{aligned}$$

Evaluating at zero produces

$$E[X^2]=np+n(n-1)p^2.$$

The formula includes both product-rule contributions. Retaining only one would give an incorrect second moment.

## Subtract the squared mean

$$\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X)
&=np+n(n-1)p^2-(np)^2\\
&=np+(n^2-n)p^2-n^2p^2\\
&=np-np^2\\
&=\boxed{np(1-p)=npq.}
\end{aligned}$$

## An intuitive check using indicators

Each Bernoulli trial has mean $p$ and variance $pq$. Adding $n$ such variables gives mean $np$ by linearity of expectation. Independence makes their covariances zero, so their variances add to $npq$.

This explains the result directly: more independent trials contribute more average successes and more total variation. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{npq}$, measured in successes, while the variance is measured in squared successes.
