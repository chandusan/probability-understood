# Read and check the complete multinomial model

The multinomial PMF describes category counts from $n$ independent trials with fixed probabilities $p_1,\ldots,p_k$. Its coefficient counts sequences, while its probability product weights each sequence. Both parts are necessary.

## The formula with its support

For nonnegative integers summing to $n$,

$$p(x_1,\ldots,x_k)=\frac{n!}{x_1!\cdots x_k!}
\prod_{i=1}^k p_i^{x_i}.$$

The PMF is zero otherwise. The source's left-hand argument list ends at $x_n$; it should end at $x_k$, because there are $k$ category counts and $n$ trials.

If $x_i=0$, its factorial is $0!=1$ and the empty product contributes 1. If a category has probability zero, any count vector assigning it a positive count has probability zero.

## Why the probabilities sum to one

Every ordered trial sequence has exactly one count vector. Grouping all sequences by their vector partitions the entire sample space into disjoint events. The addition rule therefore gives total probability 1.

Algebraically, the same grouping is the multinomial theorem:

$$\sum_{x_1+\cdots+x_k=n}
\frac{n!}{x_1!\cdots x_k!}\prod_i p_i^{x_i}
=(p_1+\cdots+p_k)^n=1.$$

## Recover the binomial as a special case

With two categories, specifying $X_1=x$ automatically forces $X_2=n-x$. Set $p_1=p$ and $p_2=1-p$:

$$P(X_1=x)=\frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!}p^x(1-p)^{n-x}.$$

That is precisely the binomial PMF. The extra categories introduce additional counts, not a different probability rule.

## Combine categories when the question allows it

If we only care whether a trial belongs to categories 1 or 2, merge them into a single success event of probability $p_1+p_2$. Then

$$X_1+X_2\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p_1+p_2).$$

This often avoids a long sum over the full count table. Merging categories is justified because they are disjoint outcomes on each trial, and independent trials remain independent after this relabeling.
