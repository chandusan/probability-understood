# Read the population-and-sample diagram as a counting argument

The diagram divides a population into category A with $a$ objects and category B with $b$ objects. A sample of size $n$ takes $x$ from A and $n-x$ from B. The two sample counts must add to $n$ because the categories partition the population.

## Turn the picture into three choices

To construct a sample with $X=x$:

1. Select $x$ of the $a$ category-A objects.
2. Select $n-x$ of the $b$ category-B objects.
3. Combine the two selected sets.

The first step has $\binom ax$ choices and the second $\binom b{n-x}$. Each pair of selections yields one unique complete sample, and every qualifying sample can be split back into exactly one such pair.

Therefore the number of qualifying samples is their product:

$$\binom ax\binom b{n-x}.$$

This multiplication is a **counting rule**. It does not assert that category outcomes of successive draws are independent.

## Use the same kind of object in the denominator

The numerator counts unordered samples of distinct objects, so the denominator must count unordered samples as well:

$$\#\{\text{all samples}\}=\binom{a+b}{n}.$$

Dividing an unordered numerator by an ordered denominator would mix two different sample spaces and produce the wrong probability.

## A small concrete example

Suppose there are five A objects and three B objects, and we select four. To obtain two of each category, there are

$$\binom52\binom32=10\cdot3=30$$

qualifying samples out of $\binom84=70$ equally likely samples. Hence $P(X=2)=30/70=3/7$.

## Verify that the diagram exhausts the possibilities

Every sample has exactly one count $x$. Summing the favorable counts across feasible $x$ gives

$$\sum_x\binom ax\binom b{n-x}=\binom{a+b}{n}.$$

This identity says that counting all samples by their category composition gives the same total as choosing the sample directly. Dividing through confirms that the hypergeometric probabilities total one.
