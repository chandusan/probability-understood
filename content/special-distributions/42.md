# Model September-first birthdays among five hundred people

Five hundred people attend a meeting. Approximate the probability that at most one was born on September 1. For this exercise, assume birthdays are independent and uniformly distributed over 365 dates, ignoring leap days.

## Define the count and its exact distribution

Let $X$ be the number of attendees with the specified birthday. Each person is one trial, with success meaning “born on September 1.” Thus

$$n=500,\qquad p=\frac1{365},\qquad X\sim\operatorname{Binom}\left(500,\frac1{365}\right).$$

We are counting matches to one fixed date. This is different from asking whether any two attendees share any birthday.

## Choose the matching Poisson mean

There are many trials and the probability per trial is small. Set

$$\lambda=np=\frac{500}{365}\approx1.369863.$$

The approximation uses $Y\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)$.

“At most one” means zero or one, so

$$\begin{aligned}
P(X\le1)&\approx P(Y=0)+P(Y=1)\\
&=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^0}{0!}+e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^1}{1!}\\
&=e^{-\lambda}(1+\lambda).
\end{aligned}$$

Substituting $\lambda=500/365$ gives

$$\boxed{P(X\le1)\approx0.602281184.}$$

## Keep the model assumptions attached to the answer

The uniform-birthday assumption is a simplified classroom model. It makes the success probability exactly $1/365$ for the chosen date. If the actual probability for that date were $p_*$, the same derivation would instead use $\lambda=500p_*$.

Independence also matters: simply knowing that each person has marginal probability $1/365$ does not determine the distribution of their total count if their birthdays are dependent.

The mean of about $1.37$ does not imply that one or two people must match. It is an average count across repeated groups, while an individual group can have any count from zero to five hundred.
