# Four useful things an MGF can do

An MGF is useful when it simplifies a question about a distribution. Its main jobs are checking normalization, recovering moments, transforming random variables, and handling sums of independent variables.

## Recover moments by differentiating at zero

The derivatives provide

$$E[X]=m_X'(0),\qquad E[X^2]=m_X''(0),\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=m_X''(0)-m_X'(0)^2.$$

Why zero? Differentiating $e^{tX}$ brings down powers of $X$, and then $e^{0X}=1$ removes the remaining exponential. The next slide makes this argument explicit.

## Shift and scale a variable

If $Y=aX+b$, then

$$\begin{aligned}
m_Y(t)&=E[e^{t(aX+b)}]\\
&=e^{bt}E[e^{(at)X}]\\
&=\boxed{e^{bt}m_X(at)}.
\end{aligned}$$

The nonrandom factor $e^{bt}$ can be pulled outside the expectation. A shift becomes an exponential factor; a scale changes the input of the original MGF.

## Combine independent sums

For independent $X_1,\ldots,X_n$,

$$m_{X_1+\cdots+X_n}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^n m_{X_i}(t).$$

This can be much easier than summing the probabilities of all combinations yielding each possible total. Independence is essential for factoring the expectation.

## Recognize a distribution

MGFs finite around zero uniquely determine their distributions. If the MGF of a sum matches a familiar MGF throughout a neighborhood of zero, we have identified the sum's distribution.

The practical question is still whether this saves work. For a uniform variable, integrating $x$ directly finds the mean with less algebra than differentiating its MGF. Having a valid tool does not make it the simplest tool for every calculation.
