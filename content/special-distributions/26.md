# Different stopping and sampling rules give different distributions

Several discrete distributions count successes, but they count them under different experiments. Before choosing a formula, identify what is fixed and what is allowed to vary.

## Fixed trials or a random stopping time?

In a binomial experiment, the number of trials $n$ is fixed and the success count is random. In a geometric experiment, we stop at the first success, so the success count at stopping is fixed at one and the trial count is random.

A negative binomial experiment stops at the $r$th success. The trial count can be $r,r+1,\ldots$ because at least $r$ trials are needed, with any number of failures possible in between.

| Model | What stays fixed? | What does $X$ count? |
|---|---|---|
| Binomial | $n$ trials and success probability $p$ | Successes |
| Geometric | Success probability $p$ | Trials until the first success |
| Negative binomial | Success probability $p$ and target $r$ | Trials until the $r$th success |
| Hypergeometric | Finite population and sample size | Marked objects sampled without replacement |
| Poisson | Mean count in a specified region or interval | Events in that region or interval |

## Replacement changes dependence

Drawing uniformly with replacement restores the population before each draw, so independent draws have the same category probabilities. Drawing without replacement changes the composition remaining and creates dependence. Hypergeometric probabilities account for that dependence by counting complete samples.

## Poisson counts describe another mechanism

A Poisson model is often appropriate for a large number of small, approximately independent opportunities for an event. The parameter $\lambda$ is the expected count in the chosen interval, not a probability and not necessarily an integer.

The names are useful shortcuts once the experiment is clear. If all you know is that a variable is a nonnegative integer, that alone does not identify any one of these distributions.
