# Choose success positions to derive the binomial coefficient

For $n$ independent trials with success probability $p$, a total of $k$ successes can occur in many orders. We will count those orders explicitly and explain why the factor is $\binom nk$, rather than $n!$ or $k!$.

## Label the positions, not the successes

The trials have distinguishable positions $1,2,\ldots,n$. We choose a subset of $k$ positions to be successes. For example, for $n=5$ and $k=2$, the subset $\{2,5\}$ determines the single sequence $FSFFS$.

The order in which we name the selected positions is irrelevant: choosing $2$ then $5$ and choosing $5$ then $2$ produce the same sequence.

There are

$$n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$$

ordered selections of $k$ distinct positions. Each unordered selection is counted $k!$ times, so the desired number is

$$\boxed{\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.}$$

## Attach the probability of each sequence

Each sequence has $k$ factors $p$ and $n-k$ factors $q=1-p$, hence probability $p^kq^{n-k}$. Different sequences cannot occur simultaneously, so the addition rule gives

$$P(X=k)=\binom nkp^kq^{n-k}.$$

There is no independence assumption between these whole-sequence events; they are disjoint. Independence was used earlier to find the probability **within** one sequence.

## Work one concrete example

For $n=6$, $p=0.3$, and exactly two successes,

$$P(X=2)=\binom62(0.3)^2(0.7)^4
=15(0.09)(0.2401)=0.324135.$$

The coefficient $15$ counts success-position pairs. The value $0.021609$ is the probability of any one of those patterns. Multiplying combines the fifteen mutually exclusive ways to obtain the requested count.
