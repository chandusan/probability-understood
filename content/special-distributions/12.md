# One success-or-failure trial: the Bernoulli distribution

Consider one experiment with two categories, called success and failure. Let success have probability $p$ and failure probability $q=1-p$. Define $X=1$ for success and $X=0$ for failure. Then $X$ has a Bernoulli distribution.

## Success is simply the category we count

A “success” might be a defective product, a missed appointment, or a correct answer. The word does not mean that the outcome is desirable. It identifies the event encoded by the value $1$.

The distribution is

$$P(X=0)=1-p,\qquad P(X=1)=p.$$

For $0<p<1$, these two cases can be written together as

$$\boxed{p_X(x)=p^x(1-p)^{1-x},\qquad x\in\{0,1\}.}$$

Outside that support the probability is zero. At $p=0$ or $p=1$, the two-case description also handles the degenerate distribution without any ambiguity involving $0^0$.

## Derive the mean and variance directly

$$E[X]=0(1-p)+1p=p.$$

Since squaring $0$ or $1$ does not change it, $X^2=X$. Therefore

$$E[X^2]=p,\qquad \boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X)=p-p^2=p(1-p).}$$

The mean $p$ is a long-run proportion, although a single observation is always either $0$ or $1$.

The MGF is

$$m_X(t)=(1-p)+pe^t.$$

## The building block for a binomial count

If we repeat the experiment independently with the same success probability, each outcome can be encoded by a Bernoulli variable. Adding those zeros and ones counts successes.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Check: what changes if we encode failure as 1 instead?</summary>

The new variable is $Y=1-X$. It is Bernoulli with parameter $1-p$. Its mean is $1-p$ and its variance is still $p(1-p)$.

</details>
