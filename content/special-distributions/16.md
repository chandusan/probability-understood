# Why the binomial probabilities add to one

The formula $P(X=k)=\binom nkp^kq^{n-k}$ gives the probabilities of a success count, where $q=1-p$. The binomial theorem both explains its name and verifies that the probabilities form a complete distribution.

## Expand a product of n two-term factors

Consider

$$(p+q)^n=(p+q)(p+q)\cdots(p+q).$$

To construct one term in the expansion, choose either $p$ or $q$ from each factor. This is exactly like choosing success or failure on each trial.

A term with $k$ choices of $p$ and $n-k$ choices of $q$ is $p^kq^{n-k}$. There are $\binom nk$ ways to decide which factors supply $p$. Grouping identical terms therefore yields

$$(p+q)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nkp^kq^{n-k}.$$

Since $p+q=1$,

$$\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^nP(X=k)=1.}$$

## The algebra matches the probability argument

The events $X=0,X=1,\ldots,X=n$ are disjoint and cover every sequence. Their probabilities must total one. The expansion confirms that the formula we derived does exactly that.

The term “binomial distribution” comes from this two-term expansion, not from an additional random mechanism. The random mechanism remains a fixed number of independent trials with a common success probability.

## Boundary cases provide another check

If $p=0$, every trial fails and $P(X=0)=1$. If $p=1$, every trial succeeds and $P(X=n)=1$. For these cases, interpret the PMF through the experiment, avoiding any apparent $0^0$ ambiguity in the compressed notation.

For $0<p<1$, every integer from $0$ to $n$ has positive probability, and all values outside that set have probability zero.
