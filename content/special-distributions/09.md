# Why MGF derivatives and products work

Assume the MGFs used here are finite on an interval around zero. We will derive the properties from expectation, differentiation, and independence so that each formula has a clear reason.

## The value at zero records total probability

$$m_X(0)=E[e^{0X}]=E[1]=1.$$

For a discrete variable this says $\sum_xP(X=x)=1$; for a density it says $\int f_X(x)\,dx=1$.

## Differentiation brings down powers of X

For a finite discrete distribution, differentiate the finite sum term by term:

$$\begin{aligned}
m_X(t)&=\sum_xp(x)e^{tx},\\
m_X'(t)&=\sum_xxp(x)e^{tx},\\
m_X^{(k)}(t)&=\sum_xx^kp(x)e^{tx}.
\end{aligned}$$

At zero,

$$\boxed{m_X^{(k)}(0)=\sum_xx^kp(x)=E[X^k].}$$

The same result holds for infinite sums and integrals when the MGF is finite near zero; this condition justifies passing differentiation through the expectation. Without an appropriate condition, that interchange cannot simply be assumed.

## The power series displays all the moments

Near zero, the exponential has expansion $e^{tX}=1+tX+t^2X^2/2!+\cdots$. Averaging term by term, justified here by the MGF's existence around zero, gives

$$m_X(t)=1+E[X]t+\frac{E[X^2]}{2!}t^2+\frac{E[X^3]}{3!}t^3+\cdots.$$

Thus the coefficient of $t^k$ is $E[X^k]/k!$. Differentiating $k$ times and setting $t=0$ multiplies that coefficient by $k!$ and removes the other terms. This is another way to see why the derivatives recover moments.

## Independent sums become products

Let $Y=X_1+\cdots+X_n$. Exponential algebra first gives

$$e^{tY}=e^{tX_1}\cdots e^{tX_n}.$$

Then independence permits the factorization

$$\boxed{m_Y(t)=E\!\left[\prod_i e^{tX_i}\right]=\prod_iE[e^{tX_i}]=\prod_im_{X_i}(t).}$$

The exponential identity always holds; the factorization of expectations requires independence. These are two separate steps.

## Equal MGFs identify equal distributions

If two MGFs agree on a neighborhood of zero, the distributions agree. This is the uniqueness property, which we use when recognizing a product as the MGF of a known distribution.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Check: does the product rule hold when Y = X + X?</summary>

In general, no: the two copies are the same variable and are dependent. Directly, $m_Y(t)=E[e^{2tX}]=m_X(2t)$, which need not equal $m_X(t)^2$. For a fair $0/1$ variable, these are $(1+e^{2t})/2$ and $(1+e^t)^2/4$ respectively.

</details>
