# Keep the test's conditioning directions straight

The source writes A for disease and B for a positive test. The given conditional probabilities describe test outcomes within disease groups; the requested probability describes disease status within the positive-test group. Writing each reference group explicitly prevents a reversal error.

## Match the source notation to the events

Define

$$A=\{\text{person has the disease}\},\qquad
A^c=\{\text{person does not have the disease}\},$$

$$B=\{\text{test is positive}\},\qquad
B^c=\{\text{test is negative}\}.$$

The population prevalence and test rates are

$$P(A)=0.001,\quad P(A^c)=0.999,$$

$$P(B\mid A)=0.96,\quad P(B\mid A^c)=0.02.$$

The target is $P(A\mid B)$. Reading the bar as “within the group where” makes the difference explicit: $P(B\mid A)$ is the positive rate **within the disease group**, while $P(A\mid B)$ is the disease rate **within the positive group**.

## Convert percentages before combining them

The prevalence is $0.1\%=0.001$, not 0.1. The sensitivity is $96\%=0.96$, and the false-positive rate is $2\%=0.02$.

An error at this stage changes the relative sizes of the two population groups, which can substantially change the posterior. Keep the units consistent: all factors in a probability calculation should be decimals or fractions, not a mixture of percentages and decimals.

## Build all four joint probabilities

Multiply each group probability by the conditional probability within that group:

| Joint event | Meaning | Probability |
|---|---|---:|
| $A\cap B$ | Disease and positive | $0.001(0.96)=0.00096$ |
| $A\cap B^c$ | Disease and negative | $0.001(0.04)=0.00004$ |
| $A^c\cap B$ | No disease and positive | $0.999(0.02)=0.01998$ |
| $A^c\cap B^c$ | No disease and negative | $0.999(0.98)=0.97902$ |

These events are disjoint and exhaustive, and their probabilities sum to one. The first two rows recover $P(A)=0.001$; the last two recover $P(A^c)=0.999$.

The positive group combines rows one and three:

$$P(B)=0.00096+0.01998=0.02094.$$

Restricting to that group gives

$$\boxed{P(A\mid B)=\frac{0.00096}{0.02094}=\frac{16}{349}\approx4.5845\%.}$$

## Keep the condition unchanged when complementing

Within the positive group, disease and no disease are complements:

$$P(A^c\mid B)=1-P(A\mid B)=\frac{333}{349}.$$

Within the disease group, positive and negative results are complements:

$$P(B^c\mid A)=1-P(B\mid A)=0.04.$$

These are different complement operations because the information after the bar is different. In particular, $P(A\mid B^c)$ is not $1-P(A\mid B)$. To calculate the probability of disease given a negative result, we must use the negative-test group as a new denominator.

## Condition on a negative result as a cross-check

The negative group has probability $0.00004+0.97902=0.97906$. Thus

$$P(A\mid B^c)=\frac{0.00004}{0.97906}
=\frac4{97{,}906}
\approx0.0000408555.$$

Expressed as a percentage, this is about **0.00409%**. The scaled count is four people with the disease among 97,906 people with negative results in the model's 100,000-person table.

## Check your understanding

Which of the four joint events is a false negative, and what is the difference between its probability and the false-negative rate?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Distinguish a population share from a within-group rate</summary>

A false negative is $A\cap B^c$, with probability 0.00004 in the whole population. The false-negative rate is $P(B^c\mid A)=0.04$, measured only among people with the disease. Multiplying that rate by $P(A)=0.001$ gives the joint probability.
