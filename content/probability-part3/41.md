# Solve the recurrence by adding its geometric differences

The first-step equation says that successive differences in the winning probabilities have ratio q/p. Starting from the boundary value at zero, those differences rebuild every probability by a telescoping sum. The boundary value at k then fixes their overall scale.

## Give each adjacent difference a name

Define

$$
\Delta_j=P_j-P_{j-1},\qquad1\le j\le k,
\qquad \rho=\frac qp.
$$

The previous slide established

$$
P_{i+1}-P_i=\rho(P_i-P_{i-1}),
$$

which is simply $\Delta_{i+1}=\rho\Delta_i$. Since $P_0=0$, the first difference is $\Delta_1=P_1$.

Now apply the relation one step at a time:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta_1&=P_1,\\
\Delta_2&=\rho\Delta_1=\rho P_1,\\
\Delta_3&=\rho\Delta_2=\rho^2P_1,\\
\Delta_4&=\rho\Delta_3=\rho^3P_1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Continuing gives $\Delta_j=\rho^{j-1}P_1$. The exponent is $j-1$ because the first difference has been multiplied by $\rho$ zero times.

## Expand the cancellation in the telescoping sum

To reach $P_i$ from $P_0$, add all i adjacent changes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta_1+\cdots+\Delta_i
&=(P_1-P_0)+(P_2-P_1)+\cdots+(P_i-P_{i-1})\\
&=P_i-P_0=P_i.
\end{aligned}
$$

Every interior term appears once positively and once negatively. For example, when $i=3$, the $+P_1$ cancels the $-P_1$ and the $+P_2$ cancels the $-P_2$, leaving $P_3-P_0$.

Substituting the geometric differences yields

$$
\boxed{P_i=P_1(1+\rho+\rho^2+\cdots+\rho^{i-1})}
=P_1\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\rho^j.
$$

The finite sum has i terms. It stops at exponent $i-1$, not i.

## Use the other boundary to determine the scale

The same identity at the terminal fortune k gives

$$
1=P_k=P_1(1+\rho+\cdots+\rho^{k-1}).
$$

Therefore

$$
P_1=\frac1{1+\rho+\cdots+\rho^{k-1}},
$$

and

$$
\boxed{P_i=
\frac{1+\rho+\cdots+\rho^{i-1}}
{1+\rho+\cdots+\rho^{k-1}}.}
$$

This finite-sum form already solves the problem. Every term is positive because $p,q>0$, and the numerator is the initial part of the denominator's sum. Thus $0<P_i<1$ for interior i. For $i=0$, use an empty numerator sum equal to zero.

The expression also works when $\rho=1$. The later case split is needed only when replacing the sums by a closed geometric-series formula that divides by $1-\rho$.

## Calculate the entire curve in one example

Take $p=2/3$, $q=1/3$, and $k=4$. Then $\rho=1/2$, so the differences have relative sizes

$$
1,\quad\frac12,\quad\frac14,\quad\frac18.
$$

Their sum is $15/8$. To make the total increase from $P_0=0$ to $P_4=1$ exactly one, multiply them all by $8/15$:

| Starting fortune j | Difference $\Delta_j$ | Cumulative probability $P_j$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | — | 0 |
| 1 | $8/15$ | $8/15$ |
| 2 | $4/15$ | $12/15$ |
| 3 | $2/15$ | $14/15$ |
| 4 | $1/15$ | 1 |

The gain in success probability from one extra starting dollar becomes smaller as the fortune increases in this favorable game. If $p<q$, the ratio $\rho>1$ instead makes the successive gains larger.

## Check your understanding

If $p=q=1/2$, what do the differences become, and what does the finite-sum ratio give?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Every geometric term equals one</summary>

Now $\rho=1$, so all k differences are equal. Since they sum to one, each is $1/k$. Adding i of them gives $P_i=i/k$. The numerator has i ones and the denominator k ones, so the same answer follows directly from the sum ratio.

</details>
