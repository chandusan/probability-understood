# One fox tag lost, given that at least one remains

A fox receives one tag on each ear and is released. Each tag is lost with probability $p$, and the two loss events are independent. What is the probability that **exactly one tag is lost, given that at most one tag is lost**? The condition means at least one tag remains attached. The answer is $2p/(1+p)$ for $0\le p<1$.

## Distinguish the ears even though the tags are identical

Use the source notation

$$C_1=\{\text{left ear tag is lost}\},\qquad
C_2=\{\text{right ear tag is lost}\}.$$

The assumptions are

$$P(C_1)=P(C_2)=p,\qquad P(C_1\cap C_2)=p^2.$$

Identical tags do not collapse “left lost, right retained” and “right lost, left retained” into one equally weighted state. They are distinct possibilities, and their probabilities must both contribute to “exactly one lost.”

Define

$$E=(C_1\cap C_2^c)\cup(C_1^c\cap C_2)$$

for exactly one loss, and

$$T=(C_1\cap C_2)^c$$

for at most one loss. We seek $P(E\mid T)$. Every outcome in $E$ is in $T$, so $E\cap T=E$.

## Build the four possible states

Independence and the complement rule give:

| Left tag | Right tag | Probability | In $E$? | In $T$? |
|---|---|---:|---|---|
| Retained | Retained | $(1-p)^2$ | No | Yes |
| Lost | Retained | $p(1-p)$ | Yes | Yes |
| Retained | Lost | $(1-p)p$ | Yes | Yes |
| Lost | Lost | $p^2$ | No | No |

The factor $p$ represents a loss and $1-p$ represents retention. For example, independence gives $P(C_1\cap C_2^c)=p(1-p)$, because replacing an independent event with its complement preserves independence.

The four rows need not be equally likely. Simply saying “two of three allowed states qualify, so the answer is $2/3$” works only for the special case $p=1/2$.

## Calculate numerator and denominator separately

The two exactly-one-loss cases are disjoint, so

$$P(E)=p(1-p)+(1-p)p=2p(1-p).$$

The condition excludes only losing both tags. Its probability is

$$P(T)=1-p^2.$$

Equivalently, adding the first three rows gives $(1-p)^2+2p(1-p)=1-p^2$.

Now use the conditional formula:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(E\mid T)
&=\frac{P(E\cap T)}{P(T)}\\
&=\frac{2p(1-p)}{1-p^2}\\
&=\frac{2p(1-p)}{(1-p)(1+p)}\\
&=\boxed{\frac{2p}{1+p}},\qquad 0\le p<1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The denominator includes both zero losses and one loss. Using $1-(1-p)^2$ instead would condition on **at least one lost**, a different statement.

## Why p = 1 must be excluded

At $p=1$, both tags are lost with probability one, so $P(T)=0$. The conditional probability is undefined by the elementary formula. Although the simplified expression $2p/(1+p)$ evaluates to 1 there, canceling $1-p$ was valid only when $p\ne1$. The simplified expression cannot make an impossible conditioning event valid.

At $p=0$, neither tag can be lost, so the answer is zero. As $p$ approaches 1 from below, the conditional answer approaches 1: among the increasingly rare foxes with at least one retained tag, one retained tag is much more likely than two.

## A numerical check

For $p=0.2$, the four row probabilities are $0.64,0.16,0.16,0.04$. Conditioning removes the last row, leaving total probability $0.96$. Exactly one loss has probability $0.32$, so

$$P(E\mid T)=\frac{0.32}{0.96}=\frac13=\frac{2(0.2)}{1+0.2}.$$

## Check your understanding

If $p=1/2$, why does counting two favorable states out of three permitted states happen to work?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

With independent losses of probability $1/2$, all four original states have probability $1/4$. After excluding “both lost,” the three retained states remain equally likely. Two have exactly one loss, giving $2/3$, consistent with $2p/(1+p)$. That equal-weight argument does not extend to arbitrary $p$.

</details>
