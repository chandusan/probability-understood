# A positive test: count true and false positives together

In the slide's hypothetical population, 0.1% have a disease. A test is positive for 96% of people who have it and for 2% of people who do not. Given a positive result, what is the probability that the person has the disease? The answer is about 4.58%, and a complete count explains why it differs so much from 96%.

## Define the events and translate the percentages

Let D mean the randomly selected person has the disease, and let $+$ mean the test is positive. Assume the stated rates apply to the population from which the person is selected. Then

$$P(D)=0.1\%=0.001,\qquad P(D^c)=0.999,$$

$$P(+\mid D)=0.96,\qquad P(+\mid D^c)=0.02.$$

The requested probability is $P(D\mid +)$. It is the reverse of the supplied sensitivity $P(+\mid D)$.

These are stipulated rates for a mathematical example. We are calculating within this model rather than estimating the performance of a particular real test.

## Distinguish the two test properties

**Sensitivity** is $P(+\mid D)=0.96$: among people with the disease, the fraction receiving positive results. The corresponding false-negative rate is $P(-\mid D)=1-0.96=0.04$.

**Specificity** is $P(-\mid D^c)=0.98$: among people without the disease, the fraction receiving negative results. Its complement is the false-positive rate $P(+\mid D^c)=0.02$.

Neither sensitivity nor specificity uses positive tests as its denominator. The desired probability does, so it requires combining both population groups.

## Scale the model to 100,000 people

At prevalence 0.1%, a model population of 100,000 contains 100 people with the disease and 99,900 without it. Apply each rate only within the group to which it refers:

| Population group | Positive | Negative | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Disease | $100(0.96)=96$ | $100(0.04)=4$ | 100 |
| No disease | $99{,}900(0.02)=1{,}998$ | $99{,}900(0.98)=97{,}902$ | 99,900 |
| Total | 2,094 | 97,906 | 100,000 |

The table is a proportional representation of the assumed rates. Random samples need not contain exactly these counts.

After learning that the test is positive, restrict to the positive column. There are 2,094 positive results, of which 96 belong to people with the disease:

$$\boxed{P(D\mid +)=\frac{96}{2094}=\frac{16}{349}
\approx0.04584527=4.5845\%.}$$

## Derive the same answer using Bayes' rule

A positive result comes through two disjoint paths: disease-and-positive or no-disease-and-positive. Their probabilities are

$$P(D\cap +)=(0.001)(0.96)=0.00096,$$

$$P(D^c\cap +)=(0.999)(0.02)=0.01998.$$

Thus $P(+)=0.00096+0.01998=0.02094$, and

$$P(D\mid +)
=\frac{0.001(0.96)}{0.001(0.96)+0.999(0.02)}
=\frac{0.00096}{0.02094}.
$$

Multiplying numerator and denominator by 100,000 recovers exactly the table's $96/2094$.

## Why false positives outnumber true positives

The 2% false-positive rate is applied to 99,900 people, while the 96% true-positive rate is applied to only 100 people. A small fraction of the much larger group can exceed a large fraction of the smaller group.

The result still changes the probability substantially: it rises from 0.1% before seeing the test to about 4.58% after a positive result. Evidence can increase a probability markedly without making the event more likely than its complement.

The later solution slide uses 0.95 in place of the stated 0.96. We retain the problem's **96% sensitivity** consistently; slide 29 explains the discrepancy explicitly.
