# Condition on the next play to build the ruin recurrence

The entire future can be split according to one immediate event: whether A wins or loses the next dollar. Within each branch the same game restarts from a neighboring fortune. The law of total probability then connects three adjacent whole-game probabilities.

## Name the final event and the first-step events

Let G be the event that A eventually reaches k before zero. Suppose the current fortune is i, with $1\le i\le k-1$. Let W mean that the next play is a win and L that it is a loss. Conditional on starting at i,

$$
P(W\mid i)=p,\qquad P(L\mid i)=q=1-p.
$$

The notation “given i” abbreviates “given that A's current fortune is i.” Our target is $P_i=P(G\mid i)$.

The events W and L partition the possible next plays. Thus $G\cap W$ and $G\cap L$ are two disjoint routes to ultimate success:

$$
P(G\mid i)
=P(G\mid i,W)P(W\mid i)
+P(G\mid i,L)P(L\mid i).
$$

## Identify the conditional probabilities in each branch

After W, A has $i+1$ dollars. Future plays remain independent with the same win probability p, so

$$
P(G\mid i,W)=P_{i+1}.
$$

After L, A has $i-1$ dollars, giving

$$
P(G\mid i,L)=P_{i-1}.
$$

Substitution yields

$$
\boxed{P_i=pP_{i+1}+qP_{i-1}}.
$$

The products describe joint branches: win next and eventually succeed, or lose next and eventually succeed. A loss on the next play does not necessarily mean losing the entire game; A may recover if the new fortune is still positive.

## Check the recurrence next to a boundary

From fortune 1, an immediate loss is terminal, so

$$
P_1=pP_2+qP_0=pP_2.
$$

From fortune $k-1$, an immediate win is terminal:

$$
P_{k-1}=pP_k+qP_{k-2}=p+qP_{k-2}.
$$

The same recurrence handles both situations once $P_0=0$ and $P_k=1$ are included. There is no need to invent separate formulas for nearly ruined or nearly successful states.

## Solve a small system to see the mechanism

Take $k=3$, $p=3/5$, and $q=2/5$. The two unknown interior probabilities satisfy

$$
P_1=\frac35P_2,\qquad
P_2=\frac35+\frac25P_1.
$$

Substitute the second equation into the first:

$$
P_1=\frac35\left(\frac35+\frac25P_1\right)
=\frac9{25}+\frac6{25}P_1.
$$

Therefore $(19/25)P_1=9/25$, giving $P_1=9/19$ and $P_2=15/19$. Winning a play with probability $3/5$ does not make either eventual probability equal to $3/5$; the initial distance from the stopping boundaries matters.

## Rearrange into differences without skipping the algebra

Because $p+q=1$, replace the left side by $(p+q)P_i$:

$$
pP_i+qP_i=pP_{i+1}+qP_{i-1}.
$$

Move $pP_i$ to the right and $qP_{i-1}$ to the left:

$$
qP_i-qP_{i-1}=pP_{i+1}-pP_i.
$$

Factor and divide by $p>0$:

$$
p(P_{i+1}-P_i)=q(P_i-P_{i-1}),
$$

$$
\boxed{P_{i+1}-P_i=\frac qp(P_i-P_{i-1}).}
$$

This says adjacent increases in the success probability follow a geometric progression. It does not say the success probabilities themselves have constant ratio. The next slide uses that distinction to solve the recurrence.

## Check your understanding

Why is the recurrence a weighted sum rather than $(P_{i+1}+P_{i-1})/2$ in every game?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Use the probabilities of the branches being combined</summary>

The branches have probabilities p and q. They receive equal weights only when $p=q=1/2$. For a biased game, an unweighted average would assign the wrong probability to the next-play outcomes even if both neighboring success probabilities were known correctly.
