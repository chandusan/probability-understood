# Listening history changes the reference group

The slide uses music recommendations to motivate conditional probability: a prediction about whether someone will enjoy a track can use information about their listening history. We will treat this as a hypothetical example of probabilistic prediction, rather than a description of any particular service's algorithm.

## Define a prediction and a piece of evidence

Imagine observing listener–track encounters. For each encounter, record whether the listener enjoyed the proposed track and whether their earlier listening history had a specified feature. Define

$$
L=\{\text{the listener likes the proposed track}\},
$$

$$
H=\{\text{the listener's history has the chosen feature}\}.
$$

The feature could be “frequently listens to jazz.” The unconditional probability $P(L)$ refers to the whole population of encounters being studied. The conditional probability $P(L\mid H)$ refers to the subgroup whose histories satisfy $H$.

Both the population and the feature must be specified. “The chance that somebody likes music” is too vague to supply a numerical probability.

## A small hypothetical dataset makes the adjustment concrete

Suppose we select an encounter uniformly from the following **invented 1,000-encounter dataset**:

| Listening history | Liked track | Did not like track | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Has feature $H$ | 120 | 80 | 200 |
| Does not have feature $H$ | 280 | 520 | 800 |
| Total | 400 | 600 | 1,000 |

Without using the history information, the empirical probability of liking is

$$P(L)=\frac{400}{1000}=0.40.$$

Knowing $H$ tells us to consider the 200 encounters in the first row. Of those, 120 produced a liked track, so

$$
\boxed{P(L\mid H)=\frac{120}{200}=0.60.}
$$

Using the probability formula gives the same result:

$$
P(L\mid H)=\frac{P(L\cap H)}{P(H)}
=\frac{120/1000}{200/1000}.
$$

The numerator counts encounters satisfying both the evidence and the predicted outcome. The denominator counts **all** encounters satisfying the evidence, including the 80 dislikes.

## “Restricting the universe” does not guarantee a liked song

The slide describes a universe shrinking to songs that fit a taste profile. The precise probability idea is to restrict attention to outcomes compatible with the known history. A listener with that history can still dislike a proposed track: the table has 80 such cases.

More information can change the probabilities assigned to possible outcomes without making every unfavored outcome impossible. A recommendation system might additionally choose a different track, but that is a decision based on the prediction, not the definition of conditional probability itself.

Here the evidence raises the probability from $0.40$ to $0.60$. Different evidence could lower it, and irrelevant evidence could leave it unchanged. Conditioning has no rule that the answer must increase.

## Keep the direction of the question straight

The reversed conditional is

$$P(H\mid L)=\frac{120}{400}=0.30.$$

It asks what fraction of the liked-track encounters came from listeners with the history feature. It is not the prediction $P(L\mid H)=0.60$. The numerator is the same overlap, but the denominator and the question are different.

These observed proportions also do not establish that changing someone's listening history would cause them to like the track. Conditional probability describes a relationship under the given data or model; a causal claim needs additional reasoning.
