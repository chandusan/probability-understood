# Close the geometric sum for a biased game

When p and q differ, the adjacent changes in the winning probability form a nonconstant geometric progression. Summing that progression and applying the terminal boundary gives the closed formula. Keeping the numerator and denominator derivations visible makes the powers and signs easier to check.

## Begin with the sum already obtained

Let $\rho=q/p$, and assume $p\ne1/2$, so $\rho\ne1$. From the difference recurrence and $P_0=0$,

$$
P_i=P_1(1+\rho+\rho^2+\cdots+\rho^{i-1}).
$$

There are i terms because we add i changes while moving from fortune zero to fortune i. The remaining unknown $P_1$ sets the overall scale.

## Derive the geometric-series formula

Write $S_i=1+\rho+\cdots+\rho^{i-1}$. Multiplying by $\rho$ shifts every exponent upward:

$$
\rho S_i=\rho+\rho^2+\cdots+\rho^i.
$$

Subtract the second expression from the first. Every intermediate power cancels:

$$
S_i-\rho S_i=1-\rho^i.
$$

Because $1-\rho\ne0$, division gives

$$
S_i=\frac{1-\rho^i}{1-\rho}.
$$

Therefore

$$
P_i=P_1\frac{1-\rho^i}{1-\rho}.
$$

## Use the upper boundary before cancelling

Substitute $i=k$ and $P_k=1$:

$$
1=P_1\frac{1-\rho^k}{1-\rho}.
$$

Solving for the unknown gives

$$
P_1=\frac{1-\rho}{1-\rho^k}.
$$

Now insert that value into the general expression:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_i
&=\frac{1-\rho}{1-\rho^k}
\frac{1-\rho^i}{1-\rho}\\
&=\boxed{\frac{1-\rho^i}{1-\rho^k}}
=\boxed{\frac{1-((1-p)/p)^i}{1-((1-p)/p)^k}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The power i refers to A's starting fortune; the power k refers to the total money and upper stopping boundary. Substituting $k-i$ in place of k would change the target boundary incorrectly.

## Work a numerical example from beginning to end

Suppose A and B each start with two dollars, so $i=2$ and $k=4$. Let A win each play with probability $p=3/5$, giving $q=2/5$ and $\rho=2/3$.

First, the lower-state probability is

$$
P_1=\frac{1-2/3}{1-(2/3)^4}
=\frac{1/3}{65/81}=\frac{27}{65}.
$$

Then either add the first two differences,

$$
P_2=P_1(1+\rho)=\frac{27}{65}\left(1+\frac23\right)=\frac9{13},
$$

or use the final ratio directly:

$$
P_2=\frac{1-4/9}{1-16/81}
=\frac{5/9}{65/81}=\frac9{13}\approx0.692308.
$$

This agrees with the two-play win-win, lose-lose, or return-to-start calculation from the opening example.

## Check signs when A is disadvantaged

If $p<1/2$, then $\rho>1$. Both $1-\rho^i$ and $1-\rho^k$ are negative for positive i, so their ratio is positive. Multiplying numerator and denominator by minus one gives the equivalent form

$$
P_i=\frac{\rho^i-1}{\rho^k-1}.
$$

For the same $i=2,k=4$, but $p=2/5$, we have $\rho=3/2$:

$$
P_2=\frac{(3/2)^2-1}{(3/2)^4-1}
=\frac{5/4}{65/16}=\frac4{13}.
$$

The probability is below one half, as the unfavorable play probability suggests.

## Check the boundaries and the fair limit

At $i=0$, the numerator is zero. At $i=k$, numerator and denominator agree. For intermediate i, the finite-sum representation makes the probability's range transparent:

$$
P_i=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\rho^j}{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\rho^j}.
$$

As $p\to1/2$, $\rho\to1$ and these finite sums tend to i and k, respectively. Thus the fair limit is $i/k$. Directly putting $\rho=1$ into the closed fraction creates $0/0$, so the fair case must be evaluated through the sums or derived separately.

## Check your understanding

For $i=1,k=2$, does the biased formula reduce to the probability p of winning the next, decisive play?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Factor the denominator</summary>

Yes: $(1-\rho)/(1-\rho^2)=1/(1+\rho)=1/(1+q/p)=p/(p+q)=p$. This checks both the target event and the formula's exponents in the smallest possible game.
