# Mutual independence requires every relevant intersection

With two events, independence requires one product equality. With three or more events, checking pairs alone is insufficient. **Mutual independence** requires the probability of every intersection of two or more distinct events to equal the product of their individual probabilities.

## Start with three events

Events $A,B,C$ are mutually independent when all four equations hold:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A\cap B)&=P(A)P(B),\\
P(A\cap C)&=P(A)P(C),\\
P(B\cap C)&=P(B)P(C),\\
P(A\cap B\cap C)&=P(A)P(B)P(C).
\end{aligned}
$$

The first three equations define **pairwise independence**. Mutual independence additionally requires the triple equation. Checking only the triple equation also would not suffice: it says nothing by itself about whether each pair factors.

On slide 12, three events formed from four coin tosses will pass every pair test but fail the triple test. That example will show exactly where the extra requirement matters.

## Understand the general notation on the source slide

For events $A_1,\ldots,A_n$, choose any $k$ distinct indices $i_1,\ldots,i_k$, with $2\le k\le n$. Mutual independence requires

$$
\boxed{
P\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^k A_{i_j}\right)
=\prod_{j=1}^k P(A_{i_j}).
}
$$

The large intersection symbol means “all of these events occur.” The product symbol means multiply the $k$ corresponding probabilities. The indices specify which events were selected. The condition must hold for **every selection**, not just the first $k$ events on the list.

For four events there are $\binom42=6$ pair checks, $\binom43=4$ triple checks, and one four-event check: 11 equalities in total. We do not list single-event checks because $P(A_i)=P(A_i)$ contains no restriction.

## A direct counting example

Toss three independent fair coins, and let $H_i$ mean the $i$th toss is heads. There are $2^3=8$ equally likely ordered outcomes.

For any one specified toss, four outcomes show heads there, so $P(H_i)=1/2$. For any two specified tosses, fixing both as heads leaves the third free: two outcomes, with probability $2/8=1/4$. Fixing all three leaves just $HHH$, with probability $1/8$.

Thus every pair probability is $(1/2)^2$ and the triple probability is $(1/2)^3$. All required tests pass, so these three events are mutually independent.

This conclusion uses independent tosses to obtain the equally likely outcome model. Fair marginal probabilities alone would not rule out a mechanism making all tosses repeat the first result.

## How to approach a problem about independence

If the task asks you to **check** independence, calculate the probabilities separately from the stated model, then compare the intersection with the product. Starting by setting them equal would assume the conclusion you were asked to test.

If mutual independence is **given**, the product rule is available for every required subcollection. This permits the calculations on the next slide, including the intersections needed for inclusion–exclusion.

Independent events also remain independent when individual events are replaced by their complements. For two events, the reason is visible by subtraction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A\cap B^c)
&=P(A)-P(A\cap B)\\
&=P(A)-P(A)P(B)\\
&=P(A)P(B^c).
\end{aligned}
$$

The same subtraction argument extends to collections. It is what justifies probabilities such as “$A$ occurs, but $B$ and $C$ do not” under mutual independence.

If $P(A)=P(B)=P(C)=1/2$ and every pair intersection has probability $1/4$, what further calculation is required before declaring mutual independence?
