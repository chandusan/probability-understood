# Multiply a first-draw probability by the correct conditional

“Black first and red second” describes one complete path through the experiment. Its probability is the chance of reaching the black-first branch multiplied by the fraction of that branch that then produces red. The conditioning bar tells us which population supplies the second denominator.

## Start from the definition of conditioning

Let $B_1$ mean black on draw 1 and $R_2$ mean red on draw 2. Provided $P(B_1)>0$,

$$P(R_2\mid B_1)=\frac{P(B_1\cap R_2)}{P(B_1)}.$$

Multiply both sides by $P(B_1)$:

$$\boxed{P(B_1\cap R_2)=P(B_1)P(R_2\mid B_1).}$$

This identity does not assume independence. The conditional factor explicitly accounts for the effect of the first draw on the second. Replacing it with $P(R_2)$ would require a separate independence justification, which this without-replacement experiment does not satisfy when both colors are present.

## Identify three different reference groups

With three red balls and two black balls, label them $R_a,R_b,R_c,B_a,B_b$. There are $5\cdot4=20$ ordered pairs of distinct balls.

The first-black event contains eight pairs: two choices for the first black ball, followed by any of the other four balls. Of those eight pairs, six have a red second ball:

$$\begin{aligned}
&(B_a,R_a),\ (B_a,R_b),\ (B_a,R_c),\\
&(B_b,R_a),\ (B_b,R_b),\ (B_b,R_c).
\end{aligned}$$

Consequently,

$$P(B_1)=\frac8{20}=\frac25,$$

$$P(R_2\mid B_1)=\frac68=\frac34,$$

and

$$P(B_1\cap R_2)=\frac6{20}=\frac3{10}.$$

The multiplication rule restores the original reference group:

$$\frac8{20}\cdot\frac68=\frac6{20}.$$

The factor $8/20$ says how much of the whole sample space reaches the branch; $6/8$ says how much of that branch succeeds. Multiplying cancels the intermediate group size.

## Why the conditional probability is not the final answer

The value $3/4$ answers a question asked **after we know the first ball is black**. The value $3/10$ answers a question asked **before either draw**, when obtaining that first black is itself uncertain.

Since $B_1\cap R_2\subseteq B_1$, the joint probability cannot exceed $P(B_1)=2/5$. This immediately rules out $3/4$ as the joint answer. Containment provides a useful check before doing any arithmetic.

## Time order is useful, but not required by the identity

We usually write the multiplication in draw order because the changing inventory makes $P(R_2\mid B_1)$ easy to calculate. The same intersection can also be written

$$P(B_1\cap R_2)=P(R_2)P(B_1\mid R_2).$$

In the example, $P(R_2)=3/5$, and among the 12 ordered pairs ending in red, six begin with black. Thus $P(B_1\mid R_2)=6/12=1/2$, giving $(3/5)(1/2)=3/10$ again.

Conditioning on a later observation describes what that observation tells us about an earlier draw; it does not change the earlier physical event. This reversal is the idea developed by Bayes' rule later in the chapter.
