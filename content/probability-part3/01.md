# Probability after learning something new

Part III asks how a probability should change when we learn something about the outcome. The central move is to make the new information our reference group. From that one idea we will develop conditional probability, independence, multiplication along a sequence of events, total probability, and Bayes' rule.

## Begin with a complete question

A fair six-sided die is rolled once. Before seeing it, the probability of a 2 is $1/6$. Now someone truthfully tells us that the result is even. What is the probability that it is 2, **given this information**?

The remaining possibilities are $\{2,4,6\}$. They were equally likely before the information, and the information excludes all three odd results without favoring one even result over another. Therefore the new probability is $1/3$.

The die has not been rolled again. We are assessing the same outcome using a more informative description of it. Write

$$
P(\text{2}\mid\text{even})=\frac13.
$$

The vertical bar means “given that.” The event to its right tells us which outcomes remain under consideration.

## The denominator records what we know

For events $A$ and $B$, with $P(B)>0$,

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.
$$

The denominator is the probability of the information $B$. The numerator is the part of $B$ where the event we want, $A$, also occurs. For the die, these are $3/6$ and $1/6$, respectively, so their ratio is $1/3$.

Much of this chapter is learning to choose that denominator correctly. “Exactly one,” “at most one,” “a positive result,” and “two red draws” each describe a different reference group. We will write those events explicitly before calculating.

## How the chapter develops

| Main idea | Question it answers | Where it leads |
|---|---|---|
| Conditional probability | Among outcomes satisfying $B$, how often does $A$ occur? | Dice, subscription groups, and retained tags |
| Independence | Does learning $B$ leave the probability of $A$ unchanged? | Distinguishing independent, disjoint, and mutually independent events |
| Multiplication rule | How do we combine successive requirements? | Cards and draws without replacement |
| Total probability | How do we combine several possible routes to an event? | Urns and mixed populations |
| Bayes' rule | How does an observation change the probability of its possible source? | Testing, urn identification, and fraud examples |
| Conditioning on the next step | How can one-step probabilities describe a longer process? | The gambler's ruin problem |

These are connected methods. Multiplication joins events along one route; addition combines disjoint routes; Bayes' rule changes which event serves as the condition.

## Keep three distinctions visible

First, “given” is a statement about information, not necessarily time. We can condition an earlier draw on something observed later. Second, new information can increase, decrease, or leave a probability unchanged. Third, knowing that two events occur together often does not establish that one causes the other.

The calculations will build on the counting and event operations from Parts I and II. The extra habit is to pause and ask: **among which outcomes am I now measuring the probability?**
