# Total probability is a weighted sum across a partition

An event can occur through several different cases. If the cases cover all possibilities and do not overlap, calculate the event's probability inside each case, multiply by the probability of that case, and add. This turns conditional probabilities into an unconditional probability.

## State what a partition requires

Events $A_1,\ldots,A_k$ form a partition of the sample space S when

$$A_i\cap A_j=\varnothing\quad\text{for }i\ne j,
\qquad A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_k=S.$$

The first requirement is mutual exclusivity: an outcome cannot belong to two cases. The second is exhaustiveness: every outcome belongs to some case. Together, they say every outcome has exactly one case label.

In the urn example, first-red and first-black form a partition. In a shipment example, the mutually exclusive cases might identify which of three factories made an item.

Suppose initially that $P(A_i)>0$ for every case so the conditional probabilities are defined, and let B be the event whose probability we want.

## Prove the formula by splitting B

Every outcome in B belongs to exactly one $A_i$. Therefore

$$B=(B\cap A_1)\cup\cdots\cup(B\cap A_k).$$

The intersections are disjoint: an outcome in both $B\cap A_i$ and $B\cap A_j$ would have to belong to both $A_i$ and $A_j$, contradicting the partition.

Additivity gives

$$P(B)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(B\cap A_i).$$

For each intersection, the multiplication rule gives

$$P(B\cap A_i)=P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i).$$

Substituting establishes the law of total probability:

$$\boxed{P(B)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i).}$$

The rule requires no independence. Indeed, it is especially useful when the conditional probability varies substantially between cases.

## Build a numerical example from group sizes

A hypothetical shipment contains items from three factories. Their shares are 50%, 30%, and 20%, and their defect probabilities are 1%, 2%, and 5%, respectively. Let $A_i$ identify the factory and let B mean defective.

| Factory | Shipment share $P(A_i)$ | Defect rate $P(B\mid A_i)$ | Joint share $P(A_i\cap B)$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $0.50$ | $0.01$ | $0.005$ |
| 2 | $0.30$ | $0.02$ | $0.006$ |
| 3 | $0.20$ | $0.05$ | $0.010$ |

Thus

$$P(B)=(0.50)(0.01)+(0.30)(0.02)+(0.20)(0.05)
=0.021.$$

The overall defect probability is **2.1%**. In a model population of 10,000 items, the factory groups have sizes 5,000, 3,000, and 2,000. Applying their rates yields 50, 60, and 100 defective items: 210 out of 10,000 in total.

These scaled counts represent the stated probability model, not a guarantee about the exact counts in any randomly sampled shipment.

## Why an ordinary average would be wrong

The unweighted average of the three rates is $(1\%+2\%+5\%)/3\approx2.67\%$. That calculation gives each factory one-third of the weight, contradicting the shipment shares.

The correct weights are nonnegative and sum to one. Consequently the overall rate lies between the smallest and largest conditional rates: here between 1% and 5%. This is a quick check, although being within those bounds does not by itself establish the correct answer.

## Handle empty-probability cases carefully

If a partition case has probability zero, its intersection with B also has probability zero. Omit that case from the conditional-probability sum rather than write a ratio conditioned on a zero-probability event. Its contribution to the unconditional total is still zero.

## Check your understanding

Why can we not use “item is red” and “item is large” as the two cases without further information?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Check both partition conditions</summary>

An item might be both red and large, so the cases may overlap. It might be neither, so they may fail to cover the sample space. Refine them into red-and-large, red-and-not-large, not-red-and-large, and neither. Those four membership patterns form a partition, and total probability can then be applied using their probabilities and conditional rates.
