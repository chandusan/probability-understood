# Red on the second draw: add the two possible histories

To find the unconditional chance of red second, account for every way the first draw could have happened. Red first and black first lead to different remaining inventories. Weight each second-draw probability by the chance of reaching its first-draw branch, then add the disjoint paths.

## Partition the target event

Let $R_i$ mean red on draw $i$, and let $B_i$ mean black on draw $i$. The first ball has exactly one color, so

$$R_2=(R_1\cap R_2)\cup(B_1\cap R_2).$$

The two sets on the right do not overlap. Hence

$$P(R_2)=P(R_1\cap R_2)+P(B_1\cap R_2).$$

This is addition across alternative histories, not multiplication of those histories. We multiply along each path and add across the two paths.

## Calculate each complete path

With $N=r+b$ and both colors present, the RR path has probability

$$P(R_1\cap R_2)=P(R_1)P(R_2\mid R_1)
=\frac rN\frac{r-1}{N-1}.$$

The BR path has probability

$$P(B_1\cap R_2)=P(B_1)P(R_2\mid B_1)
=\frac bN\frac r{N-1}.$$

Adding and showing each simplification,

$$\begin{aligned}
P(R_2)
&=\frac{r(r-1)}{N(N-1)}+\frac{br}{N(N-1)}\\
&=\frac{r[(r-1)+b]}{N(N-1)}\\
&=\frac{r(N-1)}{N(N-1)}\\
&=\boxed{\frac rN}.
\end{aligned}$$

The denominator $N(N-1)$ describes ordered pairs. The two numerators count the pairs ending in red, separated according to their first color.

## Correct an extra equality on the source slide

The source's first-path line includes a stray “$=P(R_1)$” between the multiplication rule and the product of fractions. That equality is generally false: two reds is a more restrictive event than red first.

The valid line is

$$P(R_1\cap R_2)=P(R_1)P(R_2\mid R_1)
=\frac rN\frac{r-1}{N-1}.$$

For three red and two black balls, $P(R_1)=3/5$ while $P(R_1\cap R_2)=3/10$. The rest of the source's combined calculation correctly leads to $r/N$.

## Why the conditional changes but the unconditional does not

In that same three-red, two-black urn,

$$P(R_2\mid R_1)=\frac24=\frac12,
\qquad P(R_2\mid B_1)=\frac34.$$

The first red branch occurs with probability $3/5$, while the first black branch occurs with probability $2/5$. The weighted average is

$$\frac35\cdot\frac12+\frac25\cdot\frac34=\frac35.$$

The ordinary average $(1/2+3/4)/2=5/8$ would be wrong because the branches do not occur equally often. The weights $3/5$ and $2/5$ describe their actual shares of the experiment.

## Verify the result by a direct count

Choose the second ball first in your counting argument. There are $r$ choices that make it red. After fixing that particular ball, any of the other $N-1$ balls may occupy the first position. Thus the target contains $r(N-1)$ ordered pairs out of $N(N-1)$, giving $r/N$ again.

Counting positions in this order does not change how the physical experiment is conducted. It is simply another way to enumerate its possible outcomes.

## Check your understanding

Does $P(R_2)=P(R_1)$ establish independence of the two draws?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Compare a conditional probability with its marginal</summary>

No. For $0<r<N$, $P(R_2\mid R_1)=(r-1)/(N-1)$ differs from $P(R_2)=r/N$. Equality of the two marginal probabilities only says each position has the same red probability before any observation. Independence would require learning the first color to leave the second probability unchanged.
