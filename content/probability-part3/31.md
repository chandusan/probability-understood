# Two red draws change the urn probabilities and the next draw

Choose one of two urns with equal probability, then keep drawing from that same urn without replacement. Seeing two red balls gives information about which urn was chosen and removes two red balls from it. Predicting the third draw requires both changes.

## State the whole experiment

Urn A contains 12 red and 8 black balls; urn B contains 4 red and 16 black balls. Both contain 20 balls. Choose the urn once, with probability $1/2$ for each, and draw uniformly among its remaining balls at each step.

Let A and B also denote the events that the corresponding urn was chosen. Let $R_j$ mean that draw j is red, and write $E=R_1\cap R_2$ for the observed evidence. The two questions are

$$
\text{(a) }P(A\mid E),\qquad
\text{(b) }P(R_3\mid E).
$$

The vertical bar means “given that.” The first question asks about the hidden source; the second asks about a future outcome.

## Part (a): calculate how each urn could produce the evidence

If A was chosen, the first red has probability $12/20$. After that red is removed, 11 red balls remain among 19 balls. Therefore

$$
P(E\mid A)
=P(R_1\mid A)P(R_2\mid A,R_1)
=\frac{12}{20}\frac{11}{19}
=\frac{132}{380}.
$$

For B, the corresponding calculation is

$$
P(E\mid B)=\frac4{20}\frac3{19}=\frac{12}{380}.
$$

These are likelihoods: probabilities of the observed two reds under each possible urn. They are not yet probabilities of the urns given the evidence.

Multiply each likelihood by its prior probability, then normalize:

| Chosen urn | Prior | Probability of two reds given that urn | Joint probability of urn and two reds |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | $1/2$ | $132/380$ | $132/760$ |
| B | $1/2$ | $12/380$ | $12/760$ |

The two routes are disjoint and exhaust all ways to observe E, so

$$
P(E)=\frac{132+12}{760}=\frac{18}{95}.
$$

Bayes' rule now gives

$$
\boxed{P(A\mid E)=\frac{132/760}{144/760}=\frac{11}{12}},
\qquad P(B\mid E)=\frac1{12}.
$$

The evidence is eleven times as likely under A as under B. With equal prior weights, the posterior odds are consequently 11 to 1.

## Part (b): update the contents as well as the weights

Whichever urn was chosen, the two observed red balls are gone:

| Possible urn | Remaining red | Remaining black | Conditional probability of a third red |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 10 | 8 | $P(R_3\mid A,E)=10/18$ |
| B | 2 | 16 | $P(R_3\mid B,E)=2/18$ |

Within the evidence E, A and B are still disjoint possibilities. Use their **posterior** weights in the conditional law of total probability:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(R_3\mid E)
&=P(R_3\mid A,E)P(A\mid E)
+P(R_3\mid B,E)P(B\mid E)\\
&=\frac{10}{18}\frac{11}{12}
+\frac2{18}\frac1{12}\\
&=\frac{112}{216}
=\boxed{\frac{14}{27}\approx0.518519}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is about $51.85\%$, while the probability that the urn is A is about $91.67\%$. Identifying the likely urn and predicting a red ball are different questions: even A has eight black balls remaining.

## Check your understanding

If the urn's identity were revealed as A after the first two reds, what would be the probability of a third red?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Condition on the newly revealed information</summary>

It would be $P(R_3\mid A,E)=10/18=5/9$. There would be no need to average across urns, because A would be known. The original answer $14/27$ is slightly lower because it retains a $1/12$ chance of B, whose remaining red fraction is only $2/18$.
