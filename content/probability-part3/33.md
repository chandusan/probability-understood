# Normalize the two routes to the observed reds

The evidence could have come from either urn. Bayes' denominator adds every route capable of producing that evidence, and the numerator keeps the route through the urn of interest. The result is a probability distribution over urns after observing the two red draws.

## First calculate the probability of the evidence

Let $E=R_1\cap R_2$, with prior probabilities $P(A)=P(B)=1/2$. The known-urn likelihoods are

$$
P(E\mid A)=\frac{132}{380},\qquad
P(E\mid B)=\frac{12}{380}.
$$

The events $A\cap E$ and $B\cap E$ are disjoint: one experiment uses only one urn. Their union is E. The multiplication rule and addition rule therefore give

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(E)
&=P(A\cap E)+P(B\cap E)\\
&=P(A)P(E\mid A)+P(B)P(E\mid B)\\
&=\frac12\frac{132}{380}+\frac12\frac{12}{380}\\
&=\frac{144}{760}=\frac{18}{95}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each product is a complete route through the experiment: select that urn, then draw two reds. Adding the likelihoods alone would omit the probabilities of selecting the urns.

## Restrict the denominator to experiments matching the evidence

Conditional probability now says

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A\mid E)
&=\frac{P(A\cap E)}{P(E)}\\
&=\frac{132/760}{144/760}
=\frac{132}{144}
=\boxed{\frac{11}{12}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Likewise,

$$
P(B\mid E)=\frac{12}{144}=\frac1{12}.
$$

The denominator 144 accounts for all observed red-red routes; the numerator 132 counts those among them arising from A. The fractions add to one, as probabilities of the two possible urns should.

## See Bayes' rule as a literal counting ratio

Label the balls distinctly. Since the urn priors are equal and both urns contain 20 balls, every outcome of the form “chosen urn, first labeled ball, second labeled ball” has probability

$$
\frac12\frac1{20}\frac1{19}=\frac1{760}.
$$

There are 760 elementary outcomes. Exactly $12\cdot11=132$ are red-red from A, and $4\cdot3=12$ are red-red from B. Conditioning on E retains these 144 outcomes. Of those, 132 identify A. This is the same derivation without any unnamed normalizing constant.

If the urn priors were unequal, these elementary outcomes would have different weights across urns. Bayes' weighted formula would still work, but the unweighted count ratio would require adjustment.

## Update one observation at a time

After only the first red,

$$
P(A\mid R_1)
=\frac{(1/2)(12/20)}{(1/2)(12/20)+(1/2)(4/20)}
=\frac34.
$$

After that draw, the next-red probabilities are $11/19$ under A and $3/19$ under B. Treat $3/4$ and $1/4$ as the updated urn weights:

$$
P(A\mid R_1,R_2)
=\frac{(3/4)(11/19)}{(3/4)(11/19)+(1/4)(3/19)}
=\frac{33}{36}=\frac{11}{12}.
$$

The one-step and two-step updates agree because they condition on exactly the same final evidence. In the sequential route, both the urn weights and the remaining-ball counts must advance after the first observation.

## Check your understanding

Would the posterior probability of A still be $11/12$ if it had only a $1/10$ chance of being selected initially?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Retain the priors while normalizing</summary>

No. The common likelihood denominator cancels, but the priors remain:

$$
P(A\mid E)=\frac{(1/10)132}{(1/10)132+(9/10)12}
=\frac{132}{240}=\frac{11}{20}.
$$

The evidence still favors A by a likelihood ratio of 11, but it begins with prior odds of 1 to 9. Posterior odds are 11 to 9, giving posterior probability $11/20$.
