# Update the urn before writing the second fraction

The second-draw denominator is the number of balls remaining after the first draw. The numerator is the number of the required color still present. For black followed by red, removing a black decreases the total by one while leaving the red count unchanged.

## Keep an inventory table

Write $N=r+b$. The relevant inventories are:

| Information available | Red balls left | Black balls left | Total left |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Before any draw | $r$ | $b$ | $N$ |
| First draw was black | $r$ | $b-1$ | $N-1$ |
| First draw was red | $r-1$ | $b$ | $N-1$ |

The second row applies to the path requested in part (a). It gives

$$P(B_1)=\frac bN,\qquad
P(R_2\mid B_1)=\frac r{N-1}.$$

Therefore,

$$\boxed{P(B_1\cap R_2)=\frac{br}{(r+b)(r+b-1)}.}$$

Every symbol now has a counting interpretation: choose one of $b$ black balls, then one of $r$ red balls, from a sample space with $N(N-1)$ equally likely ordered choices.

## Read the source's remaining total correctly

The prose describing the remaining total on the original slide is clipped at the right edge. The correct total is **$r+b-1$**, as shown in its displayed probability formula. One ball was removed, and none was added. Writing the inventory explicitly avoids relying on an incomplete text line.

This also prevents a common numerator error. Because the removed ball was black, the red numerator stays $r$. The expression $(r-1)/(r+b-1)$ belongs to the different condition that the first ball was red.

## Work the factors with actual balls

Suppose the urn contains four red balls and three black balls. Then

$$P(B_1)=\frac37,$$

and a first black leaves four red and two black balls, so

$$P(R_2\mid B_1)=\frac46.$$

The requested joint probability is

$$\frac37\cdot\frac46=\frac{12}{42}=\frac27.$$

The direct count is 12 favorable pairs: each of three black balls can be followed by any of four red balls. There are $7\cdot6=42$ possible ordered pairs in total.

## Compare sampling with and without replacement

If the first ball were replaced, the second draw would again see all seven balls, giving

$$P(\text{black then red, with replacement})=\frac37\cdot\frac47=\frac{12}{49}.$$

Without replacement, removing a black makes red a larger fraction of the remaining urn, so black-then-red is more likely: $12/42>12/49$.

For two reds, the effect goes the other way. Without replacement the probability is $(4/7)(3/6)=2/7$; with replacement it is $(4/7)^2=16/49$. A first red depletes the color required for the second draw.

Thus “without replacement” does not simply increase or decrease every probability. Its effect depends on which first outcome occurred and which later event is being requested.

## Separate a valid zero from an undefined conditional

If $b=0$, black-then-red has probability zero. However, $P(R_2\mid B_1)$ is undefined by the elementary ratio definition because the conditioning event has probability zero. In that case use the direct favorable count $br=0$, rather than pretending an impossible branch has an ordinary conditional probability.
