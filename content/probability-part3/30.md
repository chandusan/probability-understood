# Update every category with the same evidence denominator

Bayes' theorem extends from two possible groups to any finite or countable partition. For each category, multiply its prior probability by the likelihood of the observation. Then divide every resulting contribution by their common sum to obtain posterior probabilities that add to one.

## State the partition and the observation

Let $A_1,\ldots,A_k$ be mutually exclusive and exhaustive events. Every outcome belongs to exactly one category, and

$$\sum_{j=1}^{k}P(A_j)=1.$$

Let B be the observed event, with $P(B)>0$. For each category having positive prior probability, suppose we know $P(B\mid A_j)$.

The general finite formula is

$$\boxed{
P(A_i\mid B)=
\frac{P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)}
{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}P(B\mid A_j)P(A_j)}.}$$

The index i selects the category whose posterior we want. The index j runs through **all** categories in the denominator, including category i.

## Derive every part of the ratio

The definition of conditioning gives

$$P(A_i\mid B)=\frac{P(A_i\cap B)}{P(B)}.$$

The numerator is one joint contribution:

$$P(A_i\cap B)=P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i).$$

The intersections $A_j\cap B$ partition B, so total probability supplies

$$P(B)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}P(A_j\cap B)
=\sum_{j=1}^{k}P(A_j)P(B\mid A_j).$$

Substituting proves the formula. Summing its right side over i yields one because the summed numerators equal the denominator. This confirms that the result is a probability distribution over the categories.

## Work a three-factory example

Suppose factories 1, 2, and 3 supply 50%, 30%, and 20% of a shipment. Their defect probabilities are 1%, 2%, and 5%. An item is selected uniformly from the shipment and is found defective. Which factory made it?

Let $A_i$ identify the factory and let B mean defective. The joint contributions are

$$w_1=(0.50)(0.01)=0.005,$$

$$w_2=(0.30)(0.02)=0.006,$$

$$w_3=(0.20)(0.05)=0.010.$$

Their sum is $P(B)=0.021$. Therefore:

| Factory | Defective contribution | Posterior given defective |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | $0.005$ | $0.005/0.021=5/21\approx23.81\%$ |
| 2 | $0.006$ | $0.006/0.021=6/21\approx28.57\%$ |
| 3 | $0.010$ | $0.010/0.021=10/21\approx47.62\%$ |

In a model population of 10,000 items, the factories contribute 50, 60, and 100 defective items. Restricting to the 210 defective items gives the same fractions $50/210$, $60/210$, and $100/210$.

Factory 3 has the largest posterior even though it supplies the smallest share of the shipment. Its higher defect rate produces the largest joint defective contribution. Neither prior share alone nor defect rate alone determines the answer; their product does.

## Understand the source's infinite sum

The source writes a denominator summed from $j=1$ to infinity. This covers a **countably infinite** partition $A_1,A_2,\ldots$. The same proof uses countable additivity, and the nonnegative joint contributions sum to $P(B)$.

For a finite problem, stop the sum at the number of categories. Categories with prior probability zero have posterior probability zero for positive-probability B and can be omitted from expressions involving conditional rates. If every joint contribution is zero, then $P(B)=0$ and the displayed ratio is undefined under this model.

## Interpret the unknown category

Here we can define a categorical variable I with $A_i=\{I=i\}$. Before the observation its probabilities are the priors; afterward they are the posteriors. The source's reference to an “indicator” points to identifying which category occurred. An individual indicator $\mathbf1_{A_i}$ is binary, whereas I can take many category labels.

This is a discrete illustration of Bayesian updating. It does not require every Bayesian parameter in other problems to be a category or an indicator.

If every factory had the same defect probability q, with $q>0$, what would a defect tell us about the source factory?
