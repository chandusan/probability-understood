# Update the prior, keeping the stated test sensitivity

Bayes' rule changes the disease probability from the population prevalence to the probability within the positive-test group. The source's calculation switches sensitivity from 96% to 95%; using the stated 96% throughout gives a posterior of $16/349$, approximately 4.5845%.

## Correct the inconsistent numerical input

Slides 27 and 28 specify

$$P(D)=0.001,\qquad P(+\mid D)=0.96,
\qquad P(+\mid D^c)=0.02.$$

The original slide 29 substitutes **0.95** in both the numerator and denominator. That is inconsistent with the problem statement. The correct substitution is

$$\begin{aligned}
P(D\mid +)
&=\frac{P(+\mid D)P(D)}
{P(+\mid D)P(D)+P(+\mid D^c)P(D^c)}\\
&=\frac{0.96(0.001)}{0.96(0.001)+0.02(0.999)}\\
&=\frac{0.00096}{0.02094}
=\boxed{\frac{16}{349}\approx0.04584527}.
\end{aligned}$$

The complementary posterior is

$$P(D^c\mid +)=1-\frac{16}{349}=\frac{333}{349}\approx0.95415473.$$

For comparison, using 95% sensitivity would give $95/(95+1998)\approx0.0453894$. That explains the source's roughly 0.045 figure, but it solves a slightly different numerical problem.

## Identify prior, likelihood, and posterior

The **prior** describes disease status before incorporating this test result. The **likelihoods** describe how compatible a positive result is with each disease status. The **posterior** describes disease status after restricting to positive results.

| Status | Prior | Positive-result likelihood | Posterior after positive |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Disease | $0.001$ | $0.96$ | $16/349\approx0.0458453$ |
| No disease | $0.999$ | $0.02$ | $333/349\approx0.9541547$ |

The prior and posterior columns each sum to one. The likelihood column need not: its entries are probabilities within different groups.

The positive result supplies evidence for disease because a positive is much more likely in the disease group than in the no-disease group. The no-disease group nevertheless contributes more positive cases because it is initially 999 times as large.

## Express the update using odds

Prior odds of disease to no disease are

$$\frac{P(D)}{P(D^c)}=\frac1{999}.$$

Divide the two Bayes formulas. Their common evidence denominator cancels:

$$\frac{P(D\mid +)}{P(D^c\mid +)}
=\frac{P(+\mid D)}{P(+\mid D^c)}
\frac{P(D)}{P(D^c)}.$$

The likelihood ratio is $0.96/0.02=48$. Hence posterior odds are

$$48\cdot\frac1{999}=\frac{16}{333}.$$

An odds ratio of 16 to 333 corresponds to probability $16/(16+333)=16/349$. This explains both the strong increase in support for disease and why the posterior remains below one-half. The likelihood ratio multiplies **odds**, not the probability itself.

## Change the prevalence while holding test rates fixed

If the prior disease probability is p, the same model gives

$$P(D\mid +)=\frac{0.96p}{0.96p+0.02(1-p)}.$$

At $p=0.001$ the posterior is about 4.58%. At $p=0.01$ it is about 32.65%. At $p=0.10$ it is about 84.21%. These differences arise from the sizes of the groups supplying true and false positives, even though sensitivity and specificity remain unchanged.
