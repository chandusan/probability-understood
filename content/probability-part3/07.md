# Independence means the joint probability factors

Two events are independent when their joint probability equals the product of their individual probabilities. When the relevant conditional probabilities are defined, this is equivalent to saying that learning one event occurred leaves the probability of the other unchanged. Independence is a precise property of the probability model, not just an impression that two descriptions sound unrelated.

## Begin with the definition

Events $A$ and $B$ are independent if and only if

$$\boxed{P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).}$$

This definition applies even when one event has probability zero. The conditional formulations require positive denominators. If $P(B)>0$, then

$$
P(A\mid B)
=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}
=\frac{P(A)P(B)}{P(B)}
=P(A).
$$

Conversely, if $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$, multiplying by $P(B)$ gives the product definition. Similarly, when $P(A)>0$,

$$P(B\mid A)=P(B).$$

Thus neither event provides information that changes the probability of the other. The two directions express the same independence property whenever their conditional probabilities exist.

## Check an example by counting

Toss two independent fair coins. The four equally likely outcomes are

$$S=\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}.$$

Let $A$ mean the first toss is heads, and $B$ mean the second toss is heads. Then

$$A=\{HH,HT\},\qquad B=\{HH,TH\},\qquad A\cap B=\{HH\}.$$

Therefore

$$P(A)=\frac12,\quad P(B)=\frac12,\quad P(A\cap B)=\frac14.$$

Since $1/4=(1/2)(1/2)$, the events are independent. Conditioning gives the same conclusion: within $B=\{HH,TH\}$, exactly one of the two equally likely outcomes is also in $A$.

## Events from one experiment can also be independent

Roll one fair die. Let $C=\{2,4,6\}$ mean “even” and $D=\{3,6\}$ mean “a multiple of 3.” Their intersection is $\{6\}$, so

$$P(C\cap D)=\frac16=\frac36\cdot\frac26=P(C)P(D).$$

They are independent even though both describe the same roll. Among all six results, half are even; among the multiples of 3, $\{3,6\}$, half are still even. Independence concerns the probabilities of events, not whether they came from separate physical objects.

By contrast, if $E=\{2\}$, then $P(C\mid E)=1$, rather than $1/2$. Learning the result is 2 guarantees evenness, so $C$ and $E$ are dependent.

## Zero-probability and certain events

If $P(A)=0$, then $A\cap B\subseteq A$ forces $P(A\cap B)=0=P(A)P(B)$. Such an event is independent of every event by the product definition, although $P(B\mid A)$ is not defined by the elementary ratio.

Similarly, a probability-one event is independent of every event. These endpoint cases explain why the product definition is more general than stating two conditional equalities without qualifications.

## Check your understanding

Suppose $P(A)=0.4$, $P(B)=0.5$, and $P(A\cap B)=0.3$. Are $A$ and $B$ independent?

No: $P(A)P(B)=0.20$, which differs from $0.30$. Equivalently, $P(A\mid B)=0.30/0.50=0.60$, while $P(A)=0.40$. Knowing $B$ raises the probability of $A$ in this model.
