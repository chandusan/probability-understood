# Gambler's ruin: win the whole game before reaching zero

Each play moves one dollar from one gambler to the other. The question concerns the chance of eventually collecting all the money, not the chance of winning the next play. Conditioning on that next play converts the potentially long game into a relation between neighboring starting fortunes.

## Write down the model and the event

There are k dollars in total. Gambler A starts with i dollars and gambler B with $k-i$, where $1\le i\le k-1$. On every play, independently of previous plays,

- A wins one dollar with probability p.
- A loses one dollar with probability $q=1-p$.

Assume $0<p<1$. The total k stays fixed. Play stops as soon as A's fortune reaches either 0 or k. Reaching k means A has won all B's money; reaching 0 means A is ruined.

Define

$$
P_i=P(\text{A reaches }k\text{ before }0\mid\text{A starts with }i).
$$

The subscript i specifies the starting fortune, not the number of plays. The endpoint probabilities are $P_0=0$ and $P_k=1$.

## Follow the first play into a smaller version of the same question

If A wins the next play, the new fortune is $i+1$ and the remaining success probability is $P_{i+1}$. If A loses it, the fortune is $i-1$ and the remaining probability is $P_{i-1}$.

These two first-play outcomes are disjoint and exhaustive, so

$$
\boxed{P_i=pP_{i+1}+qP_{i-1}},\qquad1\le i\le k-1.
$$

For example, with five dollars total and two dollars initially,

$$
P_2=pP_3+qP_1.
$$

This does not yet solve for $P_2$, because the neighboring probabilities are unknown too. Together with the boundary conditions, however, the relations form a finite system that determines every starting state's success probability.

## Solve a small case directly

Take $k=4$, $i=2$, and $p=3/5$, so $q=2/5$. Look at the next two plays. From fortune 2:

| Two-play outcome | Probability | Result |
|---|---:|---|
| Win, win | $p^2$ | Reach 4 and win |
| Lose, lose | $q^2$ | Reach 0 and lose |
| Win, lose | $pq$ | Return to 2 |
| Lose, win | $qp$ | Return to 2 |

The last two cases restart the same problem. Thus

$$
P_2=p^2+2pqP_2,
$$

and solving gives

$$
P_2=\frac{p^2}{1-2pq}
=\frac{p^2}{p^2+q^2}
=\frac{9/25}{9/25+4/25}
=\boxed{\frac9{13}}.
$$

All four paths have been accounted for with their actual probabilities. When p differs from $1/2$, simply counting paths without their weights would be incorrect.

## The general answer we will derive

The following slides solve the first-step recurrence by examining successive differences. The result is

$$
P_i=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1-(q/p)^i}{1-(q/p)^k},&p\ne\tfrac12,\\[8pt]
\dfrac{i}{k},&p=\tfrac12.
\end{cases}
$$

The fair-game formula depends on the share of the money initially held. A fair next play does not make the probability of winning the whole game $1/2$ for every starting fortune.

## Does the game eventually stop?

Yes, with probability one under this finite model. From any interior fortune, a sufficiently long uninterrupted run of wins reaches k. Such a run has a positive probability in every fixed-length block of future plays. The chance of avoiding absorption through more and more blocks tends to zero; the boundary-condition lesson will make that argument precise. This does not impose a fixed maximum game length.
