# An alternative solution uses characteristic roots

The preceding difference-and-sum method already gives the ruin probabilities. This slide offers an optional algebraic route: find simple sequences that satisfy the recurrence, combine them, and choose the combination that meets the two boundary conditions. The method explains why constants and geometric powers appear together.

## Rewrite the recurrence as a homogeneous equation

Starting from

$$
P_i=pP_{i+1}+qP_{i-1},
$$

move all terms to one side:

$$
pP_{i+1}-P_i+qP_{i-1}=0.
$$

The coefficients p and q do not depend on i. This suggests trying a geometric sequence, because shifting its index simply multiplies it by a constant.

Use the trial sequence $u_i=r^i$. We are looking for building blocks; we have not assumed that the final probability must consist of just one power.

## Substitute the trial sequence and factor

Substitution gives

$$
pr^{i+1}-r^i+qr^{i-1}=0.
$$

For $r\ne0$, divide by $r^{i-1}$ to obtain the characteristic equation

$$
pr^2-r+q=0.
$$

Because $p+q=1$, the middle term can be rewritten and factored:

$$
\begin{aligned}
pr^2-r+q
&=pr^2-(p+q)r+q\\
&=pr(r-1)-q(r-1)\\
&=(pr-q)(r-1).
\end{aligned}
$$

The two roots are $r=1$ and $r=q/p$. They are both nonzero since $p,q>0$.

## Combine the two solutions when the roots differ

Write $\rho=q/p$. If $p\ne1/2$, then $\rho\ne1$, and both sequences $1$ and $\rho^i$ satisfy the recurrence. Any linear combination does too:

$$
P_i=C+D\rho^i.
$$

The slide writes its constants as A and B. We use C and D to avoid confusing the constants with the two gamblers.

Why does this cover every solution? Since $p>0$, the recurrence can be written

$$
P_{i+1}=\frac{P_i-qP_{i-1}}p.
$$

Thus $P_0$ and $P_1$ determine all later terms. The two distinct-root sequences let us match any prescribed first two values by solving $C+D=P_0$ and $C+D\rho=P_1$. Their difference gives $D(\rho-1)=P_1-P_0$, which has a unique solution because $\rho\ne1$.

## Impose the boundary values

The lower boundary gives $0=P_0=C+D$, so $C=-D$. The upper boundary gives

$$
1=P_k=C+D\rho^k=D(\rho^k-1).
$$

Therefore $D=1/(\rho^k-1)$ and

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_i
&=D(\rho^i-1)\\
&=\frac{\rho^i-1}{\rho^k-1}
=\boxed{\frac{1-\rho^i}{1-\rho^k}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The constants C and D need not themselves lie between zero and one; they are coefficients, not probabilities. The completed expression is the probability.

For example, with $p=2/3$ and $k=4$, $\rho=1/2$, so $C=16/15$ and $D=-16/15$. Starting from two dollars gives

$$
P_2=\frac{16}{15}\left(1-\frac14\right)=\frac45,
$$

agreeing with the previous slide's cumulative-difference table.

## Handle the repeated root separately

If $p=q=1/2$, both characteristic roots equal one. Writing $C+D1^i$ would produce only constant sequences, so it cannot satisfy both $P_0=0$ and $P_k=1$.

In this case the sequences 1 and i both satisfy the recurrence: $i=\tfrac12(i+1)+\tfrac12(i-1)$. Their combination is $P_i=C+Di$. The boundaries force $C=0$ and $D=1/k$, giving $P_i=i/k$. The equal-differences derivation on the next fair-game slide reaches the same result directly.

## Check your understanding

Why is trying $P_i=r^i$ useful even though the final answer is a difference of powers?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Distinguish building blocks from the final boundary-value solution</summary>

The trial finds sequences preserved by the recurrence. The recurrence is linear, so combinations of those sequences remain solutions. The boundary conditions select the required combination. A single nonzero power has value one at $i=0$, so by itself it could not satisfy the required lower boundary $P_0=0$.

</details>
