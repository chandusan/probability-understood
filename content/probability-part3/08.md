# The same fraction inside B as in the whole sample space

The source picture makes independence visible using a grid of 100 equally likely cells. Event $A$ occupies half the grid, event $B$ occupies four tenths, and their intersection occupies two tenths. Inside $B$, the fraction belonging to $A$ is still one half—the same fraction as before conditioning.

## Turn the grid into a probability experiment

Choose one of the 100 cells uniformly. Label a cell by its column and row, each from 1 through 10. Define

$$A=\{\text{cell is in columns 1 through 5}\},$$

$$B=\{\text{cell is in rows 1 through 4}\}.$$

There are five choices of column and ten choices of row for $A$, giving 50 cells. For $B$, there are ten choices of column and four choices of row, giving 40. Both conditions hold in the $5\times4$ lower-left rectangle, containing 20 cells.

Thus

$$P(A)=\frac{50}{100}=0.5,\quad
P(B)=\frac{40}{100}=0.4,\quad
P(A\cap B)=\frac{20}{100}=0.2.$$

These match the values printed on the slide.

## Compare the right ratios

Before receiving any information, 50 of all 100 cells belong to $A$:

$$P(A)=\frac{50}{100}=\frac12.$$

After learning $B$, we restrict attention to its 40 cells. Of those, 20 belong to $A$:

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{20}{40}=\frac12.$$

The denominator changed from 100 to 40, and the favorable count changed from 50 to 20. They changed in the same proportion, leaving the probability unchanged.

In probability notation,

$$
\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}
=\frac{0.2}{0.4}
=0.5
=\frac{P(A)}{P(S)}.
$$

The slide's $P(S)$ in the final denominator equals 1. This expression emphasizes the comparison: $A$'s proportion within $B$ equals its proportion within the whole space.

## Verify independence in both directions

The product test gives

$$P(A)P(B)=0.5\cdot0.4=0.2=P(A\cap B).$$

Conditioning in the reverse direction also leaves the relevant probability unchanged:

$$P(B\mid A)=\frac{20}{50}=0.4=P(B).$$

The four regions form this probability table:

| | $B$ | $B^c$ | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| $A$ | $0.20$ | $0.30$ | $0.50$ |
| $A^c$ | $0.20$ | $0.30$ | $0.50$ |
| Total | $0.40$ | $0.60$ | $1$ |

Independence permits overlap. In fact, when both events have positive probability, independence requires a positive intersection equal to their product.

## Shape alone does not prove independence

The grid calculation works because all cells are equally likely. With unequal cell weights, counting area would not necessarily measure probability. Likewise, merely drawing two overlapping regions does not establish whether their events are independent; their probability masses must satisfy the product test.

If $P(A)=0.5$ and $P(B)=0.4$ stayed fixed but their overlap increased to $0.3$, then $P(A\mid B)=0.3/0.4=0.75$. The condition would favor $A$, so independence would fail.

Outside $B$ there are 60 cells, 30 of which are in $A$. Thus $P(A\mid B^c)=30/60=1/2$. It equals $P(A)$. When $A$ and $B$ are independent, replacing $B$ with its complement also preserves independence.
