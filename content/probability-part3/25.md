# Express the same overlap using the known conditional

Bayes' rule works because the intersection in a reversed conditional can be calculated in the original direction. Knowing how often B occurs within $A_1$, together with how common $A_1$ is, determines the probability that both occur.

## Start with the conditional direction we know

Assume $P(A_1)>0$. By definition,

$$P(B\mid A_1)=\frac{P(B\cap A_1)}{P(A_1)}.$$

Multiplying by $P(A_1)$ gives

$$P(A_1\cap B)=P(A_1)P(B\mid A_1).$$

The intersection is unchanged when its order is reversed: $B\cap A_1=A_1\cap B$. That set identity is the connection between the given and requested directions.

Now substitute this product into

$$P(A_1\mid B)=\frac{P(A_1\cap B)}{P(B)}.$$

For $P(B)>0$, we obtain the simple form of Bayes' theorem:

$$\boxed{P(A_1\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A_1)P(A_1)}{P(B)}.}$$

We have used only the definitions of the two conditional probabilities. No independence assumption and no causal interpretation is required.

## Check the substitution using a deck of cards

Let F mean face card and R mean red in a uniformly sampled standard deck. We know

$$P(F)=\frac{12}{52},\qquad P(R\mid F)=\frac6{12},
\qquad P(R)=\frac{26}{52}.$$

The numerator first converts the within-face-card red fraction into a fraction of the whole deck:

$$P(R\mid F)P(F)=\frac6{12}\frac{12}{52}=\frac6{52}.$$

Then conditioning on red rescales that overlap:

$$P(F\mid R)=\frac{6/52}{26/52}=\frac6{26}=\frac3{13}.$$

The intermediate denominator 12 cancels because it was the size of the first reference group. The deck denominator 52 cancels when we restrict to the red group. What remains is exactly the desired favorable count over the restricted total.

## Interpret Bayes as a change from prior to posterior

When $P(A_1)>0$, the formula can also be written

$$\frac{P(A_1\mid B)}{P(A_1)}=\frac{P(B\mid A_1)}{P(B)}.$$

The left side compares the probability of $A_1$ after and before learning B. The right side compares how often B occurs inside $A_1$ with how often B occurs overall.

If B is more frequent within $A_1$ than overall, observing B raises the probability of $A_1$. If it is less frequent, observing B lowers it. If the two rates are equal, the probability is unchanged.

In the card example, $P(R\mid F)=1/2=P(R)$, so learning red leaves the face-card probability unchanged: $P(F\mid R)=P(F)=3/13$. This is a case of independence, even though the two reversed conditional probabilities, $3/13$ and $1/2$, are different.

## What remains to finish the proof?

The compact formula contains $P(B)$ in its denominator. Sometimes that probability is directly known, as with the 26 red cards. Often we know B's rate separately within several groups but not its overall rate. The next slide supplies the denominator using total probability.

## Check your understanding

In the two-box model, $P(A_1)=0.30$, $P(B\mid A_1)=0.80$, and $P(B)=0.38$. By what factor does observing red increase Box 1's probability?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Compare the within-group and overall observation rates</summary>

The factor is $0.80/0.38=40/19\approx2.1053$. Multiplying the prior 0.30 by this factor gives $12/19\approx0.6316$. The factor can exceed one, but the resulting posterior remains a probability between zero and one because the inputs come from a consistent joint model.
