# Exactly one red, and the color of the last ball

The two questions on this slide extend the urn model in different directions. “Exactly one red in two draws” includes two possible orders. “The last ball is red” concerns a position in a complete random ordering. Both can be solved by counting outcomes explicitly.

## Question 1: exactly one of two drawn balls is red

Keep $N=r+b\ge2$, with uniform sampling without replacement. Define

$$E=\{\text{exactly one of the first two balls is red}\}.$$

Success occurs through either red then black or black then red:

$$E=(R_1\cap B_2)\cup(B_1\cap R_2).$$

These paths are mutually exclusive. Their probabilities are

$$P(R_1\cap B_2)=\frac rN\frac b{N-1},$$

$$P(B_1\cap R_2)=\frac bN\frac r{N-1}.$$

The two products are equal because each numerator counts one choice from each color. Therefore

$$\boxed{P(E)=\frac{2rb}{N(N-1)}.}$$

The factor two represents the two permitted color orders. It does not represent independence of the draws.

## Verify with unordered pairs

If the question ignores order, we can use the sample space of $\binom N2$ equally likely two-ball sets. A successful pair contains one of the $r$ red balls and one of the $b$ black balls, giving

$$\binom r1\binom b1=rb$$

favorable sets. Thus

$$P(E)=\frac{rb}{\binom N2}
=\frac{rb}{N(N-1)/2}
=\frac{2rb}{N(N-1)}.$$

Each unordered pair corresponds to exactly two ordered draws, so both sample-space choices give the same probability when used consistently.

For three red and two black balls, the result is $2(3)(2)/(5\cdot4)=3/5$. “At least one red” is a different event: it also includes RR and has probability $1-(2/5)(1/4)=9/10$.

## Question 2: draw the entire urn and inspect the last ball

Label all $N$ balls individually and draw them all without replacement. Any particular complete ordering has probability

$$\frac1N\frac1{N-1}\cdots\frac11=\frac1{N!}.$$

Hence the $N!$ permutations are equally likely. To count those ending in red:

1. Choose the final red ball in $r$ ways.
2. Arrange the remaining $N-1$ balls in the earlier positions in $(N-1)!$ ways.

The favorable count is $r(N-1)!$, so

$$\boxed{P(\text{last ball is red})
=\frac{r(N-1)!}{N!}
=\frac rN.}$$

This proves the equal-position idea by a concrete count. The same argument works for any specified position: fix its red ball and permute all other balls around it.

## What if the earlier colors have already been revealed?

Before observing any draws, the final red probability is $r/N$. If we have observed $j$ draws containing $a$ reds, the remaining urn has $r-a$ red balls among $N-j$ balls. Conditional on that information, the final red probability is

$$\frac{r-a}{N-j}.$$

After $N-1$ observed draws, only one ball remains, so that conditional probability is either zero or one. There is no contradiction: the unconditional and conditional questions use different information.

## Connect the earlier calculation to total probability

The previous slide computed $P(R_2)$ by splitting on the first color:

$$P(R_2)=P(R_1)P(R_2\mid R_1)+P(B_1)P(R_2\mid B_1).$$

This is the **law of total probability**. It reconstructs an unconditional probability from disjoint cases, using each case's probability as its weight. The next slide generalizes the two cases to an entire partition.
