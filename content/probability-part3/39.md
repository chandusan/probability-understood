# Set the ruin probabilities at the two stopping boundaries

A's current fortune is enough to describe the next stage of the game because the total money stays fixed and future plays are independent. Define the success probability for every possible fortune, including the two endpoints where the outcome is already decided.

## Separate the model's four quantities

The total capital is an integer k, A's current capital is i, the probability of winning the next dollar is p, and the probability of losing it is $q=1-p$. We assume $0<p<1$, so both directions are possible.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| k | Total dollars held by both gamblers |
| i | Dollars currently held by A |
| $k-i$ | Dollars currently held by B |
| p | Probability A wins the next play |
| q | Probability A loses the next play |
| $P_i$ | Probability A ultimately reaches k before 0, starting at i |

Lowercase p is a single-play probability. Uppercase $P_i$ is a whole-game probability that varies with i. Confusing them would replace the question about eventual success with a question about only the next move.

## Explain the boundary values directly

At fortune zero, A has already lost and the game is stopped:

$$
\boxed{P_0=0.}
$$

At fortune k, B has zero and A has already won:

$$
\boxed{P_k=1.}
$$

These are called boundary conditions. They are not extra assumptions about future play at the endpoints. There is no further play after reaching either endpoint.

For an interior state $1\le i\le k-1$, the next fortune is $i+1$ with probability p or $i-1$ with probability q. These are the only possible transitions because exactly one dollar changes hands on each play.

## Why A's fortune alone is a sufficient state

Knowing A has i dollars automatically tells us B has $k-i$. The earlier sequence of wins and losses cannot change the probabilities of future independent plays. For example, reaching i through win-lose and reaching i through lose-win lead to the same remaining problem.

This property lets us reuse the same $P_i$ whenever the game returns to fortune i. If future win probabilities depended on fatigue, previous outcomes, or an unobserved changing game, i alone might not capture the necessary information. The source's independent constant-p model explicitly excludes such changes.

## Verify that one boundary is eventually reached

Because the interval is finite, absorption at 0 or k occurs with probability one. Here is a direct bound rather than an appeal to intuition.

Imagine the independent potential outcomes are specified for arbitrarily many future plays, even though the actual game stops at a boundary. In any block of k potential plays, the probability that all are wins is $p^k>0$. From any interior fortune, that sequence must reach k before the block finishes. Therefore, conditional on the game still running at the start of a block, its chance of continuing through that block is at most $1-p^k$.

After j such blocks,

$$
P(\text{game still running after }jk\text{ plays})
\le(1-p^k)^j.
$$

The right side tends to zero as j grows. Hence there is no positive probability assigned to continuing forever. This bound may be loose, but it establishes what is needed: eventual success and eventual ruin are complementary events.

## Use the boundaries to constrain an answer

Any proposed formula for $P_i$ should lie between zero and one, give zero at $i=0$, and give one at $i=k$. These checks do not by themselves prove the formula, but they catch many reversed ratios or misplaced exponents.

For instance, in a fair game with $k=5$, the eventual answer will be $P_i=i/5$. It gives the boundary values exactly. Its values at the interior states must still be justified by the next-play recurrence, which the following slide develops.

Does eventual absorption mean that every game ends within k plays?

No. A path can repeatedly move between interior states, such as fortunes 1 and 2 when $k\ge3$. Any specified finite sequence of such alternating moves has positive probability. The block argument says the probability of surviving arbitrarily many blocks tends to zero, not that long finite games are impossible.
