# Begin Bayes' proof by restricting the sample space

The first step of Bayes' theorem is simply the definition of conditional probability. Once event B is known to have occurred, only outcomes inside B remain relevant. We measure how much of that restricted probability mass also belongs to the event of interest.

## Explain the numerator and denominator separately

For $P(B)>0$,

$$\boxed{P(A_1\mid B)=\frac{P(A_1\cap B)}{P(B)}.}$$

The numerator keeps outcomes that meet both requirements: they satisfy the observed information B and the requested property $A_1$. The denominator is the probability of everything compatible with the observed information.

Dividing by $P(B)$ rescales the restricted group to total probability one. In particular,

$$P(B\mid B)=\frac{P(B\cap B)}{P(B)}=1.$$

If the original outcomes have unequal probabilities, conditioning retains those relative weights inside B. It does not make them equally likely merely because we have restricted the sample space.

## See the restriction in the box example

From the preceding slide, Box 1 is selected with probability 0.30 and produces red with probability 0.80. Box 2 is selected with probability 0.70 and produces red with probability 0.20.

Let $A_1$ mean Box 1 and let B mean red. Their overlap has probability 0.24, while all red outcomes have probability 0.38. Thus

$$P(A_1\mid B)=\frac{0.24}{0.38}=\frac{12}{19}.$$

The scaled count interpretation is $240/380$: 240 Box-1 red outcomes among 380 red outcomes. The 620 non-red outcomes are outside the newly restricted group and do not belong in the denominator.

## Reverse the condition in a concrete card example

Draw one card uniformly from a standard 52-card deck. Let F mean a face card, meaning a jack, queen, or king, and let R mean a red card. There are 12 face cards, 26 red cards, and six red face cards.

Given red, there are 26 possible cards, of which six are face cards:

$$P(F\mid R)=\frac6{26}=\frac3{13}.$$

Given a face card, there are 12 possible cards, of which six are red:

$$P(R\mid F)=\frac6{12}=\frac12.$$

The numerator counts the same intersection in both questions. The denominators differ because the information supplied differs. The unconditional intersection probability is a third quantity,

$$P(F\cap R)=\frac6{52}=\frac3{26}.$$

Keeping these three fractions separate is essential to understanding Bayes' rule.

## Why the condition must have positive probability

The elementary definition divides by $P(B)$, so it requires $P(B)>0$. A zero-probability conditioning event would give a zero denominator and cannot be handled by this ratio. The formulas here concern ordinary events with positive probability; conditioning on exact values in continuous models requires additional machinery.

This is a mathematical domain condition, not a reason to alter a positive-probability calculation such as the red-card or red-ball examples.

## Check your understanding

For the same deck, find $P(F^c\mid R)$. Which other probability should it add to one with?

<summary>Take a complement inside the same restricted group</summary>

Twenty of the 26 red cards are not face cards, so $P(F^c\mid R)=20/26=10/13$. It adds to one with $P(F\mid R)=3/13$. It does not form a complement pair with $P(R\mid F)$, because that probability conditions on a different group.
