# Two draws from an urn: solve both questions

An urn contains $r$ red balls and $b$ black balls. Draw one ball uniformly, keep it out of the urn, and then draw uniformly from the remaining balls. We want the probability of black followed by red, and the probability of red on the second draw regardless of what happened first.

## Name the experiment and the events

Write $N=r+b$ for the initial number of balls, with $N\ge2$. First suppose both colors are present. We may temporarily label the balls to distinguish individual outcomes, even though the question records only their colors.

There are $N$ choices for the first ball and $N-1$ for the second. Thus there are $N(N-1)$ equally likely ordered pairs of distinct balls. The colors RR, RB, BR, and BB are four groups of these pairs; those four groups need not have equal probabilities.

Define

$$R_1=\{\text{first ball is red}\},\quad
B_1=\{\text{first ball is black}\},\quad
R_2=\{\text{second ball is red}\}.$$

Part (a) asks for $P(B_1\cap R_2)$. Part (b) asks for $P(R_2)$. The second question includes both RR and BR.

## Part (a): black first, then red

Initially, $b$ of the $N$ balls are black:

$$P(B_1)=\frac bN.$$

Given that the first ball was black, all $r$ red balls remain, but the total has fallen to $N-1$. Hence

$$P(R_2\mid B_1)=\frac r{N-1}.$$

The multiplication rule follows the two stages:

$$\boxed{
P(B_1\cap R_2)
=P(B_1)P(R_2\mid B_1)
=\frac bN\frac r{N-1}
=\frac{br}{N(N-1)}.}$$

A direct count gives the same answer. There are $b$ possible first black balls and $r$ possible second red balls, producing $br$ favorable ordered pairs out of $N(N-1)$.

## Part (b): red second, with either first color

Split success into two mutually exclusive paths:

$$R_2=(R_1\cap R_2)\cup(B_1\cap R_2).$$

These paths cannot overlap because the first ball cannot be both red and black. They cover every red-second outcome because the first ball must have one of the two colors.

On the RR path, the first red draw leaves $r-1$ red balls among $N-1$ balls. Therefore

$$P(R_1\cap R_2)=\frac rN\frac{r-1}{N-1}.$$

Adding both paths,

$$\begin{aligned}
P(R_2)
&=\frac rN\frac{r-1}{N-1}+\frac bN\frac r{N-1}\\
&=\frac{r(r-1)+br}{N(N-1)}\\
&=\frac{r(r+b-1)}{N(N-1)}\\
&=\boxed{\frac rN}.
\end{aligned}$$

The factors $N-1=r+b-1$ cancel only after combining the two possible first-draw histories.

## Follow a small numerical example

Take three red and two black balls, so $N=5$.

| Two colors in order | Calculation | Probability |
|---|---|---:|
| RR | $(3/5)(2/4)$ | $6/20$ |
| RB | $(3/5)(2/4)$ | $6/20$ |
| BR | $(2/5)(3/4)$ | $6/20$ |
| BB | $(2/5)(1/4)$ | $2/20$ |

The four probabilities sum to one. Part (a) is the BR row, $3/10$. Part (b) is RR plus BR, giving $12/20=3/5$.

## Equal first- and second-draw probabilities do not mean independence

We found $P(R_2)=r/N=P(R_1)$, but knowing the first color changes the second-draw probability. In the example,

$$P(R_2\mid R_1)=\frac12,\qquad
P(R_2\mid B_1)=\frac34,\qquad
P(R_2)=\frac35.$$

The unconditional probability averages the different histories with their probabilities. It does not say the urn stays unchanged after a draw.

If one color is absent, reason directly: black-then-red is impossible, while red-second has probability zero when $r=0$ and one when $b=0$. The final counting formulas give these same answers without conditioning on an impossible first color.
