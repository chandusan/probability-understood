# Build each urn likelihood from conditional draws

Before reversing a conditional probability with Bayes' rule, compute the forward probability of the evidence. Here that means asking how likely two red draws would be if the urn were known. Sampling without replacement makes the second factor depend on the first draw.

## Define events without hiding the conditioning

The urn is selected once. Write A for choosing the urn containing 12 red and 8 black balls, and B for choosing the urn containing 4 red and 16 black balls. Initially $P(A)=P(B)=1/2$.

Let $R_1$ and $R_2$ describe red balls on the first and second draws, and set $E=R_1\cap R_2$. The source abbreviates this intersection as $R_1R_2$. Writing the intersection explicitly helps distinguish an event from a product of probabilities.

The multiplication rule within urn A is

$$
P(R_1\cap R_2\mid A)
=P(R_1\mid A)P(R_2\mid A,R_1).
$$

The comma in $P(R_2\mid A,R_1)$ means we know **both** A and $R_1$ have occurred.

## Explain all four numbers in the product

Under A, there are initially 12 favorable red balls among 20 balls. Once the first red is drawn, the available collection contains 11 red and 8 black balls. Consequently,

$$
P(E\mid A)=\frac{12}{20}\frac{11}{19}=\frac{33}{95}.
$$

Under B, the first red leaves 3 red and 16 black balls:

$$
P(E\mid B)=\frac4{20}\frac3{19}=\frac3{95}.
$$

The denominator decreases from 20 to 19 because one ball has been removed. The red numerator decreases by one because the removed ball was observed to be red. If the first ball had been black, the red numerator would instead remain 12 or 4.

## Verify the same likelihoods by counting labeled balls

Imagine that every ball has a distinct label, even when colors match. From either urn there are $20\cdot19=380$ equally likely ordered pairs of distinct balls. In A, the number of ordered red-red pairs is $12\cdot11=132$; in B, it is $4\cdot3=12$.

Thus favorable-count divided by total-count gives exactly the sequential products. If order is ignored instead, the ratios are

$$
\frac{\binom{12}{2}}{\binom{20}{2}}
=\frac{66}{190}=\frac{33}{95},
\qquad
\frac{\binom42}{\binom{20}{2}}
=\frac6{190}=\frac3{95}.
$$

Both counting models work because they consistently treat the numerator and denominator either as ordered pairs or as unordered pairs.

## Conditional on the urn does not mean independent draws

For urn A,

$$
P(R_2\mid A,R_1)=\frac{11}{19}
\ne\frac{12}{20}=P(R_2\mid A).
$$

The unconditional-within-A second-draw red probability is $12/20$: among all 380 ordered pairs, $19\cdot12$ have a red second ball. Learning that the first ball was red changes the remaining collection and hence changes this probability.

Using $(12/20)^2$ would describe independent draws with replacement from A. The source explicitly removes the first ball, so the correct product uses $11/19$ instead.

## What the likelihood ratio tells us

The ratio is

$$
\frac{P(E\mid A)}{P(E\mid B)}=\frac{33/95}{3/95}=11.
$$

Two reds are eleven times as likely under A. This compares explanations of the evidence; the next step combines that comparison with the urn-selection probabilities. With equal priors the posterior becomes $11/12$, but unequal priors would give a different posterior despite these same likelihoods.
