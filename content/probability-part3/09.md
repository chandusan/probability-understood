# Mutually exclusive and independent describe different relationships

Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together. Independent events satisfy a product rule for their joint probability. If both events have positive probability, these properties are incompatible: learning that one of two mutually exclusive events occurred makes the other impossible, so its probability changes.

## Write the two definitions beside each other

Events $A$ and $B$ are **mutually exclusive**, or disjoint, when

$$A\cap B=\varnothing.$$

Consequently, $P(A\cap B)=0$. They are **independent** when

$$P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).$$

For disjoint events to be independent, we would need

$$0=P(A)P(B).$$

Because probabilities are nonnegative, this happens exactly when at least one of $P(A),P(B)$ equals zero. If both probabilities are positive, their product is positive, which cannot equal the zero-probability intersection.

That is the special case identified on the slide. No additional calculation is needed to show that two disjoint positive-probability events are dependent.

## See the change in probability on a die

Roll a fair six-sided die. Let $A=\{2\}$ and $B=\{4\}$. Each has probability $1/6$, but they cannot both occur on one roll. Therefore

$$P(A\cap B)=0\ne\frac1{36}=P(A)P(B).$$

The conditional view makes the dependence intuitive. Before any information, the chance of 2 is $1/6$. Given that the result is 4,

$$P(A\mid B)=0.$$

Knowing $B$ determines that $A$ did not happen. The events may sound like separate alternatives, but that very exclusivity connects them.

## Independent events can occur together

Toss two independent fair coins. Let $H_1$ mean “the first coin is heads” and $H_2$ mean “the second coin is heads.” Their intersection is the outcome $HH$, with probability $1/4$:

$$P(H_1\cap H_2)=\frac14=\frac12\cdot\frac12.$$

They are independent and overlap. Knowing the first result does not remove either possibility for the second.

These two examples also show why “or” and “and” use different rules:

| Relationship | Consequence for a union | Consequence for an intersection |
|---|---|---|
| Disjoint | $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$ | $P(A\cap B)=0$ |
| Independent | $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A)P(B)$ | $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$ |

Adding probabilities without subtracting overlap needs disjointness. Multiplying the two unconditional probabilities needs independence.

## An event and its complement are usually dependent

$A$ and $A^c$ are always disjoint. Their probabilities are $p$ and $1-p$, so independence would require

$$0=p(1-p).$$

This holds only at $p=0$ or $p=1$. If $0<p<1$, learning that $A$ occurred makes $A^c$ impossible; learning that $A$ failed makes $A^c$ certain.

The source's phrase “highly dependent” is an informal description of this logical exclusion. The precise conclusion needed here is simply **not independent**, not a numerical degree of dependence.

## Check your understanding

Two events each have probability $0.5$. Can they be disjoint? Can they be independent? Can they be both?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

They can be disjoint, as heads and tails on one fair toss are. They can instead be independent, as heads on the first and second independent fair tosses are. They cannot be both: disjointness gives joint probability zero, whereas independence requires $0.5\cdot0.5=0.25$.
