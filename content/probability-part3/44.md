# In a fair game, equal probability increments give i over k

When each play is fair, every additional starting dollar adds the same amount to A's chance of winning the entire game. The two boundary conditions determine that amount: k equal increases must take the success probability from zero to one. This yields i/k without substituting into an undefined fraction.

## Start with the fair first-step recurrence

For $p=q=1/2$ and $1\le i\le k-1$,

$$
P_i=\frac12P_{i+1}+\frac12P_{i-1}.
$$

Multiply by two and rearrange:

$$
2P_i=P_{i+1}+P_{i-1},
$$

$$
P_{i+1}-P_i=P_i-P_{i-1}.
$$

Thus adjacent differences are equal. Starting from $P_0=0$, call the common difference d. Then

$$
P_1=d,\quad P_2=2d,\quad P_3=3d,\quad\ldots,\quad P_i=id.
$$

These are consequences of the conditional recurrence, not an assumption that every starting fortune is equally likely to win.

## Normalize the increments using the terminal boundary

At fortune k, A already owns all the money. Therefore

$$
1=P_k=kd,
$$

so $d=1/k$. Consequently,

$$
\boxed{P_i=\frac{i}{k}}.
$$

The finite-sum derivation says exactly the same thing. When $q/p=1$, its numerator has i terms equal to one and its denominator has k terms equal to one. Their ratio is i/k.

## Interpret what “fair” does and does not imply

Suppose the total is ten dollars and A starts with three. Then

$$
P(\text{A wins everything})=\frac3{10},\qquad
P(\text{A is ruined})=1-\frac3{10}=\frac7{10}.
$$

Each individual play is still a 50–50 event. The whole game is not a 50–50 contest from these unequal starting fortunes: A begins closer to the losing boundary than to the winning boundary. If both begin with five dollars, the same derivation gives $5/10=1/2$.

Fairness also does not make all complete game histories equally likely. A specified history of t fair plays has probability $2^{-t}$, and possible histories can have different lengths. The first-step recurrence accounts for those varying lengths without trying to count them as equally weighted outcomes.

## Collect both cases into the final answer

For integer $0\le i\le k$, total capital k, independent one-dollar plays, and $0<p<1$,

$$
\boxed{
P_i=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1-((1-p)/p)^i}{1-((1-p)/p)^k},&p\ne\tfrac12,\\[9pt]
\dfrac{i}{k},&p=\tfrac12.
\end{cases}}
$$

The ruin probability for A is $1-P_i$, because the finite game reaches one of its two boundaries with probability one. In every case, the formula gives $P_0=0$ and $P_k=1$.

For two dollars each, the effect of changing the single-play probability is visible in exact fractions:

| A's probability p of winning a play | A's probability $P_2$ of winning all four dollars |
|---:|---:|
| $2/5$ | $4/13\approx0.307692$ |
| $1/2$ | $1/2$ |
| $3/5$ | $9/13\approx0.692308$ |

Keep the starting fortune and total capital fixed while changing p, then keep p fixed while changing the starting fortune. The first change alters the likelihood of each step; the second changes the distance to the two stopping boundaries.

## A compact way to reproduce the derivation

The essential chain is: condition on the next play, obtain $P_i=pP_{i+1}+qP_{i-1}$, subtract neighboring terms, sum the resulting geometric differences, and apply $P_0=0$ and $P_k=1$. For a fair game, the geometric differences are constant. For a biased game, their ratio is $q/p$.

Keeping this chain in mind is more useful than memorizing the final fraction alone. It explains which assumptions must be reconsidered if the stakes, win probabilities, or stopping boundaries change.
