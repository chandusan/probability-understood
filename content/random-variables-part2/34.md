# Turn a formula of joint weights into a usable model

The final example gives relative weights for nine combinations of subscription tier and API-limit breaches. We must first turn those weights into probabilities, then compute marginals, and finally update the tier distribution after observing two breaches.

## State the variables, support, and every question

For a randomly selected observation from the cloud platform's modeled population, let

$$
X\in\{1,2,3\}
$$

denote subscription tier: 1 is Basic, 2 is Pro, and 3 is Enterprise. Let

$$
Y\in\{0,1,2\}
$$

be the number of API threshold breaches recorded in the modeled hour. The example restricts the support to these three breach counts; it does not assign probability to higher counts.

The joint PMF is specified as

$$
p(x,y)=k\,\frac{x^y}{y!}\,2^{-x},
\qquad x\in\{1,2,3\},\quad y\in\{0,1,2\},
$$

and zero outside those nine combinations. The tasks are:

1. Find the normalizing constant $k$.
2. Find the marginal PMFs of $X$ and $Y$.
3. Give the best predicted tier after observing $Y=2$ breaches.

## Read the unnormalized expression one factor at a time

Define

$$
w(x,y)=\frac{x^y}{y!}\,2^{-x}.
$$

This is a **weight** assigned to the pair $(x,y)$. Its numerical value comes from the supplied model, not from an assumption that the nine pairs are equally likely.

The factor $2^{-x}$ means $1/2^x$. The factorials are $0!=1$, $1!=1$, and $2!=2$. Since all allowed $x$ values are positive, $x^0=1$. For example,

$$
w(3,2)=\frac{3^2}{2!}\,2^{-3}
=\frac92\cdot\frac18=\frac9{16}.
$$

This is not yet $P(X=3,Y=2)$: it must still be multiplied by $k$.

## Why one common constant is enough

Multiplying every weight by the same positive constant preserves their relative proportions. Choose $k$ so that the nine resulting probabilities sum to one:

$$
1=\sum_{x=1}^{3}\sum_{y=0}^{2}p(x,y)
=k\sum_{x=1}^{3}\sum_{y=0}^{2}w(x,y).
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{k=\frac1{\text{sum of all nine weights}}.}
$$

The next page computes that sum explicitly. Once the joint PMF is normalized, row totals give tier probabilities, column totals give breach-count probabilities, and the $Y=2$ column can be normalized again to answer the prediction question.

## Make “best prediction” precise

If we must name one of the three tiers and want the greatest chance of being correct, choose the tier with the largest conditional probability given $Y=2$. This is the natural classification interpretation of the exercise. A numerical estimate judged by the size of its error can have a different best answer; the completed solution makes that distinction explicit.
