# From the joint table to marginals and a tier prediction

The normalized model gives a full distribution over subscription tier and breach count. Its margins describe each variable before any additional observation; normalizing the two-breach column gives the tier distribution after learning that two breaches occurred.

## Start with the complete normalized joint table

Let $X=1,2,3$ denote Basic, Pro, and Enterprise tiers, and let $Y=0,1,2$ count breaches. With $k=16/57$, the model is

$$
p(x,y)=\frac{16}{57}\frac{x^y}{y!}2^{-x}
$$

on those nine pairs and zero elsewhere. Its probabilities are

| Tier $X$ | $Y=0$ | $Y=1$ | $Y=2$ | $P(X=x)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basic: 1 | $8/57$ | $8/57$ | $4/57$ | $20/57$ |
| Pro: 2 | $4/57$ | $8/57$ | $8/57$ | $20/57$ |
| Enterprise: 3 | $2/57$ | $6/57$ | $9/57$ | $17/57$ |
| $P(Y=y)$ | $14/57$ | $22/57$ | $21/57$ | $1$ |

The original slide finishes with this table. We will also complete the prediction requested in part (c) of the problem.

## Part (b): read and verify both marginal PMFs

Sum across each row to remove the breach-count distinction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X=1)&=\frac{8+8+4}{57}=\frac{20}{57},\\
P(X=2)&=\frac{4+8+8}{57}=\frac{20}{57},\\
P(X=3)&=\frac{2+6+9}{57}=\frac{17}{57}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Sum down each column to remove the tier distinction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Y=0)&=\frac{8+4+2}{57}=\frac{14}{57},\\
P(Y=1)&=\frac{8+8+6}{57}=\frac{22}{57},\\
P(Y=2)&=\frac{4+8+9}{57}=\frac{21}{57}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Both marginal distributions sum to one: $20+20+17=57$ and $14+22+21=57$. Each is zero outside its listed support.

## Part (c): restrict attention to observations with two breaches

We now observe $Y=2$. The relevant group is the last column, whose total probability is $21/57$. Divide every entry in this column by that total:

$$
P(X=1\mid Y=2)=\frac{4/57}{21/57}=\frac4{21},
$$

$$
P(X=2\mid Y=2)=\frac{8/57}{21/57}=\frac8{21},
$$

$$
P(X=3\mid Y=2)=\frac{9/57}{21/57}=\frac9{21}.
$$

Thus the conditional, or **posterior**, tier distribution is

| Tier | Before observing breaches | After observing $Y=2$ |
|---|---|---|
| Basic: 1 | $20/57\approx35.09\%$ | $4/21\approx19.05\%$ |
| Pro: 2 | $20/57\approx35.09\%$ | $8/21\approx38.10\%$ |
| Enterprise: 3 | $17/57\approx29.82\%$ | $9/21\approx42.86\%$ |

The new probabilities sum to one because the three tiers exhaust the observations with two breaches. The denominator is 21, rather than 57, after the common factor cancels: we are measuring proportions within the observed column.

## Choose the tier with the highest chance of being correct

If predicting Basic, the chance of being correct is $4/21$. If predicting Pro, it is $8/21$. If predicting Enterprise, it is $9/21$, the largest of the three.

Therefore the most likely tier prediction is

$$
\boxed{\widehat X=3\quad\text{(Enterprise)}.}
$$

This is called the **maximum a posteriori**, or **MAP**, prediction: choose the value with the greatest probability after observing the evidence. It gives about a 42.86% chance of being correct. A best available guess need not have probability greater than one half, especially when there are more than two possibilities.

## Why the prediction changes after seeing the evidence

Enterprise had the smallest marginal probability, $17/57$, before we knew the breach count. But two breaches are more common within Enterprise:

$$
P(Y=2\mid X=1)=\frac4{20},\quad
P(Y=2\mid X=2)=\frac8{20},\quad
P(Y=2\mid X=3)=\frac9{17}.
$$

Multiply each tier's prior probability by its probability of producing two breaches. This gives the three joint cells $4/57,8/57,9/57$. Dividing by their sum produces $4/21,8/21,9/21$.

This is Bayes' updating rule expressed directly through the table: begin with the tier frequencies, account for how compatible each tier is with the observed breaches, and normalize.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Would a different meaning of “best guess” give a different answer?</summary>

Yes. MAP is appropriate when we must choose one tier and every incorrect label counts as one mistake. It maximizes the probability of an exactly correct classification.

If instead the numerical codes $1,2,3$ are treated as meaningful equally spaced quantities, and errors are scored by their **squares**, the best unrestricted numerical estimate is the conditional mean:

$$
\mu=E[X\mid Y=2]
=1\frac4{21}+2\frac8{21}+3\frac9{21}
=\boxed{\frac{47}{21}\approx2.2381}.
$$

Here the mean is a probability-weighted average, so it need not be one of the possible tier labels. To see why it minimizes squared error, let $g$ be a proposed numerical guess. Writing $X-g=(X-\mu)+(\mu-g)$ and expanding gives

$$
E[(X-g)^2\mid Y=2]
=E[(X-\mu)^2\mid Y=2]+(\mu-g)^2.
$$

The cross term is zero because $E[X-\mu\mid Y=2]=0$. The first term does not depend on our guess, while the second is smallest at $g=\mu$.

If a squared-error prediction must itself be an allowed integer tier, choose the closest allowed value to $47/21$, which is 2. Indeed, the conditional average squared errors for guesses 1, 2, and 3 are respectively $44/21$, $13/21$, and $24/21$.

For the exercise's natural goal of naming the most likely subscription category, **Enterprise, tier 3, is the answer**. The numerical mean solves a different prediction objective.
