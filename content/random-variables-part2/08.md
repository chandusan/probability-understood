# Record two measurements from the same random experiment

A joint distribution describes two random variables together. Instead of asking only which value $X$ takes, we ask which pair $(X,Y)$ occurs. The word “joint” means that both measurements come from the same performance of the experiment.

## Begin with a small experiment

Toss a fair coin twice, independently. Define

$$
X=\text{the total number of heads},\qquad
Y=\begin{cases}1,&\text{the first toss is heads},\\0,&\text{the first toss is tails}.\end{cases}
$$

The four ordered outcomes are equally likely:

| Coin outcome | $X$ | $Y$ | Recorded pair $(X,Y)$ | Probability |
|---|---:|---:|---|---:|
| HH | 2 | 1 | $(2,1)$ | $1/4$ |
| HT | 1 | 1 | $(1,1)$ | $1/4$ |
| TH | 1 | 0 | $(1,0)$ | $1/4$ |
| TT | 0 | 0 | $(0,0)$ | $1/4$ |

For example, $(X,Y)=(1,0)$ means **one head in total and a tail on the first toss**. Only TH satisfies both requirements, so its probability is $1/4$.

## A pair is more informative than either measurement alone

Knowing $X=1$ leaves two possibilities, HT and TH. Knowing both $X=1$ and $Y=0$ selects TH.

The notation

$$
p(x,y)=P(X=x,\ Y=y)
$$

uses the comma to mean **and**:

$$
P(X=x,\ Y=y)=P\bigl(\{X=x\}\cap\{Y=y\}\bigr).
$$

The capital letters $X,Y$ name the random variables. The lowercase letters $x,y$ name particular values we are testing. Thus $p(1,0)$ is one specific entry of the whole function $p$.

## Possible individual values do not guarantee a possible pair

In this experiment, $X$ can equal 2 and $Y$ can equal 0, considered separately. But they cannot occur together: two heads force the first toss to be heads. Therefore

$$p(2,0)=0.$$

The set of pairs with positive probability is called the **joint support**. Here it is

$$\{(0,0),(1,0),(1,1),(2,1)\}.$$

Finding this support is part of finding the joint distribution. It prevents us from assigning probability to combinations the experiment cannot produce.

## What “multivariate” adds

“Variate” refers to a variable. A bivariate distribution tracks two variables; a multivariate distribution tracks several. Three measurements would give a triple $(X,Y,Z)$ and, in the discrete case, masses $P(X=x,Y=y,Z=z)$.

A joint distribution does not assume the measurements are independent. In fact, it lets us describe their relationship. We will first construct probabilities for pairs, then learn how to recover one variable's distribution and how to update it when the other variable is known.
