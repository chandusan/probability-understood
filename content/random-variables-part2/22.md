# Why summing over the other variable gives a marginal

The marginal formula follows directly from the addition rule for disjoint events. Fixing $X=x$ and listing every possible value of $Y$ divides the event $X=x$ into pieces that neither overlap nor leave anything out.

## First describe the event we want

Let $Y$ have possible values $y_1,y_2,y_3,\ldots$. Consider one specific value $x$ of $X$. Whenever $X=x$ occurs, $Y$ must still take one of its possible values. Hence

$$
\{X=x\}
=\{X=x,Y=y_1\}\cup\{X=x,Y=y_2\}\cup\cdots.
$$

This is an equality of events. The left side gives one requirement. The right side sorts the outcomes meeting that requirement according to their $Y$ values.

## Explain why addition is allowed

An outcome cannot have both $Y=y_1$ and $Y=y_2$ when $y_1\ne y_2$. Therefore the pieces on the right are disjoint. By the addition rule,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X=x)
&=P(X=x,Y=y_1)+P(X=x,Y=y_2)+\cdots\\
&=p(x,y_1)+p(x,y_2)+\cdots\\
&=\boxed{\sum_y p(x,y)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

There is no need for an independence assumption. Whether $X$ and $Y$ are related or unrelated, each outcome belongs to exactly one of these pieces.

## See what the sum does to actual outcomes

Roll a fair die independently five times; let $X$ count sixes and $Y$ count fives. An outcome such as $(6,5,6,2,4)$ belongs to the cell $(X,Y)=(2,1)$. When calculating $P(X=2)$, we include that outcome alongside all outcomes in cells $(2,0),(2,2),$ and $(2,3)$.

We have stopped distinguishing outcomes by their number of fives. We have **not** required the number of fives to be zero, averaged over the possible counts equally, or assumed those counts are equally likely.

The probabilities already contain their correct weights. Adding them retains those weights.

## Why marginals cannot usually reconstruct the joint table

Imagine two random bits, each individually equally likely to be zero or one. Here are two possible joint models:

| Joint outcome $(X,Y)$ | Always equal | Always opposite |
|---|---:|---:|
| $(0,0)$ | $1/2$ | $0$ |
| $(0,1)$ | $0$ | $1/2$ |
| $(1,0)$ | $0$ | $1/2$ |
| $(1,1)$ | $1/2$ | $0$ |

Both models have the same marginals: $P(X=0)=P(X=1)=P(Y=0)=P(Y=1)=1/2$. Yet one has $P(X=Y)=1$ and the other has $P(X=Y)=0$.

The margins describe each variable separately. The locations of the probability inside the table describe their relationship.
