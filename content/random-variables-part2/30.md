# Given the number of fives, the remaining six count is binomial

For five independent rolls of a fair die, let $X$ count sixes and $Y$ count fives. Given $Y=y$, there are $5-y$ non-five rolls, and each has five equally likely possible faces. This leads to a binomial distribution with $5-y$ trials and six probability $1/5$.

## State exactly what we are conditioning on

The observation $Y=y$ fixes the number of fives, where $y$ is one of $0,1,\ldots,5$. It does not tell us which positions contain those fives.

We want

$$
p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=P(X=x\mid Y=y),
\qquad x=0,1,\ldots,5-y.
$$

The upper limit $5-y$ follows because one roll cannot be both a five and a six.

## Derive the conditional PMF from the probability ratio

The joint PMF and the marginal PMF of $Y$ are

$$
p(x,y)=\frac{5!}{x!\,y!\,(5-x-y)!}
\frac{4^{5-x-y}}{6^5},
$$

$$
p_Y(y)=\binom5y\frac{5^{5-y}}{6^5}.
$$

The first numerator counts arrangements of $x$ sixes, $y$ fives, and the remaining other faces. The second counts all sequences with $y$ fives, allowing any non-five face in every remaining position.

Now divide and cancel the common denominator $6^5$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(X=x\mid Y=y)
&=\frac{p(x,y)}{p_Y(y)}\\
&=\frac{5!}{x!\,y!\,(5-x-y)!}
\frac{y!\,(5-y)!}{5!}
\frac{4^{5-x-y}}{5^{5-y}}\\
&=\frac{(5-y)!}{x!\,(5-y-x)!}
\frac{4^{5-y-x}}{5^{5-y}}\\
&=\boxed{\binom{5-y}{x}
\left(\frac15\right)^x
\left(\frac45\right)^{5-y-x}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

In the last step, write $5^{5-y}=5^x5^{5-y-x}$. This separates the factors for the $x$ sixes and the $5-y-x$ other non-five faces.

The result has the binomial form: choose which remaining positions are sixes, multiply by their success probabilities, then multiply by the probabilities of the other positions. Thus

$$
\boxed{X\mid(Y=y)\sim\operatorname{Bin}(5-y,1/5).}
$$

## See why unknown five positions do not change the answer

Imagine first fixing one particular set of $y$ positions for the fives. Conditional on exactly those positions being fives, the remaining $5-y$ positions are independent, uniformly chosen from $\{1,2,3,4,6\}$. They therefore contain a binomial number of sixes with success probability $1/5$.

There are $\binom5y$ possible sets of five positions. **Every such set gives the same distribution for the count of sixes.** Averaging over the unknown set therefore leaves that count distribution unchanged.

The counting version makes the cancellation explicit:

$$
\frac{
\overbrace{\binom5y}^{\text{five positions}}
\overbrace{\binom{5-y}{x}}^{\text{six positions}}
\overbrace{4^{5-y-x}}^{\text{other faces}}
}{
\underbrace{\binom5y}_{\text{five positions}}
\underbrace{5^{5-y}}_{\text{all non-five fillings}}
}
=\binom{5-y}{x}\frac{4^{5-y-x}}{5^{5-y}}.
$$

This does not say that the original five roll positions remain independent after conditioning on the total number of fives. Their five/not-five indicators are constrained to sum to $y$. The argument concerns the count of sixes in the remaining positions after accounting for which positions those are.

Each column sums to one. The whole table sums to six because it contains **six separate conditional distributions**, one for each possible observation. It is not a joint PMF.

For $Y=0$, five non-five rolls remain, so $P(X=0\mid Y=0)=(4/5)^5=0.32768$. For $Y=5$, no positions remain for a six, so $X=0$ with probability one. This last case is the degenerate binomial distribution with zero trials.
