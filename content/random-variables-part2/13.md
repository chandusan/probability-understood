# Read a joint PMF as a landscape of probability masses

The source graph displays the same image-audit distribution in three dimensions. $X$ counts minor-error images and $Y$ counts completely mislabeled images among 7 drawn without replacement from the batch of 25. Each bar stands above one possible pair, and its height is the probability of that pair.

## Locate a bar by its two coordinates

The two horizontal axes locate $(x,y)$. The vertical axis gives

$$p(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y).$$

For example, the bar at $(2,1)$ represents samples with 2 minor-error images, 1 completely mislabeled image, and therefore 4 perfect images. Its height is

$$p(2,1)=\frac{\binom82\binom51\binom{12}4}{\binom{25}7}\approx0.14416476.$$

This is the tallest bar: $(2,1)$ is the **most likely individual pair**, sometimes called the joint mode. That does not mean this exact pair usually occurs. Its probability is only about 14.42%; the remaining 85.58% is spread across other pairs.

## The bars show masses, so add heights

For a discrete joint distribution, each bar's height is already a probability. To find the probability of several pairs, add the heights of the selected bars.

For example, “at least one completely mislabeled image” means all bars with $y\ge1$. It is easier to remove the row of bars with $y=0$ from the total:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Y\ge1)
&=1-P(Y=0)\\
&=1-\frac{\binom{20}7}{\binom{25}7}\\
&=\frac{403180}{480700}\approx\boxed{0.83873518.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The drawn width or depth of a bar is a display choice. It is not an extra probability factor. We do not integrate the drawn bar surfaces to calculate these discrete probabilities.

## Understand the empty part of the plot

Every sample contains seven images, so any pair with $x+y>7$ is impossible. Those locations have no positive bar. There are also only five completely mislabeled images in the batch, so $y>5$ is impossible.

The constraint connects the two counts. If we learn $Y=5$, only two sample positions remain for minor-error or perfect images, so $X\le2$. If $Y=0$, values of $X$ through 7 remain possible.

That is already evidence that the two counts cannot vary independently: information about one changes which values of the other are possible.

## Be careful with a three-dimensional view

Perspective can partly hide a bar behind another, and the apparent area of a bar can draw attention away from its height. Use the table or formula when you need an exact probability. The plot is most useful for seeing where the probability is concentrated and which combinations are ruled out.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Check your interpretation</summary>

Which is larger: the probability of the tallest bar, or the probability of at least one completely mislabeled image?

The tallest bar is only one exact composition, with probability about $0.1442$. The event $Y\ge1$ collects many bars, with total probability about $0.8387$. A collection of individually smaller possibilities can have much more probability than the single most likely possibility.
