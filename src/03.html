01 · The art of not making a list

One group. Six different stories.

You choose Asha, Ben, and Chen for a team. Then you assign them chair, deputy, and treasurer. Did you just make the same choice twice?

As a team, {Asha, Ben, Chen} is one selection. As officeholders, Asha–Ben–Chen and Ben–Asha–Chen are different assignments. In fact those three people fill the three roles in 3! = 6 ways.

Counting begins with one question: what makes two outcomes different? Formulas come afterward. This lesson develops every major counting formula by building an outcome, then correcting any duplicate descriptions.

The working method

Describe one outcome precisely. Break its construction into choices. Check whether each outcome was counted exactly once.

02 · Branches and alternatives

Multiply along a path. Add across disjoint cases.

Eight runners contest gold and silver. There are eight choices for gold. For each of those, seven runners remain for silver. That gives eight groups of seven assignments: 8 × 7 = 56.

This is the multiplication principle. If each valid first choice has n₂ possible continuations, and each continuation has n₃ next choices, the complete construction has n₁n₂n₃ possibilities. The counts may change from stage to stage. They must be consistent across branches at the corresponding stage, or you must split into cases.

A theoretical license plate with two letter slots followed by five digit slots has 26²10⁵ = 67,600,000 possibilities, assuming repetition and leading zeroes are allowed. If letters may appear anywhere among seven positions, it is a different problem with an extra factor C(7,2).

Adding counts is appropriate for disjoint alternatives. If a valid outcome is either type I or type II, and cannot be both, add the two counts. If it can be both, subtract the overlap. For one card from a 52-card deck, “ace or diamond” counts 4 + 13 − 1 = 16 cards, because the ace of diamonds was counted twice.

Counting multiplication is not probabilistic independence.

The seven silver choices depend on which runner won gold. We can still multiply because every gold winner leaves exactly seven options. Independence is a property of probability weights, not a prerequisite for multiplying branch counts.

03 · Remove the overcount

Where factorials come from.

Arrange n distinct objects in a row. Choose the first in n ways, the second in n − 1, and continue to the last. The product n(n − 1)…1 is n factorial, written n!. There is one way to arrange no objects—the empty arrangement—so 0! = 1.

Stop after r places: permutations

P(n,r) = n(n − 1)···(n − r + 1) = n!/(n − r)!

Three named roles assigned to ten people give P(10,3) = 10·9·8 = 720. You cannot give two roles to one person under this model.

Erase the role labels: combinations

Now choose only the three people. Each team appeared six times among the ordered assignments, once per ordering. Divide by that redundancy:

C(n,r) = P(n,r)/r! = n!/[r!(n − r)!]Read C(n,r) as “n choose r.” The notation (n over r) means the same thing.

C(10,3) = 720/6 = 120. Division by r! works because every team has the same r! orderings. Never divide by a number merely because a formula looks familiar; identify exactly what was overcounted.

Experiment · Watch order disappear

Roles or a team?

Choose a small pool and compare complete ordered lists with unordered groups. For larger lists, only the first 24 are shown; the total is exact.

For four people choosing two, AB and BA are two assignments but one team. The ordered count 12 collapses to 6. For three selected people, every group collapses six arrangements, not three.

A four-digit PIN is an ordered string with repetition: 10⁴. A four-person committee selected from ten people is an unordered set without repetition: C(10,4).

Why combinations with repetition use stars and bars

Buy five doughnuts from three flavors. An outcome records only the counts of each flavor, such as (2,0,3). Represent that purchase by ★★ | | ★★★. The five stars are doughnuts; the two bars divide the three flavors. Every purchase corresponds to exactly one string containing five stars and two bars.

C(5 + 3 − 1, 5) = C(7,5) = 21

In general, r identical objects distributed among n labeled categories require r stars and n − 1 bars. Choose the positions of either the stars or the bars.

Counts are not automatically probabilities.

The 21 flavor-count outcomes are not equally likely if you instead choose the flavor of each doughnut independently and uniformly. Some count patterns can be produced in more orders than others.

Repeated objects: distinguish temporarily, then erase labels

Arrange the letters in BANANA. Pretend its three As and two Ns have tiny labels. There are 6! labeled arrangements, but permuting the A labels in 3! ways and N labels in 2! ways leaves the visible word unchanged. So there are 6!/(3!2!) = 60 distinct arrangements.

Multiset arrangements = n!/(n₁!n₂!···nₖ!)

05 · Count the story, not the symbols

From shelves to lottery tickets.

Books together: make blocks, then open the blocks

The slides ask you to arrange ten distinct books: four mathematics, three chemistry, two history, one language, keeping each subject together. Treat the subjects as four blocks. Arrange those in 4! ways. Then arrange the books within their blocks:

4! × 4! × 3! × 2! × 1! = 6912

A constrained pipeline: subtract forbidden constructions

Choose three distinct features in order from A,…,G. The first must be A or B. Features C and D cannot both appear. Ignoring the last restriction gives 2·6·5 = 60. Invalid pipelines have A or B first, then CD or DC, giving 2·2 = 4. Therefore 56 are valid.

Coins and the binomial theorem

A string of ten independent fair coin tosses has probability 1/2¹⁰. To have exactly three heads, select which three positions contain H. There are C(10,3) = 120 such strings. Thus P(exactly 3) = 120/1024. Three or fewer has probability [1 + 10 + 45 + 120]/1024 = 176/1024 = 11/64.

The same construction explains algebra. When multiplying n copies of (x + y), choosing x from k factors and y from the rest creates xᵏyⁿ⁻ᵏ. There are C(n,k) ways to choose those factors:

(x + y)ⁿ = ∑k=0n C(n,k)xᵏyⁿ⁻ᵏ
The slides’ extension to noninteger powers

For real α, define C(α,k) = α(α−1)…(α−k+1)/k!, with C(α,0)=1. Then (1+x)ᵅ = ∑k≥0C(α,k)xᵏ for |x| < 1. This is an infinite power series, not a count of selections from a fractional number of objects. For nonnegative integer α it terminates and becomes the ordinary binomial theorem.

06 · A masterclass in overcounting

A poker hand has no first card.

There are C(52,5) = 2,598,960 five-card hands. To count exactly one pair, first choose its rank (13), then its two suits (C(4,2)), then three different ranks from the other twelve (C(12,3)), then one suit for each (4³). The distinct kicker ranks prevent accidentally counting two pairs or a full house.

13 × C(4,2) × C(12,3) × 4³ = 1,098,240

A full house has a triple and a pair. Their roles are different, so choose their ranks in 13·12 ways. Then choose three suits for the triple and two for the pair: 13·12·4·6 = 3744.

Worked practice: all three committee officers are male

Under uniform selection of distinct officeholders from ten members, six of whom are male, the probability is P(6,3)/P(10,3) = 120/720 = 1/6. You can also use C(6,3)/C(10,3) = 20/120. Either representation works if numerator and denominator use the same type of outcome.

Bridge to Lesson 4: do two tennis players ever meet?

In a symmetric single-elimination tournament with n equally skilled players and random pairings/byes, n − 1 matches occur. There are C(n,2) possible unordered pairs, and all pairs have the same chance to meet. Each pair meets at most once. Thus that chance is (n − 1)/C(n,2) = 2/n. Lesson 4 develops the symmetry argument.
