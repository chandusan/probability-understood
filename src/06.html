01 · Give every outcome a number

The experiment tells a story. X keeps one detail.

A user sees three ads. Their session might be click–ignore–click. That is a story. The number of clicks is 2. A random variable is the rule that turns the story into a number.

A random variable X is a function from outcomes to real numbers. The function is fixed; the outcome it receives is random. For a discrete random variable, we call the values with positive probability its support. All probability is concentrated on a finite or countably infinite list of such values. A continuous measurement can instead have a density; later we will combine a point mass with a density in a mixed model. We write uppercase X for the rule and lowercase x for a possible numerical value.

Different stories can produce the same x. If X is the number of clicks, CNN, NCN, and NNC all produce 1. A distribution tells us how much probability arrives at each value after this grouping.

Other examples on PDF p. 2 use the same idea: X might count earthquakes in a specified region and period, or record the value of a stock index on a future date. A count and a measurement require different probability models, but both are fixed numerical rules applied to uncertain outcomes. The slide’s quoted index value is context for its example, not a current market quote.

The course comes together here.

Sample spaces give the outcomes. Counting or conditional probability gives their weights. The random variable groups those weights into a numerical distribution.

02 · Gather all roads to the same number

A probability mass function is a collection of piles.

For a discrete random variable, define p(x)=P(X=x). In full, this means P({ω : X(ω)=x}): collect the entire event of outcomes mapped to x, then measure its probability. In a finite or countable underlying sample space, this becomes a sum of individual outcome probabilities.

p(x) = P({ω : X(ω)=x})
0 ≤ p(x) ≤ 1,   ∑x in the supportp(x) = 1
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∑a ≤ x ≤ bp(x)

Set p(x)=0 outside the support. The distinction between the underlying outcomes and the possible values matters: a discrete X can even be defined on a continuous sample space. If U is uniform on [0,1] and X=1 when U≤0.2 and 0 otherwise, then P(X=1)=0.2, found from an interval’s length, not by adding its zero-probability individual points.

For two independent fair dice, the sample space is S={(i,j): i,j∈{1,2,3,4,5,6}}: 36 equally likely ordered face pairs. X(i,j)=i+j maps this square grid to a sum. Their sums have weights 1,2,3,4,5,6,5,4,3,2,1 divided by 36, at x=2,…,12. The peak at seven means six different outcomes arrive there.

03 · Equal stories are not equal weights

Clicks remember the previous ad.

The first ad is clicked with probability 0.05. After a click, the next click probability is 0.15; after an ignored ad it is 0.05. Assume the next-click probability depends only on the immediately previous outcome, as described in the slides.

For a particular sequence, multiply along its conditional branches. CNN has probability 0.05·0.85·0.95 = 0.040375. NCN has probability 0.95·0.05·0.85 = 0.040375. NNC has probability 0.95·0.95·0.05 = 0.045125. All have one click, but their path probabilities need not agree.

Why not III? It already stopped at II. Valid paths must avoid an earlier stopping pattern. If the baseline epoch is included in the reported epoch total, add one to these N values. Probabilities cannot be assigned without a model for D/I/U transitions.

04 · The universal cumulative question

How much probability have we passed?

A cumulative distribution function asks one question at every threshold:

F(x) = P(X ≤ x)

Move x from left to right. You only collect more outcomes, never lose any. This explains why F cannot decrease. Far left, accumulated probability tends to zero. Far right, it tends to one. The CDF always lies between zero and one and is right-continuous: it includes a point’s mass at the point itself.

For a discrete distribution, the CDF stays flat between values and jumps at each mass. The jump at x is p(x)=F(x)−F(x−), where F(x−) means the limiting value just before x.

P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a)
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a−)

The first formula excludes a because F(a) already includes all its mass and subtracts it. Endpoints are part of the question.

Prove the CDF rules from sets · PDF pp. 29–32

For a<b, the event {X≤b} splits into two disjoint pieces: {X≤a} and {a<X≤b}. Add their probabilities:

F(b) = F(a) + P(a<X≤b)

The last term is nonnegative, proving that F cannot decrease. Rearranging proves the interval formula. For a discrete X, this same accumulation is F(x)=∑u≤xp(u).

Approaching x from below accumulates exactly P(X<x)=F(x−). The extra probability gained at x is the atom P(X=x)=F(x)−F(x−). Approaching from above, the events {X≤x+1/n} shrink down to {X≤x}; continuity of probability for decreasing events gives F(x+)=F(x). Thus a CDF is continuous at x precisely when there is no atom at x. If there are no atoms anywhere, the CDF is continuous, although—as the density section explains—it need not possess a density.

For any distribution, P(X>a)=1−F(a), whereas P(X≥a)=1−F(a−). With a density there are no endpoint atoms, so all four choices of strict or non-strict endpoints give the same interval probability. For a discrete or mixed model they may differ.

Use the slides’ four-point distribution

At values 3,5,8,10, the masses are 0.3,0.2,0.3,0.2. The final 0.2 follows from the total-one requirement. F(x) is 0 below 3; 0.3 on [3,5); 0.5 on [5,8); 0.8 on [8,10); and 1 from 10 onward.

Thus P(X≥5)=0.7, P(X>5)=0.5, and P(4<X≤8)=0.5. The 0.2 gap between the first two is exactly the mass at five.

05 · Spread probability like paint

Density is concentration, not a point probability.

On a continuum, probability can be spread smoothly. No single point needs any mass. Instead, an interval gets probability from the area under a density curve f:

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab f(x) dx
f(x) ≥ 0,   ∫−∞∞ f(x) dx = 1

Think of density as probability per unit length. A short interval of width Δx near x has probability approximately f(x)Δx when the density varies little there. Its width matters. An exact point has zero width and therefore probability zero in this model.

Density may exceed one. A uniform distribution on [0,0.2] has density 5 on that interval. Height 5 times width 0.2 gives area 1. There is no violation of the probability bounds.

CDF and density: total paint versus local thickness

F(x) = ∫−∞x f(t) dt,   f(x) = F′(x) where the derivative relation holds

The integral accumulates probability; the derivative describes its local rate of accumulation. For distributions with a density, this relation holds almost everywhere, not necessarily at every corner or boundary. A continuous CDF alone does not guarantee a density exists; absolute continuity is the stronger condition. That subtlety is beyond the calculations here, but prevents an overgeneralization.

Worked slide exercise: differentiate a polynomial CDF

Let F(x)=(x²+x³)/12 for 0≤x≤2, extend it to 0 for x<0 and to 1 for x>2. The endpoint values agree: F(0)=0, F(2)=1.

f(x)=(2x+3x²)/12 for 0<x<2, and 0 outside.

The density is nonnegative and its integral is [x²+x³]₀²/12=1. Values assigned to f at the isolated endpoints do not change probabilities.

06 · Derive a distribution from a picture

A growing circle sweeps a square.

Choose a point (U,V) uniformly from the unit square. Let X=√(U²+V²), its distance from the lower-left corner. The farthest possible point is (1,1), at distance √2. The distribution lives between 0 and √2.

Ask the CDF question geometrically: what fraction of the square lies within distance r of the corner? Draw a quarter-circle of radius r. Its overlap with the square is exactly F(r), because the square has area one.

Experiment · Probability as swept area

Grow the radius.

Watch what happens at r=1. The circle starts spilling beyond the square, so the quarter-circle formula must change.

  1. r≤0: the selected area is zero.
  2. 0<r≤1: the quarter-circle fits completely; F(r)=πr²/4.
  3. 1<r<√2: remove the two circular caps sticking outside the square.
  4. r≥√2: the square is fully covered; F(r)=1.

For the middle regime, one protruding cap has area ∫₁ʳ√(r²−u²)du = (r²/2)arccos(1/r) − √(r²−1)/2. Remove two such caps from the quarter-circle:

F(r) = √(r²−1) + r²[π/4 − arccos(1/r)],   1<r<√2

The slides use arctan√(r²−1), which equals arccos(1/r) in this range. Check the joins: at r=1 we get π/4; at r=√2 we get 1. These checks catch both sign and support mistakes.

The density is the length of the advancing arc

For r<1, adding a thin radial band adds approximately quarter-circumference × thickness. Thus f(r)=πr/2. Beyond one, only the arc still inside the square adds area. Its angular span is π/2 − 2 arccos(1/r), so:

f(r) = πr/2,   0<r<1
f(r) = r[π/2 − 2 arccos(1/r)],   1<r<√2
f(r) = 0 outside the support

We have explained the derivative geometrically before doing calculus. The density rises while the entire arc contributes, then falls as the square clips it. It reaches zero at the far corner. At the join r=1, both one-sided density limits equal π/2; assigning that value makes this density continuous there. Values at isolated endpoints do not change any probability.

Match the slides’ sector geometry and derivative · PDF pp. 15, 23–25

For 1<r<√2, put s=√(r²−1) and φ=arctan(s)=arccos(1/r). The clipped quarter-circle can also be split into two right triangles, whose combined area is s, and a central sector of angle π/2−2φ. Its area is (r²/2)(π/2−2φ). Hence F(r)=s+r²(π/4−φ), agreeing with the cap-subtraction argument.

To differentiate, s′=r/s. The chain rule gives φ′=s′/(1+s²)=(r/s)/r²=1/(rs). Now use the product rule:

F′(r) = r/s + 2r(π/4−φ) − r²/(rs)
= 2r(π/4−φ)
= πr/2 − 2r arctan√(r²−1)

The two r/s terms cancel. Each separately grows near r=1, but their difference is zero; taking the simplified expression’s limit gives π/2. The support ends at √2, the diagonal of the unit square—not at 2.

07 · Some probability jumps; some flows

Free users and paying users in one model.

The slides’ spending model puts probability 0.4 at X=0. The remaining probability is spread with density 0.3x on 0<x≤2, where X is measured in hundreds of dollars.

The continuous piece integrates to 0.6, not one. That is correct because it represents only the continuous part of the entire mixed distribution. Its density conditional on being a paying user would be 0.3x/0.6=0.5x.

F(x) = 0 for x<0
F(x) = 0.4 + 0.15x² for 0≤x≤2
F(x) = 1 for x>2

Experiment · A jump followed by a curve

At zero, collect a whole group at once.

At least $100 means X≥1. There is no atom at one, so its probability is ∫₁²0.3x dx = 0.45. At zero, however, strictness matters: P(X≥0)=1 but P(X>0)=0.6.

A CDF handles discrete, continuous, and mixed distributions with the same question. That universality is why it is so useful.

08 · Build and check a distribution

The probability budget must still balance.

Worked slide exercise: normalize an infinite PMF

For x=1,2,…, suppose p(x)=c x(1/3)ˣ. Start with the geometric series ∑x≥0rˣ=1/(1−r) for |r|<1. Differentiate and multiply by r to get ∑x≥1x rˣ=r/(1−r)².

At r=1/3 this sum is 3/4, so c=4/3. Then p(1)=4/9 and p(2)=8/27. Therefore P(X>2)=1−4/9−8/27=7/27.

Infinite support does not mean infinite total probability. The weights shrink fast enough for their sum to converge to one.
