01 · A clue changes the denominator

The die is already rolled. Your knowledge moves.

I roll a die and cover it. The chance it shows 2 is one in six. Then I tell you the result is even. Nothing about the die changes. Yet your probability should.

Only 2, 4, and 6 remain compatible with the clue. They are still equally likely in this model, so the chance is now one in three. Conditional probability describes a restricted world and redistributes the remaining probability to total one.

This is the thread linking a simple die, a suspicious transaction, an unknown urn, and a gambler’s fortune: condition on what you know, then average over what you do not.

02 · Crop and rescale

Why the formula divides by P(B).

Let B be the information we know. Outcomes outside B are excluded. Inside B, only the part also belonging to A can answer our question. That piece has original probability P(A∩B), out of a remaining total P(B).

P(A | B) = P(A∩B)/P(B),   P(B) > 0

Read the vertical bar as “given.” B is the new reference population. A condition need not happen earlier in time: learning tomorrow’s outcome can change your beliefs about yesterday’s cause. Conditional probability is about information, not a chronology requirement.

Streaming subscriptions: a denominator trap

The slides give P(S)=0.45, P(B)=0.60, and P(S∩B)=0.25. The four disjoint groups are: S only 0.20, B only 0.35, both 0.25, neither 0.20. “At most one” includes the first, second, and fourth groups, totaling 0.75.

P(neither | at most one) = 0.20/0.75 = 4/15 ≈ 0.2667

It is not 0.20, because we are no longer sampling from the whole population. It is not zero, because “at most one” includes zero.

03 · When a clue tells you nothing

Independence is an equality you can check.

A and B are independent if P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B). When P(B)>0, that is equivalent to P(A|B)=P(A). Learning B leaves the probability of A unchanged.

Disjoint positive-probability events behave very differently. If A and B cannot both happen, learning B makes A impossible. Their intersection is zero while P(A)P(B)>0, so they are dependent.

Two-way independence is not the whole story

Toss a fair coin four independent times. Let A mean the first two tosses match, B mean the total number of heads is odd, and C mean the last two tosses match. Each has probability 1/2. Every pair has intersection probability 1/4.

Yet if A and C both occur, each matching pair contributes an even number of heads. The total cannot be odd. Thus P(A∩B∩C)=0, not 1/8. The events are pairwise independent but not mutually independent.

Experiment · Inspect all 16 worlds

Pairs can hide a three-way constraint.

Mutual independence requires the product rule for every subcollection, not just pairs or the full collection. For mutually independent A,B,C each with probability 0.1: P(A∪B)=1−0.9²=0.19, P(A∪B∪C)=1−0.9³=0.271, and exactly one has probability 3·0.1·0.9²=0.243.

04 · Walk forward through a tree

Multiply along a branch, add complete routes.

Rearrange the definition of conditional probability:

P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B|A)

This rule does not require independence. Independence only lets us replace P(B|A) with P(B). For several events, keep the entire relevant history:

P(A₁∩A₂∩A₃) = P(A₁)P(A₂|A₁)P(A₃|A₁∩A₂)

Without replacement, the world changes

An urn contains r red and b black balls. Draw twice without replacement. For black then red, the first branch is b/(r+b); after removing a black ball, the second branch is r/(r+b−1). Multiply:

P(B₁∩R₂) = br / [(r+b)(r+b−1)]

Now ask only whether the second ball is red. We do not know the first color. There are two disjoint routes: red–red and black–red. Add their probabilities:

P(R₂) = [r/(r+b)]·[(r−1)/(r+b−1)]
+ [b/(r+b)]·[r/(r+b−1)] = r/(r+b)

The dependence disappears from the marginal answer, not from the process. Before seeing any draws, every position in a random ordering is equally likely to contain any original ball. That also proves the last ball is red with probability r/(r+b).

The law of total probability

If A₁,…,Aₖ are disjoint cases that cover the sample space, any event B can occur through those separate routes:

P(B) = ∑i P(Aᵢ)P(B|Aᵢ)

Each conditional probability is weighted by how common its case is. A plain average of the conditionals works only when the cases have equal weights. Terms with zero-probability cases contribute zero and can be omitted.

Worked cards, fox tags, and the slides’ sports model

Cards: king, queen, jack in that order without replacement has probability (4/52)(4/51)(4/50). Any order has 3! disjoint orders, giving six times that probability, about 0.002896.

Fox tags: independent tag losses each have probability p. Exactly one is 2p(1−p); at most one is 1−p². Their conditional ratio is 2p/(1+p), for 0≤p<1. At p=1, the condition has probability zero and the ratio is undefined.

Sports model: using the slide’s assumed values P(score first)=0.86, P(win|score first)=0.90, P(win|concede first)=0.20, with no ties, P(win)=0.86·0.90+0.14·0.20=0.802. These are exercise inputs, not independently verified historical sports statistics.

Exactly one red in two draws: add the BR and RB routes to obtain 2br/[(r+b)(r+b−1)].

05 · Follow the tree backward

Evidence asks which route you probably took.

A detector’s true-positive rate answers: “If the underlying condition is present, how often do we see a positive?” But after a positive, we ask the reverse: “Among all positives, how many actually have the condition?” Those are different denominators.

Take the slides’ hypothetical testing model: prevalence 0.1%, sensitivity 96%, false-positive rate 2%. Think of 100,000 people. There are 100 with the condition and 99,900 without. Expected positives: 96 from the first group, 1998 from the second. Of 2094 expected positives, only 96 come from the condition-present group.

P(condition | +) = 96/(96+1998) ≈ 0.045845 = 4.5845%

The positive result is informative: it raises the probability from 0.1% to about 4.58%. But a large majority of positives still come from the much larger condition-absent group. Both facts can be true.

Derive Bayes rather than memorize it

Start with P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B). Expand the numerator using the multiplication rule, and the denominator using all possible causes of B:

P(Aᵢ|B) = P(B|Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ) / ∑jP(B|Aⱼ)P(Aⱼ)

The prior P(Aᵢ) is the starting weight. The likelihood P(B|Aᵢ) scores how compatible the evidence is with that case. Their product is an unnormalized updated weight. Divide by the sum of all those weights to get a posterior that totals one.

Memory sentence

Posterior = the evidence coming from this explanation ÷ the evidence coming from every explanation.

Source correction: PDF page 27 specifies sensitivity 0.96, but page 29 substitutes 0.95. The calculation here consistently uses the stated 0.96.

Odds form: how much did the evidence multiply your belief?

For two complementary explanations, posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio. In the detector example, the likelihood ratio is 0.96/0.02=48. Starting odds are 1:999, so posterior odds are 48:999. That corresponds to 48/1047 ≈ 4.5845%.

This extension shows why strong evidence may still leave a rare explanation below 50%.

06 · Update, then predict

The urn you cannot see.

Choose urn A or B with equal probability. A has 12 red and 8 black balls; B has 4 red and 16 black. You draw two reds without replacement. Which urn did you probably choose?

The likelihoods are (12/20)(11/19)=132/380 for A and (4/20)(3/19)=12/380 for B. Equal priors cancel in the ratio. Posterior P(A|RR)=132/(132+12)=11/12.

Now predict a third red. The posterior tells us which urn is plausible; it does not restore the balls. If A was chosen, 10 of 18 remaining balls are red. If B, 2 of 18 are red.

P(R₃|RR) = (11/12)(10/18) + (1/12)(2/18) = 14/27 ≈ 0.51852

Notice the two separate updates: our belief about the urn changes, and its physical contents change. Forget either, and the predictive probability is wrong.

Worked slide exercise: flag plus unconfirmed message

The fraud prior is 0.05. The system flags fraud with probability 0.85 and legitimate transactions with probability 0.08. Thus flagged legitimate weight is 0.95·0.08=0.076; flagged fraud weight is 0.05·0.85=0.0425. P(legitimate|flag)=0.076/0.1185=152/237≈0.64135.

Given true status, assume the SMS response and flag mechanism are conditionally independent, as stated in the exercise. An unconfirmed SMS has probability 0.95 for fraud and 0.10 for legitimate. Updated weights are 0.0425·0.95=0.040375 and 0.076·0.10=0.0076.

P(fraud|flag, unconfirmed) = 0.040375/0.047975 ≈ 0.841584

Conditional independence means the multiplication works within each true-status group. It does not assert that flags and unconfirmed messages are independent overall.

07 · A large future from one next step

Gambler’s ruin: the next step contains the whole problem.

A gambler starts with i dollars. Each independent round adds one with probability p or subtracts one with probability q=1−p. The game stops at 0 or k. Let hᵢ be the probability of reaching k before 0 from i.

At the boundaries, h₀=0 and hₖ=1. From an interior state, condition on the next round:

hᵢ = p hᵢ₊₁ + q hᵢ₋₁

You do not need to enumerate the possible infinite-looking futures. After one step, the problem is identical except for the starting fortune.

A fair game produces a straight line

When p=q=1/2, each interior value is the average of its neighbors. Rearranging gives hᵢ₊₁−hᵢ=hᵢ−hᵢ₋₁: all increments are equal. The only straight line through (0,0) and (k,1) is hᵢ=i/k.

A biased game produces geometric increments

In general, p(hᵢ₊₁−hᵢ)=q(hᵢ−hᵢ₋₁). Each increment is q/p times the previous one. Summing these geometric increments and forcing the endpoint to be one yields:

hᵢ = [1 − (q/p)ⁱ] / [1 − (q/p)ᵏ],   p ≠ 1/2
hᵢ = i/k,   p = 1/2

Experiment · Small bias, large consequences

Can you reach 20 before zero?

Compare p=0.50 and p=0.45 from a starting fortune of 10. The curve is exact. Each sampled path is just one possible game.

A fair individual round does not mean a 50% chance of reaching a distant target: starting with 2 out of a total 20 gives only 10%. A small repeated disadvantage can matter greatly across a whole sequence.

The alternative recurrence method in the slides

Try hᵢ=rⁱ. Substitution gives pr²−r+q=0=(pr−q)(r−1). For p≠q, the two roots are 1 and q/p, giving hᵢ=A+B(q/p)ⁱ. Enforce h₀=0 and hₖ=1 to obtain the same expression. For p=q the repeated-root solution is linear, A+Bi.

The random-walk aside in the source concerns ideal infinite-lattice recurrence. Probability-one eventual return is not a finite-time guarantee or practical advice for finding someone. Simultaneous meetings of two walkers also require care about their movement rules and parity. Those distinctions are separate from this finite absorbing-boundary problem.
