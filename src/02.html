01 · Before the outcome exists

A crossroads with eight possible futures.

Three cars approach an intersection. Each will turn left or right. You do not know what they will do. But you can describe every future before any car moves.

Write the decisions in arrival order: LLL, LLR, LRL, LRR, RLL, RLR, RRL, RRR. That list is a sample space. A single item such as LRL is an outcome. A collection such as “exactly one turns right” is an event.

This distinction does more work than almost any formula in probability. If you describe outcomes carelessly, you can calculate perfectly and still answer the wrong question.

The three layers

First decide what can happen. Then decide which outcomes answer your question. Only then assign probabilities.

02 · A model of uncertainty

Unpredictable does not mean structureless.

A temperature conversion is deterministic: 68°F corresponds to 20°C. Given the input, the mathematical rule returns one output. A coin toss is modeled probabilistically: before observing it, we assign weight to heads and tails.

Probability can describe intrinsic variability, limited information, or both. A chaotic deterministic system may be hard to predict because small measurement errors grow. “Unpredictable” does not by itself prove that a system is intrinsically random.

Repeated fair, independent tosses have a long-run head proportion approaching one half. That does not require exactly 50 heads in every 100 tosses. Nor do five tails make a head “due.” The model assigns one half to the next toss regardless of that history.

More broadly, a model can assign probabilities to an entire future path—such as a sequence of stock prices—not just to a single number. We will later use random variables to extract numerical summaries from such outcomes.

The model behind “deterministic” and “stochastic” (PDF pp. 2–4, 9)

A deterministic rule is Y = f(x): fixing all relevant inputs fixes the output. It need not have a neat closed-form formula. The conversion rule is TC = (TF − 32)·5/9.

A common stochastic model is Y = f(x) + ε. If f(x) = E[Y | x], then E[ε | x] = 0; the remaining conditional variance describes how much uncertainty remains at those inputs. Under squared-error loss, the conditional mean is the best single-number prediction, just as the sample mean was the best constant in Lesson 1. A distribution tells us more than that one prediction.

Epistemic uncertainty comes from incomplete knowledge of the rule or its parameters. Aleatoric uncertainty is the variation the model leaves at fixed available inputs. What is irreducible depends on what we observe: adding a useful predictor can reduce conditional uncertainty. Hidden variables or chaos alone do not establish intrinsic randomness.

In the stock example, the current price is $125.50 and an outcome can be an entire path (p₁,…,p₁₀₀) over the next 100 days. “The day-100 price exceeds $150” is then an event containing many possible paths. These are illustrative model statements, not investment forecasts.

What makes a binomial model plausible?

Counting sixes in 10 independent fair-die rolls gives 10 yes/no trials with the same success probability 1/6. Counting email clicks has the same mathematical structure only if the corresponding independence and equal-probability assumptions are reasonable.

03 · Choose the right world

Finite, countable, or continuous?

Kind of spaceExampleWhat an outcome looks like
FiniteTwo diceAn ordered pair (first face, second face)
Countably infiniteToss until the first headH, TH, TTH, …; indexed by 1,2,3,…
Uncountably infiniteA point in a squareA pair of real coordinates (u,v)

“Countable” means you can list the possibilities in a sequence, even if the list never ends. Real numbers across an interval cannot be listed this way. We need lengths, areas, and eventually integrals to distribute probability continuously.

Formally, a countably infinite set has a one-to-one correspondence with 1, 2, 3, …: item i identifies one outcome and every outcome has an index. Unbounded counts, such as arrivals in a queue, often use such spaces; later models include geometric, Poisson, and negative-binomial distributions. In the “first head” example, the listed space records finite stopping times. If we model every possible infinite coin sequence instead, we also include the all-tails nontermination outcome; under independent fair tosses it has probability zero.

Two dice: distinguish the dice before counting

Use a red die and a blue die. The sample space is S = {(i,j): i,j ∈ {1,…,6}}, with 36 outcomes. (1,6) and (6,1) are different physical results. With independent fair dice, each ordered pair has probability 1/36.

If instead we record only the sum, possible values are 2 through 12. Those 11 values are not equally likely: sum 2 has one underlying pair, while sum 7 has six. Compressing the outcome changes the weights.

Experiment · Color the event

For sum at least 10, the six pairs are (4,6), (5,5), (6,4), (5,6), (6,5), and (6,6). That gives 6/36 = 1/6. Listing these is the proof.

What does “random chord” mean?

The slides pose a circle puzzle. To calculate a probability, “random” must identify a sampling procedure. Choosing two endpoints independently and uniformly on a circumference is one model; choosing a midpoint by another rule is a different model. We will solve the stated endpoint model in Lesson 4.

For Buffon’s needle, a useful outcome is the distance x from the needle’s midpoint to the nearest parallel line, together with its angle θ to the lines. Then 0 ≤ x ≤ d/2 and 0 ≤ θ ≤ π/2 after using symmetry. Under the standard uniform placement and orientation model, these two coordinates are independent and uniform on those intervals. For needle length ℓ ≤ d, crossing means x ≤ (ℓ/2)sin θ. The event is a region in this coordinate space.

Optional geometric bridge: how π enters the needle experiment

For a fixed angle θ, the fraction of possible midpoint distances that cross a line is ℓ sin θ/d. Average that fraction over a uniform angle:

P(cross) = (2/π) ∫₀π/2 (ℓ/d) sin θ dθ = 2ℓ/(πd).

The extra horizontal coordinate of the midpoint is irrelevant because shifting along a ruled line does not affect crossing. The grid variation in the slides adds distances to both vertical and horizontal lines. Choosing useful coordinates simplifies the problem before any calculation.

Recover the full needle and grid sample spaces (PDF pp. 8, 15–17)

The slides first describe a needle by its midpoint (u,v) and angle θ: u ∈ ℝ runs along the lines, v ∈ (0,d) locates it between two neighboring lines, and θ ∈ [0,2π) gives its orientation. Endpoints may be included without changing probabilities under a continuous model. Using [0,2π) avoids recording the same direction twice at 0 and 2π.

This is a geometric description, not yet a probability distribution. There is no uniform probability distribution on the entire real line, so we cannot simply say “uniform in u ∈ ℝ.” Since u has no effect on crossing, remove it. Fold the perpendicular distance to the nearest line and the angle using symmetry, leaving the compact space [0,d/2] × [0,π/2]. Now independent uniform coordinates are well-defined.

For the Buffon–Laplace grid, vertical lines are a units apart and horizontal lines b units apart. Use x ∈ [0,a/2] and y ∈ [0,b/2] for distances to the nearest lines, and θ ∈ [0,π/2] for angle to the horizontal. For ℓ < min(a,b), a crossing happens when x ≤ (ℓ/2)cos θ or y ≤ (ℓ/2)sin θ. Notice the union: crossing both still counts as a crossing.

A similar spatial problem is a dart landing uniformly on a rectangular board: the outcome is a coordinate pair and “inside the target” is a region. The recurring strategy is to name the coordinates first, then draw the event.

04 · Probability’s grammar

“Or,” “and,” and “not” are shapes.

On one die, let A = {2,4,6}, the even outcomes, and B = {1,2,3}, the outcomes below 4. Think of each event as a filter applied to the same list.

The symbol e ∈ A means “outcome e belongs to event A”; e ∉ A means it does not. Thus 2 ∈ A but 3 ∉ A. This is the same membership test used to filter a category in a data table. The empty set ∅ contains no outcomes and never occurs; the whole space S contains every outcome and always occurs.

Experiment · Logic you can inspect

One die, two filters.

Disjoint is a logical statement

Two events are disjoint, or mutually exclusive, if they share no outcomes: A ∩ B = ∅. For the cars, “exactly one right” and “all the same direction” cannot both happen. We have not yet defined independence; do not substitute that word for disjoint.

Subset means a guarantee within the model

A ⊆ B means every outcome in A is also in B. Then A ∩ B = A and A ∪ B = B. Complementing reverses inclusion: if every A is a B, then anything outside B must also be outside A, so Bᶜ ⊆ Aᶜ. Sets are only partially ordered by inclusion: {1} and {2} are incomparable.

De Morgan’s laws: negate the whole sentence

“Not (tea or coffee)” means “no tea and no coffee.” “Not (tea and coffee)” means “at least one of them is missing.” This translates into:

(A ∪ B)ᶜ = Aᶜ ∩ Bᶜ
(A ∩ B)ᶜ = Aᶜ ∪ Bᶜ

For many events, the same idea holds: none happen = every one fails; not all happen = at least one fails. Remembering the sentence is safer than memorizing where to put the bars.

Many events, one membership test (PDF pp. 22, 35–38)
⋃i=1k Aᵢ = A₁ ∪ ··· ∪ Aₖ   :   at least one happens
⋂i=1k Aᵢ = A₁ ∩ ··· ∩ Aₖ   :   every one happens

Replace k by ∞ for a countable list. If Aᵢ means “trial i succeeds,” the union over all positive integers means success occurs at some finite trial; the intersection means every trial succeeds.

(⋃i∈I Aᵢ)ᶜ = ⋂i∈I Aᵢᶜ
(⋂i∈I Aᵢ)ᶜ = ⋃i∈I Aᵢᶜ

These are set identities for any index set I. The proof is a sentence: an outcome is outside the union exactly when it is outside every member. In a probability model, closure under countable unions is guaranteed; an uncountable union of measurable events need not itself be measurable. Set algebra and probability additivity are different claims.

A compact set-algebra toolkit

Union and intersection are commutative and associative. They distribute over each other: A ∩ (B ∪ C) = (A ∩ B) ∪ (A ∩ C), and A ∪ (B ∩ C) = (A ∪ B) ∩ (A ∪ C).

A ∪ A = A ∩ A = A; (Aᶜ)ᶜ = A; A ∪ Aᶜ = S; A ∩ Aᶜ = ∅. Also A ∪ ∅ = A, A ∩ ∅ = ∅, A ∪ S = S, A ∩ S = A.

The especially useful partition is A = (A ∩ B) ∪ (A ∩ Bᶜ). The two pieces cannot overlap. To prove any identity, take an arbitrary outcome and check whether membership in the left-hand side is equivalent to membership in the right.

For example, if A ⊆ B, an outcome in Bᶜ cannot be in A, so Bᶜ ⊆ Aᶜ. Conversely, if Bᶜ ⊆ Aᶜ and some outcome were in A but outside B, it would also be outside A, a contradiction. Hence A ⊆ B if and only if Bᶜ ⊆ Aᶜ. We write ⊆ for inclusion allowing equality; some texts, including these slides, also use ⊂ that way.

05 · Give the world a budget of one

Probability is consistent bookkeeping.

Assign nonnegative probability to every measurable event. The entire sample space gets probability 1. When events have no overlap, their probabilities add—even for a countably infinite list. These are the three axioms.

  1. Nonnegativity: P(A) ≥ 0.
  2. Normalization: P(S) = 1.
  3. Countable additivity: for pairwise disjoint A₁,A₂,…, P(∪Aᵢ) = ∑P(Aᵢ).

The empty event has probability zero. Every event has probability at most one. And if there are N equally likely outcomes, each must get 1/N of the budget. An event containing m of them gets m/N:

P(A) = number of favorable outcomes / total number of outcomesThis counting shortcut requires a finite, equally likely sample space.

The cars’ eight futures are equally likely only under suitable assumptions—for example, independent choices with left/right each having probability one half. Then exactly one right has probability 3/8, and all the same has probability 2/8. If right turns have probability 0.8 independently, the eight strings no longer have equal weights.

Finish the cars exercise from the outcomes up (PDF pp. 20, 31, 41)

A = {RLL, LRL, LLR} contains exactly one right turn. C = {LLL, RRR} contains all turns in the same direction. The lists have no common string, so A ∩ C = ∅.

Under independent fair choices, each string has weight (1/2)³ = 1/8. Hence P(A) = 3/8 and P(C) = 2/8 = 1/4. Under independent right-turn probability 0.8, each of the three strings in A instead has probability 0.8·0.2²; P(A) = 0.096 and P(C) = 0.2³ + 0.8³ = 0.520. Counting outcomes alone works only in the equal-weight model.

A point can have probability zero without being impossible.

On a uniform continuum, each exact point has probability zero, yet some point is selected. Countable additivity does not let us add an uncountable collection of zeros to conclude that an interval has zero probability.

The formal layer: (Ω, ℱ, P)

Ω (also written S) contains outcomes. ℱ is the collection of measurable events; it contains Ω and is closed under complements and countable unions. P assigns their probabilities. In our finite examples, every subset is measurable. In continuous spaces, restricting the event collection avoids sets for which a consistent length or area cannot be assigned.

Worked practice: sketch the slides’ rectangle exercise (PDF p. 24)

A = {(x,y): 0 < x < 4, 0 < y < 3}; B = {(x,y): 2 < x < 4, 2 < y < 4}. Their intersection is the rectangle 2 < x < 4, 2 < y < 3, of area 2. The union is the two rectangles together, of area 12 + 4 − 2 = 14. These areas are not probabilities until a sampling region and distribution are specified.

Dashed edges mark strict inequalities: boundary points do not belong to the corresponding rectangle. The union’s outline is a stepped shape; its internal dashed lines are only construction guides, not removed points. For example, (3,3) belongs to B and hence the union, even though it is on A’s excluded top edge.

Worked practice: grid search and subset relationships

The grid-search sample space is the Cartesian product {100,200,300,400} × {10,20,30} × {gini,entropy}; there are 4·3·2 = 24 configurations. A configuration such as (200,20,gini) is one outcome.

Among A, B, A ∪ B, and Aᶜ ∩ B: A ⊆ A ∪ B, B ⊆ A ∪ B, and Aᶜ ∩ B ⊆ B ⊆ A ∪ B. Additional relationships require information about A and B.

World → question → weight.

Sample space: the world of possibilities. Event: the possibilities that answer yes. Probability: the weight assigned to that event.
