Normal models & practice · Slide 28

The 68–95–99.7 rule

For a normal-shaped distribution, SD becomes more than a distance scale: it tells us roughly how much of the distribution falls inside a centered interval. One, two, and three SDs give the familiar 68%, 95%, and 99.7% landmarks.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment
Let’s understand it

Three nested intervals

For a normal model,

$$ P(\mu-\sigma\le X\le\mu+\sigma)\approx0.68, $$

$$ P(\mu-2\sigma\le X\le\mu+2\sigma)\approx0.95, $$

$$ P(\mu-3\sigma\le X\le\mu+3\sigma)\approx0.997. $$

The source diagram marks $\mu$ in the center and $\mu\pm\sigma$, $\mu\pm2\sigma$, and $\mu\pm3\sigma$ along the horizontal axis. Each colored central region includes the narrower ones.

“Within one SD” means no more than one SD away on either side. Do not add 68%, 95%, and 99.7% together: these intervals overlap.

Apply the rule to the birth weights

Using the displayed mean 3.39 kg and SD 0.55 kg as a normal approximation:

Region Calculation Weight interval Approximate share
Within 1 SD $3.39\pm0.55$ 2.84–3.94 kg 68%
Within 2 SDs $3.39\pm1.10$ 2.29–4.49 kg 95%
Within 3 SDs $3.39\pm1.65$ 1.74–5.04 kg 99.7%

These are model-based approximations, not exact counts of the 3,226 babies. A finite sample from a normal population need not place exactly 68% of its values inside its sample mean plus or minus one sample SD.

Split the tails before reading a percentage

If approximately 95% lie within two SDs, approximately 5% lie outside. Symmetry splits that remainder equally:

$$ P(X>\mu+2\sigma)\approx\frac{5\%}{2}=2.5\%. $$

Thus about 2.5% would exceed 4.49 kg under the birth-weight approximation, and about 2.5% would fall below 2.29 kg.

Distance from the mean Approximate share inside Outside in both tails In each tail
1 SD 68% 32% 16%
2 SDs 95% 5% 2.5%
3 SDs 99.7% 0.3% 0.15%

The region between one and two SDs contains approximately $95\%-68\%=27\%$ across both sides, or about 13.5% on either side.

The shape assumption matters

The slide says “symmetric and bell-shaped.” Use these percentages when the shape is close enough to normal for the approximation to be useful. Symmetry alone is insufficient: a uniform distribution is symmetric but flat, and symmetric single-peaked distributions can have much heavier tails than a normal distribution.

Knowing only a mean and an SD does not guarantee the empirical-rule percentages. A standardized distance and a probability are different pieces of information.

Check your understanding

For approximately normal data, what fraction lies between one and two SDs above the mean? What fraction lies more than two SDs above it?

Show the answer

The first fraction is approximately $(95\%-68\%)/2=13.5\%$. The second is approximately $(100\%-95\%)/2=2.5\%$. The division by two separates the symmetric left and right sides.
