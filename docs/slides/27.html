Normal models & practice · Slide 27

Read a bell-shaped distribution with a spread ruler

A histogram shows the observed landscape. A normal curve is a model laid over it. The mean places the model's center, and standard deviation sets its horizontal scale.

~2 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

The birth-weight picture

The slide illustrates its bell-shaped pattern using birth weights of 3,226 newborn babies, credited to O'Cathain et al., 2002. Its annotations are:

Quantity Displayed value
Number of newborns $n=3{,}226$
Mean birth weight $3.39$ kg
Standard deviation $0.55$ kg

The horizontal axis measures birthweight in kilograms, displayed roughly from 0 to 7 kg. The vertical axis is frequency, displayed from 0 to about 400. Most observations form a mound around 3–4 kg, with thinner tails on either side; a smooth bell-shaped curve is drawn over the bars.

The figure contains rounded summaries and a histogram, not the individual weights or an exact table of bin counts. We should not invent those unavailable observations.

A normal distribution is a specific model

A normal distribution, also called a Gaussian distribution, has a symmetric bell shape. Most probability is near its mean; the tails taper away on both sides. Increasing its SD widens the bell; decreasing SD narrows it. Changing its mean shifts the whole curve.

But not every symmetric distribution is normal. Even a bell-like, single-peaked shape can have different tail proportions. Treat the slide's “symmetric and bell-shaped” description as an introduction to the normal model, not a test that proves normality.

Put a weight on the SD ruler

The displayed mean is 3.39 kg and one SD is 0.55 kg. A weight of 4.49 kg is

$$ 4.49-3.39=1.10\text{ kg} $$

above the mean. In SD units,

$$ z=\frac{4.49-3.39}{0.55}=2. $$

So it is two SDs above the mean. Likewise,

$$ \frac{2.84-3.39}{0.55}=-1, $$

so 2.84 kg is one SD below the mean.

A $z$-score answers “how far from the mean, measured with this spread ruler?” To answer “what percentage of the distribution lies below this value?” we also need its shape. The empirical rule supplies approximate percentages for a normal shape.

Check your understanding

Does a $z$-score of 2, by itself, prove that only about 2.5% of observations are above that value?

Show the answer

No. It says the value is two SDs above the mean. The approximately 2.5% upper-tail interpretation additionally uses a normal approximation. Other distributions can have different tail probabilities at the same standardized distance.
