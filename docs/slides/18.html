Understanding spread · Slide 18

Quartiles divide probability, not distance

Quartiles mark where accumulated probability reaches one quarter, one half, and three quarters. In the density picture, those are equal areas. They do not have to be equal-width intervals or points where the curve has a particular height.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Read the running total

The cumulative distribution function, or CDF, is

$$ F(x)=P(X\le x). $$

Imagine sweeping a vertical line from left to right under the density. $F(x)$ records how much probability you have collected by the time the line reaches $x$.

For the continuous distribution illustrated above:

Landmark Name Accumulated probability
$Q_1$ First quartile, 25th percentile $F(Q_1)=0.25$
$Q_2$ Median, 50th percentile $F(Q_2)=0.50$
$Q_3$ Third quartile, 75th percentile $F(Q_3)=0.75$

The source picture labels them approximately $-0.67$, $0$, and $0.67$. Those are positions on its horizontal value axis. They are not density heights.

Four colors, four equal amounts of probability

The blue region is the bottom quarter. Green runs from $Q_1$ to the median. Red runs from the median to $Q_3$. Purple is the top quarter.

For a continuous density $f$,

$$ F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt. $$

The four colored regions therefore satisfy

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{Q_1}f(t)\,dt =\int_{Q_1}^{Q_2}f(t)\,dt =\int_{Q_2}^{Q_3}f(t)\,dt =\int_{Q_3}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt =0.25. $$

Where the density is tall, a short interval can collect a quarter of the probability. In a thin tail, collecting another quarter can require a much longer interval. Equal probability does not mean equal distance.

The middle two colors together contain half the probability:

$$ P(Q_1\le X\le Q_3)=0.75-0.25=0.50. $$

Their width is the interquartile range. Using the picture's rounded cutoffs gives $0.67-(-0.67)=1.34$.

What changes for an actual finite sample?

A sample's empirical CDF is a staircase:

$$ F_n(x)=\frac{\#\{i:x_i\le x\}}{n}. $$

For five distinct observations it jumps by 20% at each observation. Ties produce larger jumps. There may be no observation or threshold at which the cumulative proportion equals exactly 25%.

A standard general population-quantile definition is

$$ Q(u)=\inf\{x:F(x)\ge u\}. $$

It selects the first location where the CDF reaches or passes the target. In a distribution with jumps, $F(Q(u))$ can exceed $u$. Interpolated sample quantiles also need not be literal inverse-empirical-CDF cutoffs.

This is why the slide's exact equalities fit its continuous illustration but cannot be imposed unchanged on every finite or discrete dataset.

Are quartiles the medians of the two halves?

That is one useful family of sample conventions, and the slide uses this description. It is not the only quantile algorithm. With an odd sample size, you must also decide whether to include the overall median in either half.

The median can be calculated without assuming normality; this is the intended sense of “nonparametric.” It resists isolated extreme values better than the mean, but it is not immune to every possible pattern of contamination.

Check your understanding

On a density graph, would you find $Q_1$ by locating the point where the density's height equals 0.25?

Reveal the reasoning

No. Find the horizontal location where the area to the left is 0.25. The density height describes how concentrated probability is near a point; the CDF records accumulated probability. The two are different quantities.
