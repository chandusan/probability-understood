Finding the centre · Slide 03

The mean preserves the total

The mean is what everyone would receive if you pooled the total and shared it equally. This is why it responds to every observation, including an extreme one: changing any contribution changes the shared total.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

The formula says “add, then share”

For $n$ numerical observations,

$$ \bar{x}=\frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n}{n}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i. $$

Read the notation first

  • $x_i$ means observation number $i$.
  • $n$ is the number of observations.
  • $\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i$ means add $x_1$ through $x_n$.
  • $\bar{x}$ is pronounced “x bar.”

For waiting times $3,6,9$:

$$ \bar{x}=\frac{3+6+9}{3}=6. $$

Picture 1: pour everything into one bowl

Pool the total waiting time:

$$ 3+6+9=18\text{ customer-minutes}. $$

Redistribute that total equally across the three customers. Each receives six minutes. The mean is therefore the equal-share value that preserves the total:

$$ n\bar{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i. $$

If total cost, total waiting time, or total output matters, this is a useful property. Multiplying the mean by the number of observations recovers the total.

Picture 2: balance equal weights on a ruler

Place equal weights at $3$, $6$, and $9$. The ruler balances at $6$.

Now move the weight at $9$ to $12$. The total increases by $3$. Because there are three observations, the mean increases by $3/3=1$:

$$ \frac{3+6+12}{3}=7. $$

In general, changing one observation by $\Delta$ changes the mean by

$$ \frac{\Delta}{n}. $$

This is why a very distant observation can pull the mean strongly: its distance affects the total.

A fact that will become important later

The signed deviations from the sample mean sum to zero:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x}) =\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i-n\bar{x} =0. $$

For $3,6,12$, the mean is $7$, and the deviations are

$$ -4,-1,5. $$

Their sum is zero. This balancing property will explain both the mean's projection geometry and why deviations from an estimated mean cannot vary independently.

Important distinctions

  • Independence is not needed to calculate a descriptive mean. It matters for some claims about what the mean estimates or how it behaves across repeated samples.
  • Calling the sample mean an “expected value” is exact if we define an empirical distribution that gives each observed entry weight $1/n$. It does not mean that the observed sample mean must equal the unknown population mean $\mu$.
  • The balancing picture assumes equal weights. A weighted mean corresponds to observations with unequal weights.

Check your understanding

You have four observations. One observation increases by 12, and the other three stay unchanged. By how much does the mean increase?

Work through the answer

The total increases by $12$ while the number of observations remains $4$. Therefore the mean increases by $12/4=3$. You do not need to know the original observations to answer this. The same information does not generally determine the change in the median, because that depends on the sorted positions.
