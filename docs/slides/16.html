Understanding spread · Slide 16

One faulty reading can squeeze the scale

Min–max normalization measures each observation's position between the recorded minimum and maximum. If one faulty reading moves an endpoint far away, it changes the scale for every healthy reading too.

~4 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment
Let’s understand it

Turn a distance into a fraction

The transformation is

$$ x'_i=\frac{x_i-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}. $$

The numerator asks how far $x_i$ lies above the minimum. The denominator gives the full observed span. Dividing tells us what fraction of that span we have traveled.

The minimum maps to 0, the maximum maps to 1, and a value halfway between them maps to 0.5. The fitting sample lies in the closed interval $[0,1]$, including its endpoints.

Watch the motor sensor

An industrial motor produces five normal temperature readings and one faulty reading:

Temperature (°C) Sensor state Scaled value shown in the slide
44.8 Normal — minimum 0.000
45.2 Normal 0.007
45.9 Normal 0.018
46.1 Normal 0.021
46.5 Normal 0.028
105.3 Hardware fault — maximum 1.000

Without the fault, the range is

$$ 46.5-44.8=1.7. $$

With the fault, it becomes

$$ 105.3-44.8=60.5. $$

The largest healthy value used to map to 1. It now maps to

$$ \frac{46.5-44.8}{60.5}=\frac{1.7}{60.5}\approx0.02810. $$

All five healthy readings now occupy only the first 2.81% of the scale.

Temperature Scaling using the healthy range 1.7 Scaling using the contaminated range 60.5

45.2 0.23529 0.00661
45.9 0.64706 0.01818
46.1 0.76471 0.02149

For instance, the new value for 45.2 is $(45.2-44.8)/60.5\approx0.00661$. Nothing about this healthy reading changed. Its denominator did.

Try it: predict before changing the maximum

Predict what will happen to the scaled gap between 44.8 and 46.5 as the largest reading moves farther away. Then change the extreme reading and compare the original temperatures with their normalized positions.

Look for the shared denominator: the ordinary readings become closer together on the normalized scale even though their differences in degrees stay fixed. Scaling cannot decide whether the extreme value is credible; that requires understanding the sensor and the data.

Why this is called a robustness problem

Replace just one observation by an arbitrarily large value and the range can grow without limit. For a sample of size $n\ge2$, the finite-sample replacement breakdown fraction is $1/n$. This tends to zero as $n$ grows, explaining the slide's phrase “zero breakdown point.” It is not literally zero for a fixed finite sample.

Distance-based methods such as k-nearest neighbors and many neural-network training procedures are sensitive to feature scales. Appropriate scaling can help, but the claim that all such methods universally “require” min–max normalization is too strong. It is one possible transformation, and a fault remains a data-quality problem.

Two boundary cases

If all observations are equal, the range is zero and the written formula divides by zero; software needs an explicit convention.

If you keep the fitted endpoints fixed, a future value outside them can transform below 0 or above 1. Min–max normalization does not automatically clip new observations.

Check your understanding

You fit min–max scaling using minimum 10 and maximum 20. What happens to a new observation of 25? Does that contradict the claim that the fitting data lie in $[0,1]$?

Reveal the calculation

The new value becomes $(25-10)/(20-10)=1.5$. There is no contradiction: the interval guarantee applies to observations inside the fitted endpoints. A future observation can lie outside them unless you separately choose to clip it.
