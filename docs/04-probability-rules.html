Complements, symmetry, collisions, and inclusion–exclusion.

01 · Ask the easier question

Twenty-three strangers. One surprising coincidence.

Twenty-three people enter a room. Would you bet that two share a birthday? Most people imagine one birthday trying to match another. But the room contains 253 possible pairs.

Our task is not to memorize a surprising percentage. It is to find a way to describe the event without drowning in overlapping cases. This lesson builds three tactics: complements, symmetry, and inclusion–exclusion.

The birthday calculation uses an idealized model: independent birthdays, each uniform over 365 days, ignoring leap days. The result describes that model, not every real group of people.

02 · None is simpler than some

Count the room where nobody matches.

“At least one shared birthday” includes one pair, several pairs, triples, and larger clusters. Listing all those patterns is unpleasant. Its complement—everyone different—is easy to build one arrival at a time.

  1. The first person can have any birthday: probability 365/365.
  2. The second must avoid one occupied day: 364/365.
  3. The third must avoid two occupied days: 363/365.
  4. Person n must avoid n − 1 occupied days: (365 − n + 1)/365.
P(at least one match) = 1 − ∏j=0n−1(365−j)/365For n ≤ 365. For n > 365, a match is certain by the pigeonhole principle.

With n = 23, the answer is about 0.5073. With 22 it is about 0.4757. The complement rule comes directly from splitting the whole probability budget: S = E ∪ Eᶜ, with disjoint pieces, so P(E) + P(Eᶜ) = 1.

Experiment · Collision laboratory

How crowded is a “small” room?

Guess when the probability first passes 50%. Change the number of people, then sample rooms. Exact probability and simulation are shown separately.

There are C(23,2)=253 pairs, each with match probability 1/365. That makes 253/365 ≈ 0.693 the expected number of matching pairs, not the probability of at least one. A room can contribute several matching pairs, so adding pair probabilities counts that room repeatedly.

Recognize the same story elsewhere

Six independently and uniformly located incidents in six districts have 6⁶ assignments. “Some district has more than one” is the complement of one incident per district. There are 6! one-per-district assignments, so the answer is 1 − 6!/6⁶ ≈ 0.98457.

Likewise, at least one six in three independent fair-die rolls has probability 1 − (5/6)³ = 91/216. The word at least should make you consider a complement, though it does not guarantee one will be easiest.

03 · A local question inside a large system

How many balls reach one bin?

Throw m distinct balls independently and uniformly into n distinct bins. Pick one particular bin before throwing. To put exactly k balls there, first choose which k of the m they are. Every remaining ball has n − 1 allowable destinations.

P(exactly k in the chosen bin)
= C(m,k)(n−1)m−k/nᵐ
= C(m,k)(1/n)ᵏ(1−1/n)m−k

The denominator counts all assignments. The numerator both puts k in and keeps all others out. That second obligation is what makes “exactly” work.

This is a binomial probability: m independent opportunities, each hitting our bin with probability 1/n. But occupancies of different bins are not independent—their total must be m. One bin receiving extra balls leaves fewer for the others.

Repeated letters: the slides’ scrambled-poem example

PDF pages 15–16 use a fixed multiset of 59 letters and ask for one exact ordering. If letter j appears nⱼ times, the number of distinguishable strings is 59!/∏nⱼ!. A uniform random permutation of physical tiles makes each visible ordering equally likely because each has exactly ∏nⱼ! labeled versions.

Therefore the desired ordering has probability ∏nⱼ!/59!, provided the target uses precisely the available counts. Spaces, capitalization, and punctuation are not randomized tiles in this model. The reasoning is the same as BANANA in Lesson 3.

04 · Repair double-counting

A person in two clubs is still one person.

Suppose 20% of outcomes belong to A, 35% to B, and 8% to both. Adding 20% + 35% counts the overlap twice. We want it once, so remove one copy:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) = 0.47

This is the slides’ Olympic-host-city example, treated as a supplied probability model. No independence assumption is needed. Partitioning into A only, B only, and both gives the same result.

Three events: why subtracting is not the final step

An outcome in all three events is initially counted three times. Subtracting the three pairwise intersections removes it three times, leaving zero. We wanted one. Add the triple intersection back.

P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = P(A)+P(B)+P(C)
− P(A∩B)−P(A∩C)−P(B∩C)
+ P(A∩B∩C)

Experiment · Follow one outcome

How many copies did we count?

Choose which events contain an outcome. Step through the arithmetic. Any outcome inside the union must finish with weight one.

For n events, alternate signs across intersections of sizes 1, 2, 3, … . An outcome in r events gets weight C(r,1) − C(r,2) + ··· + (−1)ʳ⁺¹C(r,r) = 1. The binomial theorem from Lesson 3 explains the correction.

A bridge hand: keep numerator and denominator compatible

A hand contains 13 of 52 cards, so N = C(52,13) = 635,013,559,600. Let A mean exactly five spades and B mean exactly five hearts.

P(A) = C(13,5)C(39,8)/N
P(A∩B) = C(13,5)²C(26,3)/N
P(A∪B) = 2P(A) − P(A∩B) ≈ 0.242602

The intersection chooses five spades, five hearts, and three cards from the 26 clubs and diamonds. Its count is 4,306,559,400. The source’s expanded multiplication accidentally repeats 61,523,748; the correct last factor is C(26,3)=2600. The symbolic formula and final intersection count are correct.

Here “exactly five spades or exactly five hearts” is inclusive “or.” A hand with six spades and no five-card heart suit does not satisfy this particular event.

05 · Let symmetry do the heavy lifting

Count what must happen, then share it fairly.

The tennis tournament

Every match eliminates exactly one player. To go from n players to one champion, there must be n − 1 matches, regardless of byes. No pair can meet twice because the loser leaves.

Under equally skilled players and pairings/byes chosen symmetrically, every unordered pair has the same chance p to meet. Sum the indicator “this pair meets” over all C(n,2) pairs. In every tournament it sums to n − 1. Taking expectations gives C(n,2)p = n − 1:

P(A and B ever meet) = 2/n

This proof does not require tracing every round. It uses an invariant—the number of eliminations—and symmetry. Unequal skill or a fixed bracket can break the exchangeability needed for the result.

Two chords cross

Select four endpoints independently and uniformly on a circle, joining A to B and C to D. With probability one they are distinct. Fix A as the starting point around the circumference. The clockwise orders of B,C,D are equally likely: BCD, BDC, CBD, CDB, DBC, DCB.

The chords cross exactly when their endpoints alternate: A–C–B–D or A–D–B–C. That is two orders out of six, so the answer is 1/3. The conclusion belongs to this endpoint-sampling model; “random chords” alone is incomplete.

06 · The same rule, more ambitious puzzles

Names in a hat and all six die faces.

Secret Santa: at least one person draws themselves

Model the drawing as a uniformly random permutation of n names, without replacement. Let Aᵢ be “person i draws their own name.” If a particular set of k people all draw themselves, the other n − k names can be permuted in (n − k)! ways out of n!.

There are C(n,k) such sets. Their total contribution in inclusion–exclusion simplifies to C(n,k)(n − k)!/n! = 1/k!.

P(at least one self-match) = 1 − 1/2! + 1/3! − ··· + (−1)n+1/n!
P(no self-match) = ∑k=0n(−1)ᵏ/k! → 1/e

Thus for large n, at least one self-match has probability about 0.6321. More people do not make the probability approach one: each individual self-match becomes rarer at exactly the same time.

Extension: only a specified m people must avoid themselves

Apply inclusion–exclusion only to the m forbidden self-matches. The remaining people may draw anyone, including themselves:

P = ∑k=0m(−1)ᵏ C(m,k)(n−k)!/n!

Setting m=n recovers the derangement probability. Setting m=1 gives 1−1/n.

Ten dice: every face appears

Define Aⱼ as the event that face j is missing. If a chosen group of j faces is missing, each of ten dice has 6 − j choices. Inclusion–exclusion counts the assignments with no missing faces:

P(all six appear) = ∑j=06(−1)ʲ C(6,j)(6−j)¹⁰ / 6¹⁰

The favorable count is 16,435,440, so the probability is about 0.271812. This is the birthday problem’s relative: both ask how labeled objects occupy categories.

Worked slide exercise: divisibility by 2, 3, or 7

In 1,…,2019, the counts divisible by 2, 3, 7 are 1009, 673, 288. Pair intersections count multiples of 6, 14, 21: 336, 144, 96. The triple counts multiples of 42: 48.

1009+673+288−336−144−96+48 = 1442

Divide by 2019 because integers are selected uniformly.

Worked slide exercise: a run of at least five equal digits

Consider seven-digit strings, allowing leading zeroes. A five-digit run may start at positions 1, 2, or 3. Each start gives 10³ strings. Intersections of adjacent starts force six equal digits and give 100 strings each. Starts 1 and 3 force all seven equal and give 10; the triple intersection also gives 10.

Favorable = 3000 − (100+100+10) + 10 = 2800
P = 2800/10⁷

The overlap correction is essential because one long run contains multiple five-digit windows.

Worked slide exercise: a no-repeat word uses all 26 letters

There are P(26,k)=26!/(26−k)! strings of length k with distinct letters. Under uniform choice among all nonempty such strings, the required probability is:

26! / ∑k=12626!/(26−k)!
= 1 / ∑j=0251/j! ≈ 1/e

Uniformly choosing a length first would define a different model and yield probability 1/26 instead.

07 · Choose a strategy

Before computing, simplify.

1. Why not add 1/365 over every pair in the birthday problem?

2. An outcome lies in A, B, and C. After adding singles and subtracting pairs, how many copies remain?

Finish the lottery question from the slides

In the simplified model where fewer than three matches wins nothing, P(no prize) = (6,096,454 + 5,775,588 + 1,851,150)/13,983,816 = 13,723,192/13,983,816 ≈ 0.981362. Its complement is about 0.018638.

Three escape routes from a complicated count.

Complement: count the easy opposite. Symmetry: share an invariant equally. Inclusion–exclusion: correct every extra copy.
