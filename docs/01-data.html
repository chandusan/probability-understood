The story inside the data

Mean, median, and the shape of variation.

Suggested pace: 25–35 minutes with experiments · Based on 31 PDF pages

01 · A number that tells the truth

The café with the “average” wait.

You are choosing a café before class. One promises an average wait of six minutes. Is that enough information to decide?

Imagine three customers waiting 3, 6, and 9 minutes. Six feels like a fair description. Now the last customer waits 12 minutes. The mean rises to 7, but the middle customer still waits 6. Neither answer is a mistake. They answer different questions.

A statistical summary is a deliberate compression. We trade a long list for a small number, so we must ask what we want to preserve. Total time? A middle experience? Unpredictability? This lesson turns those choices into the mean, median, range, quartiles, and standard deviation.

Your destination

Explain why two centers can both be sensible, why a center needs a spread, and which summaries survive an extreme observation.

02 · Build the mean

Pour everything into one bowl.

Put the three waiting times into a common pool: 3 + 6 + 9 = 18 customer-minutes. Redistribute that total equally. Each customer gets 6. That is the arithmetic mean: the equal-share value that preserves the total.

x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + ··· + xₙ) / nxᵢ is observation i; n is the number of observations; x̄ is pronounced “x bar.”

There is a second picture. Place equal weights at 3, 6, and 9 on a ruler. The ruler balances at 6. Moving the last weight from 9 to 12 pulls the balance point right by exactly 3/3 = 1. An outlier has leverage because its distance matters.

Why squared error chooses this center

Suppose you must predict every waiting time with one number c. Define the error for customer i as xᵢ − c. Adding signed errors is unhelpful: +5 and −5 cancel. Squaring makes both count as a cost, and makes a miss of 10 cost four times a miss of 5.

SSE(c) = ∑(xᵢ − c)²
SSE(c) = ∑(xᵢ − x̄)² + n(c − x̄)²

The second line is the whole proof. The first term does not depend on your choice of c. The second is nonnegative and becomes zero only when c = x̄. Therefore the mean minimizes squared error.

See why that identity works—and the geometry behind the slide

Write xᵢ − c = (xᵢ − x̄) + (x̄ − c), square, and sum. The cross-term vanishes because ∑(xᵢ − x̄) = 0. What remains is the identity above.

For the geometric picture, regard the dataset as one point (x₁,…,xₙ). Every constant prediction is a point (c,…,c) on a line. The closest point on that line is (x̄,…,x̄). Its residual is perpendicular to the line because the residual coordinates sum to zero. This is the simplest example of least squares.

03 · Build the median

Stand where half the people are on each side.

Now imagine putting a help desk on a straight road where your customers live. The cost is walking distance, not squared distance. Move the desk slightly right: every person to its left walks a little farther; every person to its right walks a little less.

If more people are on the right, move right. If more are on the left, move left. The movement stops helping when the counts balance. The median minimizes total absolute distance. Faraway people do not pull harder; each contributes one vote about direction.

Median ∈ arg minc ∑|xᵢ − c|

Sort the data first. With an odd number of observations, take the middle one. With an even number, the usual sample median is the average of the two middle values. Every point between those two middle values minimizes absolute error, so the minimizer need not be unique.

Experiment · The outlier’s leverage

Make the third customer wait.

Predict first: as the last wait grows, which center will move? Then move the slider. The candidate c lets you compare the two loss functions.

For 3, 6, 12, the mean is 7 and the median is 6. At c = 7, squared error is 16 + 1 + 25 = 42; at c = 6, it is 9 + 0 + 36 = 45. Absolute error reverses that comparison: 10 at c = 7, versus 9 at c = 6.

Memory image: balance point versus middle person.

The mean cares about how far. The median cares about how many are on each side.

For a roughly symmetric distribution, the two centers often agree. A long right tail typically pulls the mean above the median; a long left tail typically pulls it below. These are useful visual tendencies, not universal theorems about every distribution.

Choose the mean when totals or squared prediction errors matter. Choose the median when describing a middle experience or resisting extremes matters. Reporting both can be more honest than declaring one universally “better.”

04 · The missing half of the story

Same center. Different lives.

Two cafés record waits of 5, 6, 7 and 0, 6, 12 minutes. Both average 6. One is dependable; the other is a gamble. Likewise, scoring 88 on an exam with mean 76 feels different when almost everyone scores near 76 than when scores are widely scattered.

Range: inspect the two ends

Range = maximum − minimum

The range is 2 in the first café and 12 in the second. It is quick, understandable, and fragile: one new extreme changes everything. In the slides’ sensor example, normal temperatures span 44.8 to 46.5, a range of 1.7. A faulty value of 105.3 makes the range 60.5. Min-max scaling then compresses all the normal values into roughly the first 2.8% of the scale.

Quartiles: inspect the middle half

A percentile is a position in the ordered data. Q₁ is the 25th percentile, Q₂ the median, and Q₃ the 75th percentile. The interquartile range is the width of the middle half:

IQR = Q₃ − Q₁

Small datasets do not always split into exact quarters. There are several legitimate percentile conventions. In this course’s worked sample, we use linear interpolation at zero-based position (n − 1)p for quantile proportion p. For sorted 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, Q₁ = 2.75 and Q₃ = 6.25, giving IQR = 3.5. A median-of-halves convention instead gives Q₁ = 2.5 and Q₃ = 6.5. State the convention before comparing answers.

Read a boxplot as a compressed distribution

The box spans Q₁ to Q₃, and the line inside marks the median. In a common Tukey boxplot, whiskers extend to the most extreme observations still within the fences Q₁ − 1.5 IQR and Q₃ + 1.5 IQR. Points beyond the whiskers are shown separately. A flagged point deserves investigation; the plot cannot tell you it is a mistake.

05 · Give every deviation a voice

Why variance squares, and why we take the square root.

The range ignores all but two observations. IQR ignores much of the tail detail. If we want every distance from the mean to contribute, we can square the deviations and add them.

s² = ∑(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1),   s = √s²Sample variance s² has squared units. Sample standard deviation s has the original units. These require n ≥ 2.

For the heart rates 50, 51, 57, 61, 70, 71, 80, 84, the mean is 65.5 beats per minute. The squared deviations total 1146. Thus s² = 1146/7 ≈ 163.714 (beats/minute)² and s ≈ 12.795 beats/minute.

Standard deviation is a scale for a typical deviation, but it is not the arithmetic average absolute distance from the mean. Here the average absolute deviation is 10.75, not 12.8.

Why divide by n − 1?

We fitted the center using the same observations. That fitting makes their squared distances too small, on average, as an estimate of population variability. One constraint also appears: after you know n − 1 deviations from the sample mean, the last is forced because all deviations sum to zero.

The exact reason Bessel’s correction works

For i.i.d. observations with population mean μ and finite variance σ²:

∑(Xᵢ − X̄)² = ∑(Xᵢ − μ)² − n(X̄ − μ)²

Take expectations. The first term becomes nσ². Since Var(X̄) = σ²/n, the subtracted term has expectation σ². The remainder is (n − 1)σ². Dividing by n − 1 makes s² unbiased. The square root s is not generally unbiased for σ.

If you are describing an entire finite population, averaging its squared deviations with denominator N is appropriate. “Always use n − 1” is therefore too broad.

Adding 10 to every observation shifts the center but leaves spread unchanged. Multiplying every observation by 10 multiplies the range, IQR, and standard deviation by 10, and the variance by 100. Reordering observations changes none of these summaries.

When standard deviation becomes a ruler

A z-score, z = (x − mean)/standard deviation, expresses how far a value is from its mean in standard-deviation units. It is undefined if the standard deviation is zero.

For a normal distribution, approximately 68%, 95%, and 99.7% lie within 1, 2, and 3 standard deviations of the mean. This gives a useful approximation for data that are close to normal. Mere symmetry does not guarantee those percentages.

Connection to data science

Scaling helps compare features measured in different units, and PCA seeks directions of large variance. Large variance alone does not mean a feature predicts your target. “Variance of a feature” and “variance of a fitted model across training samples” are different quantities.

Worked practice: finish the slides’ standard-deviation comparisons

1. {2,4,6,8} has ten times the standard deviation of {0.2,0.4,0.6,0.8}.
2. {1,3,5,7,9} and {3,5,7,9,1} have equal standard deviations: order is irrelevant.
3. Adding another 9 to {1,3,5,7,9} increases sample variance from 10 to 32/3 ≈ 10.667.
4. Adding another 5 decreases sample variance from 10 to 8. The squared-deviation total stays 40 while the divisor grows from 4 to 5.

For the professor-rating dot plots, the spread ranking is B < A < D < C. B is constant; C concentrates observations at the extremes. This is about spread, not which professor has the highest average rating.

Precision notes for the source slides

Computing a descriptive mean or range does not require independent observations; assumptions matter for inferential guarantees. Quantile definitions should use a stated convention rather than mixing rank formulas. In general a p-quantile need not satisfy F(q) = p exactly; jumps can cross p.

The five-number summary uses the actual minimum and maximum; Tukey whisker endpoints can differ. Median robustness is approximately 50% in the usual large-sample breakdown sense; “up to half can always be corrupted safely” is too strong at finite-sample boundaries. Range and mean can be driven arbitrarily far by one replacement, a fraction 1/n that approaches zero.

Carry these three pictures.

Mean: a balancing ruler. Median: the middle person. Spread: how much the individual experiences differ from that one-number story.
