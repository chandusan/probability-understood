01

A joint continuous density

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two continuous random variables with a joint pdf given by

$$ f(x,y)=cx^2y(1+y),\qquad 0\le x\le3,\quad 0\le y\le3. $$

The density is zero outside this region.

(a) · 6 points

Find the value of $c$ that makes this a valid density function.

(b) · 6 points

Find the marginal pdf $f_X(x)$.

Worked solution

(a) Find the normalizing constant

A joint density describes how probability is spread across the $(x,y)$ plane. To be a valid density, it must be nonnegative, and the probability over its entire support must add up to $1$.

Here the support is the square $0\le x\le3$, $0\le y\le3$. Both $x^2$ and $y(1+y)$ are nonnegative there, so choosing a positive $c$ will ensure nonnegativity. To determine its value, impose

$$ \int_0^3\int_0^3 cx^2y(1+y)\,dx\,dy=1. $$

First integrate with respect to $x$. During this inner integral, $y$ is held fixed. Therefore $cy(1+y)$ acts as a constant, and

$$ \int_0^3 cx^2y(1+y)\,dx =cy(1+y)\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^3 =9cy(1+y). $$

This has added up the density along the entire horizontal range for each fixed $y$. Now add over all $y$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \int_0^3 9cy(1+y)\,dy &=9c\int_0^3(y+y^2)\,dy\\ &=9c\left[\frac{y^2}{2}+\frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^3\\ &=9c\left(\frac92+9\right)\\ &=9c\cdot\frac{27}{2} =\frac{243c}{2}. \end{aligned} $$

Set this total probability equal to $1$:

$$ \frac{243c}{2}=1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{c=\frac{2}{243}}. $$

This value is positive, so both requirements for a density are satisfied. The density is understood to be zero outside the stated square.

(b) Find the marginal density of $X$

The joint density keeps track of both $X$ and $Y$. The marginal density of $X$ answers a question about $X$ alone, allowing every possible value of $Y$. That is why we integrate out $y$:

$$ f_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dy. $$

For a fixed $x$ between $0$ and $3$, only $0\le y\le3$ contributes. Here $x$ stays fixed, so $2x^2/243$ can be taken outside the integral:

$$ \begin{aligned} f_X(x) &=\int_0^3\frac{2}{243}x^2y(1+y)\,dy\\ &=\frac{2x^2}{243}\int_0^3(y+y^2)\,dy\\ &=\frac{2x^2}{243}\left[\frac{y^2}{2}+\frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^3\\ &=\frac{2x^2}{243}\cdot\frac{27}{2} =\frac{x^2}{9}. \end{aligned} $$

Outside $[0,3]$, there are no possible pairs with that $x$ value. Thus the complete marginal pdf is

$$ \boxed{ f_X(x)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{x^2}{9},&0\le x\le3,\\[4pt] 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}} $$

Check the result: a marginal density must itself integrate to $1$:

$$ \int_0^3\frac{x^2}{9}\,dx =\left[\frac{x^3}{27}\right]_0^3=1. $$

Remember the direction: to keep $X$, sum or integrate over $Y$. No assumption that $X$ and $Y$ are independent is needed for this marginalization rule.

Source: original paper, page 1.

02

A continuous density on an unbounded interval

Let the probability density function of a continuous random variable $X$ be given by

$$ f(x)=\frac{c}{x^3},\qquad x\ge1. $$

The density is zero for $x<1$.

(a) · 6 points

Find $c$. (If you can't find $c$, assume $c=1$ and move on.)

(b) · 6 points

Find the cumulative distribution function $F(x)$.

(c) · 5 points

Find $P(2<X<3)$.

(d) · Bonus, +5 points

Does the moment generating function of $X$ exist? Explain why or why not.

Worked solution

(a) Find $c$

A density must assign a total probability of $1$. Since $X$ can only take values at least $1$, we require

$$ \int_1^\infty\frac{c}{x^3}\,dx=1. $$

The upper endpoint is infinite, so first integrate to a finite endpoint $b$, and then let $b$ grow:

$$ \begin{aligned} \int_1^\infty\frac{c}{x^3}\,dx &=\lim_{b\to\infty}c\int_1^b x^{-3}\,dx\\ &=\lim_{b\to\infty}c\left[-\frac{1}{2x^2}\right]_1^b\\ &=\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac c2\left(1-\frac1{b^2}\right) =\frac c2. \end{aligned} $$

Therefore $c/2=1$, giving

$$ \boxed{c=2}. $$

This also makes the density nonnegative. The fallback $c=1$ in the question is an exam instruction for continuing if stuck; the normalized model uses $c=2$ in the remaining parts.

(b) Build the entire CDF

The CDF $F(x)=P(X\le x)$ adds up all probability up to the cutoff $x$.

If $x<1$, the cutoff is below every possible value of $X$, so $F(x)=0$.

If $x\ge1$, accumulate the density from $1$ to $x$. We use $u$ as the variable inside the integral so that $x$ remains the stopping point:

$$ \begin{aligned} F(x) &=\int_1^x\frac{2}{u^3}\,du\\ &=\left[-\frac1{u^2}\right]_1^x\\ &=-\frac1{x^2}-(-1) =1-\frac1{x^2}. \end{aligned} $$

Thus

$$ \boxed{ F(x)= \begin{cases} 0,&x<1,\\[3pt] 1-\dfrac1{x^2},&x\ge1. \end{cases}} $$

At the lower endpoint, $F(1)=0$: a continuous density gives zero probability to the single point $X=1$. As $x$ tends to infinity, $F(x)$ tends to $1$. Also, differentiating $1-x^{-2}$ gives $2x^{-3}$, recovering the density on $x>1$.

(c) Find the probability between $2$ and $3$

$F(3)$ includes all probability up to $3$. Subtracting $F(2)$ removes the probability up to $2$, leaving the interval between them. Because $X$ is continuous, including or excluding either individual endpoint does not change its probability.

$$ \begin{aligned} P(2<X<3) &=F(3)-F(2)\\ &=\left(1-\frac19\right)-\left(1-\frac14\right)\\ &=\frac14-\frac19\\ &=\boxed{\frac5{36}}\approx0.1389. \end{aligned} $$

The same answer comes directly from $\int_2^3 2x^{-3}\,dx$.

(d) Does the MGF exist?

A moment generating function is

$$ M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}]=\int_1^\infty e^{tx}\frac{2}{x^3}\,dx. $$

For an MGF to exist in the usual sense, this expectation must be finite for every $t$ in some open interval containing $0$. Being finite at $t=0$ alone is not enough: every probability distribution has $E[e^{0X}]=1$.

Here it diverges for every positive $t$. Intuitively, the exponential weight $e^{tx}$ grows so quickly that the factor $x^{-3}$ cannot keep the weighted area finite.

We can make this precise without evaluating a difficult integral. For $t>0$ and $x\ge1$, the expansion

$$ e^{tx}=1+tx+\frac{(tx)^2}{2!}+\cdots $$

has nonnegative terms. Therefore it is at least its quadratic term, $(tx)^2/2$. Consequently,

$$ \frac{2e^{tx}}{x^3} \ge\frac{2}{x^3}\cdot\frac{t^2x^2}{2} =\frac{t^2}{x}. $$

But

$$ \int_1^b\frac{t^2}{x}\,dx=t^2\log b\longrightarrow\infty. $$

The MGF integral is at least this divergent integral. Hence $M_X(t)=\infty$ for every $t>0$, so there is no finite open interval around zero:

$$ \boxed{\text{The MGF does not exist in a neighborhood of }0.} $$

For comparison, it is finite for $t\le0$, because $0<e^{tX}\le1$. That one-sided finiteness does not meet the MGF requirement.

The source solution's shorter observation, “not all moments exist,” can also be checked explicitly:

$$ E[X^2]=\int_1^\infty x^2\frac{2}{x^3}\,dx =2\int_1^\infty\frac1x\,dx=\infty. $$

An MGF finite in a neighborhood of zero would imply finite moments of all orders; the infinite second moment rules that out here.

Source: original paper, page 2.

03

Events in four coin tosses

A fair coin is tossed four times, and the resulting sequence of heads and tails is recorded. Define the following events:

  • $A=\{\text{exactly two heads appear}\}$;
  • $B=\{\text{heads and tails alternate}\}$;
  • $C=\{\text{the last toss is tails}\}$.

Assume tosses are independent and each toss is equally likely to be tails or heads. Find the following probabilities:

(a) · 5 points

$P(B\cap\overline C)$.

(b) · 5 points

$P(A\mid C)$.

(c) · 5 points

$P(\overline B\cup\overline C)$.

Worked solution

There are two possible results on each of four tosses, so there are $2^4=16$ ordered sequences. Independence and fairness make each particular sequence have probability

$$ \left(\frac12\right)^4=\frac1{16}. $$

This justifies using “number of favorable sequences divided by $16$” for an unconditional event. A conditional probability will use a restricted collection of sequences instead.

(a) Alternate, and do not finish with tails

Translate the event before counting. $B\cap\overline C$ requires both alternating heads and tails and a final head, since $\overline C$ means the last toss is not tails.

There are exactly two alternating sequences:

$$ B=\{HTHT,THTH\}. $$

The first ends with tails; the second ends with heads. Therefore

$$ B\cap\overline C=\{THTH\}, \qquad \boxed{P(B\cap\overline C)=\frac1{16}}. $$

The complement bar applies to $C$ only. It does not complement the entire intersection.

(b) Exactly two heads, given a final tail

The phrase “given $C$” tells us the last toss is already known to be tails. We restrict attention to sequences ending in $T$; the first three positions remain free, so there are $2^3=8$ equally likely sequences in this restricted collection.

For the full sequence to contain exactly two heads, two of those first three positions must be heads. The possibilities are

$$ A\cap C=\{HHTT,HTHT,THHT\}. $$

There are three favorable sequences out of the eight allowed by the condition:

$$ \boxed{P(A\mid C)=\frac38}. $$

To see the same reasoning in the conditional probability formula,

$$ P(A\mid C) =\frac{P(A\cap C)}{P(C)} =\frac{3/16}{8/16} =\frac38. $$

The denominator is $8/16$, not $1$: we are asking what fraction of the sequences that finish with tails have exactly two heads. Equivalently, choose the two head positions among the first three: $\binom32/2^3=3/8$.

(c) Do not alternate, or do not finish with tails

The event $\overline B\cup\overline C$ includes a sequence if at least one condition holds: it does not alternate, or it ends in heads. Here “or” includes sequences satisfying both.

It is easier to find what this event leaves out. A sequence is excluded only if neither condition holds: it does alternate and it does finish with tails. Thus

$$ \overline{\overline B\cup\overline C}=B\cap C, $$

or equivalently, by De Morgan's law,

$$ \overline B\cup\overline C=\overline{B\cap C}. $$

Among the two alternating sequences, only $HTHT$ ends with tails:

$$ B\cap C=\{HTHT\},\qquad P(B\cap C)=\frac1{16}. $$

Remove this one excluded sequence from the full sample space:

$$ \boxed{P(\overline B\cup\overline C) =1-P(B\cap C) =1-\frac1{16} =\frac{15}{16}}. $$

Notice the distinction from part (a): part (a) selected $THTH$, whereas part (c) excludes $HTHT$. Reading each complement bar carefully keeps these two events separate.

04

Complete and use a joint probability table

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two discrete random variables whose joint and marginal distributions are partially given in the following table:

$X\backslash Y$ $1$ $2$ $3$ $p_X(x)$
$1$ $1/6$ $0$ — $1/3$
$2$ — $1/4$ — $1/3$
$3$ — — $1/4$ —
$p_Y(y)$ $1/6$ $1/3$ — —

(a) · 6 points

Complete the table.

(b) · 5 points

Find $P(X=Y)$.

(c) · 5 points

Find $E(Y)$.

(d) · 5 points

Determine the conditional pmf $p(x\mid y=2)$.

Worked solution

Each interior cell contains a joint probability $p(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y)$. A row total is $p_X(x)=P(X=x)$, obtained by adding across all $Y$ values. A column total is $p_Y(y)=P(Y=y)$, obtained by adding across all $X$ values.

(a) Fill the table using its totals

Start with row $X=1$. Its entries must add to $1/3$:

$$ \frac16+0+p(1,3)=\frac13 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(1,3)=\frac16. $$

Now use column $Y=1$. Its total is already exhausted by the $1/6$ in the first row:

$$ \frac16+p(2,1)+p(3,1)=\frac16. $$

Therefore $p(2,1)+p(3,1)=0$. Each is a probability and must be nonnegative, so neither can be positive:

$$ p(2,1)=p(3,1)=0. $$

Use row $X=2$ to find its last missing entry:

$$ 0+\frac14+p(2,3)=\frac13 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(2,3)=\frac13-\frac14=\frac1{12}. $$

Use column $Y=2$ for the remaining interior entry:

$$ 0+\frac14+p(3,2)=\frac13 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(3,2)=\frac1{12}. $$

All interior cells are now known. Add the last row and last column to find their marginal probabilities:

$$ p_X(3)=0+\frac1{12}+\frac14=\frac13, $$

$$ p_Y(3)=\frac16+\frac1{12}+\frac14 =\frac2{12}+\frac1{12}+\frac3{12} =\frac12. $$

The completed table is

$X\backslash Y$ $1$ $2$ $3$ $p_X(x)$
$1$ $1/6$ $0$ $1/6$ $1/3$
$2$ $0$ $1/4$ $1/12$ $1/3$
$3$ $0$ $1/12$ $1/4$ $1/3$
$p_Y(y)$ $1/6$ $1/3$ $1/2$ $1$

The bottom-right $1$ records the total probability. Both the row totals and the column totals sum to $1$, and every cell is nonnegative.

(b) Find $P(X=Y)$

$X=Y$ happens at the three diagonal cells: $(1,1)$, $(2,2)$, and $(3,3)$. These outcomes cannot happen simultaneously, so add their probabilities:

$$ \begin{aligned} P(X=Y) &=p(1,1)+p(2,2)+p(3,3)\\ &=\frac16+\frac14+\frac14\\ &=\boxed{\frac23}. \end{aligned} $$

Use the joint cells directly. Multiplying row and column totals would require independence, which has not been given.

(c) Find the expected value of $Y$

Expectation is a probability-weighted average of the possible values of $Y$. Since we only care about $Y$, use its marginal distribution along the bottom of the table:

$$ P(Y=1)=\frac16,\qquad P(Y=2)=\frac13,\qquad P(Y=3)=\frac12. $$

Multiply each value by its probability and add:

$$ \begin{aligned} E[Y] &=1\cdot\frac16+2\cdot\frac13+3\cdot\frac12\\ &=\frac16+\frac46+\frac96\\ &=\boxed{\frac73}\approx2.333. \end{aligned} $$

This is above $2$ because higher $Y$ values receive more probability: $Y=3$ is three times as likely as $Y=1$.

(d) Find the distribution of $X$ given $Y=2$

Once we are told $Y=2$, only the middle column remains possible. That column originally contains total probability $1/3$. Divide each entry by $1/3$ so that the retained column becomes a full probability distribution:

$$ P(X=x\mid Y=2)=\frac{P(X=x,Y=2)}{P(Y=2)} =\frac{p(x,2)}{1/3}. $$

For the three possible $x$ values,

$$ P(X=1\mid Y=2)=\frac0{1/3}=0, $$

$$ P(X=2\mid Y=2)=\frac{1/4}{1/3}=\frac34, $$

$$ P(X=3\mid Y=2)=\frac{1/12}{1/3}=\frac14. $$

Thus

$$ \boxed{ p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid2)= \begin{cases} 3/4,&x=2,\\ 1/4,&x=3,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}} $$

The conditional probabilities sum to $1$. The denominator is the column total $P(Y=2)$ because the condition restricts $Y$; it is not the row total associated with whichever $x$ we are considering.

Source: original paper, page 4.

05

A discrete distribution and its summaries

A discrete random variable $X$ has the following probability distribution:

$x$ $1$ $2$ $3$ $4$
$p(x)$ $0.5$ $0.2$ $0.1$ $0.2$

(a) · 6 points

Find the cumulative distribution function $F(x)$.

(b) · 6 points

Find $\operatorname{Var}(X)$.

(c) · 5 points

Find the mgf $m_X(t)$.

Worked solution

(a) Accumulate probability to obtain the CDF

The CDF is $F(x)=P(X\le x)$. Think of moving a cutoff $x$ from left to right and including the probability at each possible value as soon as the cutoff reaches it.

  • For $x<1$: no possible value has been included, so $F(x)=0$.
  • For $1\le x<2$: only $X=1$ is included, so $F(x)=0.5$.
  • For $2\le x<3$: include $X=1$ and $X=2$, giving $F(x)=0.5+0.2=0.7$.
  • For $3\le x<4$: also include $X=3$, giving $F(x)=0.5+0.2+0.1=0.8$.
  • For $x\ge4$: include all four possible values, so $F(x)=1$.

Therefore

$$ \boxed{ F(x)= \begin{cases} 0,&x<1,\\ 0.5,&1\le x<2,\\ 0.7,&2\le x<3,\\ 0.8,&3\le x<4,\\ 1,&x\ge4. \end{cases}} $$

Pay attention to the endpoints. Because the definition uses $X\le x$, the new probability is included at each possible value. For example, $F(2)=0.7$, while the value just before $2$ is $F(2^-)=0.5$. The jump $0.7-0.5=0.2$ is exactly $P(X=2)$. Between possible values, the CDF stays constant because there is no additional probability to collect.

(b) Calculate the variance

Variance measures the probability-weighted squared distance from the mean:

$$ \operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2],\qquad \mu=E[X]. $$

An equivalent formula that is convenient for a probability table is

$$ \operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2. $$

First find the mean. Multiply each possible value by its probability:

$$ \begin{aligned} E[X] &=1(0.5)+2(0.2)+3(0.1)+4(0.2)\\ &=0.5+0.4+0.3+0.8\\ &=2. \end{aligned} $$

Then find the mean of the squared values. Square the values of $X$, while keeping the same probabilities:

$$ \begin{aligned} E[X^2] &=1^2(0.5)+2^2(0.2)+3^2(0.1)+4^2(0.2)\\ &=0.5+0.8+0.9+3.2\\ &=5.4. \end{aligned} $$

Subtract the square of the mean:

$$ \boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X)=5.4-2^2=1.4}. $$

$E[X^2]$ and $(E[X])^2$ are different operations. The first squares each outcome before averaging; the second averages first and then squares the resulting mean.

As a direct check, use squared distances from $\mu=2$:

$$ \begin{aligned} E[(X-2)^2] &=(1-2)^2(0.5)+(2-2)^2(0.2)\\ &\quad +(3-2)^2(0.1)+(4-2)^2(0.2)\\ &=0.5+0+0.1+0.8=1.4. \end{aligned} $$

(c) Form the moment generating function

The MGF is the expectation of $e^{tX}$. Here $t$ is a real input to the function; for a chosen $t$, transform each possible value $x$ into $e^{tx}$ and take its weighted average:

$$ m_X(t)=E[e^{tX}]=\sum_x e^{tx}P(X=x). $$

For this table,

$$ \boxed{m_X(t)=0.5e^t+0.2e^{2t}+0.1e^{3t}+0.2e^{4t}}. $$

This finite sum is finite for every real $t$, so the MGF exists everywhere on the real line. At $t=0$, each exponential becomes $1$, giving $m_X(0)=0.5+0.2+0.1+0.2=1$, as required.

Its connection to part (b) explains the name “moment generating.” Differentiating brings down the factors $x$, and setting $t=0$ removes the exponentials:

$$ m_X'(0)=0.5+0.4+0.3+0.8=2=E[X], $$

$$ m_X''(0)=0.5+0.8+0.9+3.2=5.4=E[X^2]. $$

The derivatives recover the same first and second moments used to calculate the variance.

06

A noisy binary communication channel

A message is transmitted as a binary code of $0$s and $1$s. Experience with the equipment being used suggests that if a $0$ is sent, it will be received as $0$ $94\%$ of the time. If a $1$ is sent, it will be received as a $1$ $95\%$ of the time (and as a $0$ $5\%$ of the time). The text being sent is known to be $7\%$ $1$s and $93\%$ $0$s.

(a) · 6 points

What is the probability that the received message is $1$?

(b) · 6 points

Suppose the next signal sent is received as a $1$. What is the probability it was sent as a $0$?

(c) · 6 points

If the same message is sent twice, and assuming the received messages are conditionally independent, what is the probability that it is received as two $1$s?

Worked solution

Let $S$ denote the bit sent and $R$ the bit received. The question gives two kinds of information: how often each bit is sent, and what the channel does given that bit.

$$ P(S=0)=0.93,\qquad P(S=1)=0.07. $$

If a $0$ is sent, the receiver must report either $0$ or $1$. Therefore

$$ P(R=1\mid S=0)=1-0.94=0.06. $$

If a $1$ is sent, the chance of receiving a $1$ is already given:

$$ P(R=1\mid S=1)=0.95. $$

(a) Find the chance of receiving a $1$

A received $1$ has two possible sources:

  1. A $0$ was sent, and the channel changed it to a $1$.
  2. A $1$ was sent, and the channel preserved it.

These cases are disjoint and cover every way to receive a $1$. Within each case, use the multiplication rule to combine the chance of sending the bit with the conditional chance of receiving a $1$:

$$ P(S=0,R=1)=P(S=0)P(R=1\mid S=0) =0.93(0.06)=0.0558, $$

$$ P(S=1,R=1)=P(S=1)P(R=1\mid S=1) =0.07(0.95)=0.0665. $$

Now add the two routes:

$$ \boxed{P(R=1)=0.0558+0.0665=0.1223}. $$

So $12.23\%$ of received bits are $1$s. Notice that $0.95$ alone is not the answer: it applies only after we know a $1$ was sent, whereas this part asks about an arbitrary transmitted bit.

(b) Given a received $1$, how likely was the original bit $0$?

Now we restrict attention to received $1$s and ask what fraction came from sent $0$s. By conditional probability,

$$ P(S=0\mid R=1) =\frac{P(S=0,R=1)}{P(R=1)}. $$

Both quantities were calculated in part (a), so

$$ \boxed{ P(S=0\mid R=1) =\frac{0.0558}{0.1223} \approx0.4563 }. $$

The probability is about $45.63\%$.

A frequency picture makes the denominator concrete. Out of $10{,}000$ transmitted bits, the model predicts, on average:

Original bit Number sent Number received as $1$
$0$ $9{,}300$ $9{,}300(0.06)=558$
$1$ $700$ $700(0.95)=665$
Total $10{,}000$ $1{,}223$

Among the $1{,}223$ received $1$s, $558$ originated as $0$s: $558/1{,}223\approx0.4563$. These are model-based average counts, not a claim that every batch has exactly these numbers.

This is why a $6\%$ error rate for sent $0$s can produce a much larger fraction of errors among received $1$s: there are far more sent $0$s to begin with. $P(R=1\mid S=0)$ and $P(S=0\mid R=1)$ use different denominators.

(c) Send the same bit twice

Use $R^{(1)}$ and $R^{(2)}$ for the first and second received results. Both transmissions share one original bit $S$. The given conditional independence means that once the original bit is fixed, the two channel outcomes can be multiplied:

$$ P(R^{(1)}=1,R^{(2)}=1\mid S=s) =P(R^{(1)}=1\mid S=s)P(R^{(2)}=1\mid S=s). $$

Condition on the original bit before doing that multiplication.

If the shared original bit is $1$, each reception is a $1$ with probability $0.95$. Thus

$$ P(R^{(1)}=1,R^{(2)}=1\mid S=1)=0.95^2=0.9025. $$

If the shared original bit is $0$, each reception is a $1$ with probability $0.06$. Both transmissions must be changed by the channel:

$$ P(R^{(1)}=1,R^{(2)}=1\mid S=0)=0.06^2=0.0036. $$

Finally, weight these conditional probabilities by how likely their original bits are and add:

$$ \begin{aligned} P(R^{(1)}=1,R^{(2)}=1) &=P(S=1)(0.95)^2+P(S=0)(0.06)^2\\ &=0.07(0.9025)+0.93(0.0036)\\ &=0.063175+0.003348\\ &=\boxed{0.066523}. \end{aligned} $$

The probability is $6.6523\%$.

Why not square $0.1223$ from part (a)? That would multiply two unconditional receiving probabilities as though the receptions were independent without knowing $S$. They are not: both depend on the same original bit. A first received $1$ gives information about that shared bit and therefore changes our assessment of the second reception. Independence was supplied only conditional on $S$, so the correct order is: fix $S$, multiply the two reception probabilities, then average over $S$.
