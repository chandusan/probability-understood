Counting equally likely outcomes · Slide 41

Three cars: count sequences, then check their weights

Watch three cars approach an intersection in order. Each turns left or right. To compute a probability, we need both a list of possible sequences and a model for how likely those sequences are. The first is a set question; the second is a probability assumption.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment

Slide numbers follow the PDF page order; the printed footer numbers differ on some slides.

Let’s understand it

List the eight ordered outcomes

The first, second, and third letters describe the respective cars:

$$ S=\{LLL,LLR,LRL,LRR,RLL,RLR,RRL,RRR\}. $$

The event $A$ is “exactly one car turns right,” and C is “all cars turn in the same direction.” Thus

$$ A=\{LLR,LRL,RLL\}, \qquad C=\{LLL,RRR\}. $$

“Exactly one” is different from “at least one.” Likewise, “all the same” includes both all-left and all-right. Listing events before doing arithmetic helps catch those translation errors.

The equally likely model

Assume each car independently turns right with probability $1/2$ and left with probability $1/2$. Then every length-three sequence has probability

$$ \left(\frac12\right)^3=\frac18. $$

These assumptions justify treating the eight outcomes as equally likely. We can now count:

$$ P(A)=\frac38=0.375, \qquad P(C)=\frac28=\frac14=0.25. $$

There are three places to put the single R in A. There are two common directions for C. The events are disjoint: an exactly-one-right sequence cannot have all three cars turning alike.

If right turns are more or less common

Keep independence but let each car have the same right-turn probability p, with $0\le p\le1$. A specified sequence with r right turns has probability

$$ p^r(1-p)^{3-r}. $$

The sequences still form the same sample space, but generally they no longer have equal weights. Each exactly-one-right sequence has weight $p(1-p)^2$, so

$$ P(A)=3p(1-p)^2. $$

The two all-same sequences have weights $(1-p)^3$ and $p^3$, giving

$$ P(C)=(1-p)^3+p^3. $$

At $p=1/2$, these recover $3/8$ and $1/4$. At $p=0$, all cars turn left; at $p=1$, all turn right. At either endpoint, $P(A)=0$ and $P(C)=1$, just as the story predicts.

Why independence matters too

Equal left/right probabilities for each individual car do not by themselves make the eight sequences equally likely. Suppose one fair coin controls all three cars: heads makes all turn right, tails makes all turn left. Each car individually has a 50% right-turn probability, but only RRR and LLL occur. Now $P(A)=0$ and $P(C)=1$.

So the fair independent model is an assumption about the joint experiment, not something guaranteed by the phrase “can turn left or right.” If cars influence one another, or their right-turn probabilities differ, we need a different probability model.

Check your understanding

Under the independent common-p model, take $p=0.2$. Calculate both requested probabilities. Does “exactly one right” still have probability $3/8$?

Add the weights of the correct sequences

Each of LLR, LRL, and RLL has probability $0.2(0.8)^2=0.128$. Hence

$$ P(A)=3(0.128)=0.384. $$

The all-same event has probability

$$ P(C)=(0.8)^3+(0.2)^3=0.512+0.008=0.520. $$

The event sizes remain three and two, but dividing by eight is no longer valid. At this p, LLL alone carries probability 0.512, whereas RRR carries only 0.008.
