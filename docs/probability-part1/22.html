The language of events · Slide 22

Union means at least one membership succeeds

A union collects every outcome accepted by at least one of its events. Belonging to two events does not disqualify an outcome, and it does not give that outcome two copies in the resulting set.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment

Slide numbers follow the PDF page order; the printed footer numbers differ on some slides.

Let’s understand it

Start with two events

The definition is

$$ A\cup B=\{e\in S\mid e\in A\text{ or }e\in B\}. $$

For each possible outcome $e$, ask two questions: does it belong to $A$? Does it belong to $B$? Keep it if either answer is yes, including when both are yes. That is why both circles, including their overlap, are shaded in the union picture.

Unpack the indexed union one symbol at a time

The larger expression that prompted your question is

$$ \bigcup_{i\in I}A_i =\{e\in S\mid\exists i\in I\text{ such that }e\in A_i\}. $$

Read it as: “Collect every outcome that belongs to at least one event in this indexed collection.”

Symbol Meaning
$S$ Sample space: all possible outcomes
$e$ One outcome being checked
$A_i$ The event carrying label $i$
$I$ The set of labels used for the events
$\in$ “Belongs to”
$\mid$ “Such that” in this set-builder notation
$\exists$ “There exists at least one”
$\bigcup$ Take the union over the indicated events

Here $I$ is an index set, not the sample space. Its elements tell us which event labels to visit. Also, this vertical bar means “such that”; it is not the conditional-probability bar in an expression like $P(A\mid B)$.

Our three-event die example

Let

$$ S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\},\qquad I=\{1,2,3\}, $$

$$ A_1=\{1,2\},\qquad A_2=\{2,4\},\qquad A_3=\{6\}. $$

Then

$$ \bigcup_{i\in I}A_i=A_1\cup A_2\cup A_3=\{1,2,4,6\}. $$

  • For $e=4$, the needed label exists: $i=2$ works. Include 4.
  • For $e=2$, both $i=1$ and $i=2$ work. Include 2 once.
  • For $e=3$, no label works. Exclude 3.

“Exists” asks for one successful witness. It does not require every event to contain the outcome.

Try the membership test

Start with the indexed-union example. Predict what will happen if you remove 2 from $A_1$ while leaving it in $A_2$. Then change memberships and watch the result set. Switch operations to compare “at least one” with “every selected event.”

Explore the ideaOpen larger ↗

How large can the label set be?

$I$ can be finite, countably infinite, or uncountably infinite. For example, events $A_t=\{t\}$ indexed by real $t\in[0,1]$ have union $[0,1]$. The large union symbol is a description of a set, not an instruction to add individual probabilities without checking a rule.

In general probability theory, measurable events are guaranteed to remain measurable under countable unions. An arbitrary uncountable union is a valid set operation but need not remain a measurable event in the chosen model. Our finite examples avoid this issue.

Check your understanding

In the example, remove $A_2$ entirely. What is the new union? Explain the fate of 2 and 4 by naming a remaining event that contains each, if one exists.

Reveal the reasoning

$A_1\cup A_3=\{1,2,6\}$. The outcome 2 survives because $A_1$ still contains it. The outcome 4 disappears because its only membership was in $A_2$. A union needs at least one remaining witness for each outcome.
