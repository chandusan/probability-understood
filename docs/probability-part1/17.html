Building a sample space · Slide 17

Fold the needle, then measure the probability

We can describe a needle drop with just two quantities: how close its midpoint is to a line, and how much it tilts away from that line. The useful simplification is to keep every distinction that can change the crossing result, while folding together placements that behave identically.

~9 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/2 interactive experiments

Slide numbers follow the PDF page order; the printed footer numbers differ on some slides.

Let’s understand it

Why use angles from 0 to 90 degrees?

Imagine the ruled lines are horizontal. A needle at $30^\circ$ leans upward to the right. A needle at $150^\circ$ leans upward to the left. Their smaller angles with a horizontal line are both $30^\circ$, and their vertical reaches are identical.

At the same midpoint, either both cross a ruled line or neither does. We lose information about which way the needle leans, but keep all the information needed for this question.

Using an angle from $0$ to $180^\circ$ would therefore be valid. The slide chooses a shorter description: the acute angle, including its boundary values, between the needle and the lines:

$$0\le\theta\le90^\circ=\frac\pi2.$$

This folding is separate from treating the needle as an unoriented segment. First, angles differing by $180^\circ$ describe the same unlabeled segment. Then reflection pairs such as $30^\circ$ and $150^\circ$ are grouped because they have the same crossing behavior.

For a full orientation $\alpha\in[0,\pi)$, the folded angle is

$$\theta=\min(\alpha,\pi-\alpha).$$

Why does folding preserve a uniform distribution?

Suppose $\alpha$ is uniform on $[0,\pi)$. Take any interval $[a,b]\subseteq[0,\pi/2]$. It has two preimages under the fold: $[a,b]$ and $[\pi-b,\pi-a]$, ignoring shared boundary points of probability zero. Their total length is $2(b-a)$.

Thus

$$P(a\le\theta\le b)=\frac{2(b-a)}\pi=\frac{b-a}{\pi/2}.$$

Equal widths in the folded range still have equal probabilities. Folding would not automatically produce this result from an arbitrary biased orientation distribution; it works here because the original orientation is uniform.

Why does distance range only up to half the spacing?

Let $U$ be the midpoint's vertical position inside a strip between lines at $0$ and $d$. Its distances to those two lines are $U$ and $d-U$. The nearest-line distance is

$$x=\min(U,d-U).$$

The farthest a midpoint can be from its nearest line is halfway between them, at distance $d/2$. Thus $0\le x\le d/2$.

If $U$ is uniform on $[0,d]$, folding the two halves of the strip gives

$$x\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,d/2).$$

For example, positions $0.2d$ and $0.8d$ both have nearest-line distance $0.2d$. An interval of distances of width $h$ corresponds to two position intervals of total width $2h$, giving probability $2h/d$.

The symbol $x$ now denotes perpendicular distance. It was a horizontal coordinate on the previous page; this is a deliberate change of parametrization.

What does Uniform mean? Is it a function?

The notation

$$\theta\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,\pi/2)$$

means “$\theta$ is distributed uniformly between $0$ and $\pi/2$.” The symbol $\sim$ means “is distributed as.” Uniform is the name of a probability distribution, characterized by equal probabilities for equal-length intervals.

For instance,

$$P(10^\circ\le\theta\le20^\circ) =P(60^\circ\le\theta\le70^\circ)=\frac{10}{90}=\frac19.$$

The distribution can be described by a function, its probability density function, or PDF. In radians,

$$f_\theta(t)= \begin{cases} 2/\pi,&0\le t\le\pi/2,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

The graph is a flat horizontal segment above the allowed interval and zero outside. Its total area is $(\pi/2)(2/\pi)=1$.

Why is the height not the probability of one angle?

Density is probability per unit of angle. To obtain probability, we need width as well as height. In degrees, the density is $1/90$ per degree, so

$$P(a\le\theta\le b)=\underbrace{\frac1{90}}_{\text{density per degree}}\underbrace{(b-a)}_{\text{degrees}}.$$

Watch an interval around $30^\circ$ shrink:

Interval Width Probability
$25^\circ$ to $35^\circ$ $10^\circ$ $10/90$
$29.5^\circ$ to $30.5^\circ$ $1^\circ$ $1/90$
$29.995^\circ$ to $30.005^\circ$ $0.01^\circ$ $0.01/90$

The height does not change. The area shrinks because the width shrinks. One exact angle has zero width, so

$$P(\theta=30^\circ)=0.$$

An outcome can occur even though its individual probability is zero in a continuous distribution. No point owns a positive chunk of probability; intervals carry probability through area. This does not contradict countable additivity: an interval contains uncountably many points, and the rule does not say its probability is an uncountable sum of zero point masses.

A device displaying “$30^\circ$” usually reports an interval after rounding, perhaps $[29.5^\circ,30.5^\circ)$. That recorded reading does have positive probability.

Change units without changing probability

The same distribution has density $1/90$ per degree or $2/\pi$ per radian. Their numerical heights differ because a degree and a radian have different widths. The total area stays one:

$$90\left(\frac1{90}\right)=\frac\pi2\left(\frac2\pi\right)=1.$$

A $10^\circ$ interval has width $\pi/18$ radians. Either calculation gives $1/9$:

$$\frac{10}{90}=\frac2\pi\frac\pi{18}.$$

Try narrowing the highlighted interval, then switching angle units. Predict which quantities change: axis numbers and density height do; the shaded probability does not. A density can exceed one without violating probability rules, because a density is not itself an interval probability.

When does the needle reach a line?

The midpoint-to-endpoint distance is $l/2$. At angle $\theta$ from the horizontal lines, the perpendicular reach is the opposite side of a right triangle:

$$\text{perpendicular reach}=\frac l2\sin\theta.$$

The needle reaches the nearest line when that reach is at least the center's distance:

$$\boxed{x\le\frac l2\sin\theta.}$$

Check the limiting cases. At $\theta=0$, the needle is parallel and has zero perpendicular reach. At $\theta=\pi/2$, the full half-length points toward a line. The inequality also confirms that increasing the angle or bringing the midpoint closer makes crossing easier.

Move the orientation through $30^\circ$ and $150^\circ$ to see the same folded angle. Then change the midpoint distance. The boundary in the lower plot is the crossing inequality: points below the curve cross, and points above it miss. The experiment uses the dimensionless ratios $l/d$ and $x/d$ so the geometry works in any consistent length unit.

Derive Buffon's probability, one step at a time

Assume $0<l\le d$, independent uniform position and orientation, and an ideal needle of zero thickness. At a fixed angle $\theta$, the permissible distance interval is

$$0\le x\le\frac l2\sin\theta.$$

The entire distance interval has length $d/2$. Because $x$ is uniform,

$$P(\text{cross}\mid\theta)=\frac{(l/2)\sin\theta}{d/2}=\frac ld\sin\theta.$$

Now average over the uniform angles. The density in radians is $2/\pi$:

$$\begin{aligned} P(\text{cross}) &=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac ld\sin\theta\,\frac2\pi\,d\theta\\ &=\frac{2l}{\pi d}\left[-\cos\theta\right]_0^{\pi/2}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{2l}{\pi d}}. \end{aligned}$$

The formula has sensible behavior: doubling a short needle doubles the crossing probability; increasing the spacing makes crossings less frequent. When $l=d$, the probability is $2/\pi\approx0.6366$, not one, because many nearly parallel placements still miss.

See the same calculation as area in an outcome rectangle

The reduced sample space is the rectangle

$$0\le\theta\le\pi/2,\qquad0\le x\le d/2.$$

Its area is $\pi d/4$. Independence and the two uniform distributions make probability proportional to area inside this rectangle. The crossing region lies below $x=(l/2)\sin\theta$, whose area is

$$\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac l2\sin\theta\,d\theta=\frac l2.$$

Therefore the area ratio is

$$\frac{l/2}{\pi d/4}=\frac{2l}{\pi d}.$$

This is area in the distance-angle outcome space, not area of the needle on the physical floor. For $l>d$, the curve extends beyond the allowed rectangle for some angles; we must use $\min\{1,(l/d)\sin\theta\}$ as the conditional probability. Extending the short-needle formula without that correction can produce an impossible answer greater than one.

The rectangular-grid variant

The slide also introduces a Buffon–Laplace grid with vertical lines spaced $a$ apart and horizontal lines spaced $b$ apart. Let $x\in[0,a/2]$ and $y\in[0,b/2]$ be midpoint distances to the nearest vertical and horizontal lines. Let $\theta\in[0,\pi/2]$ be the folded angle measured from the horizontal direction.

For $l\le\min(a,b)$, crossing a vertical line is the event

$$V:\quad x\le\frac l2\cos\theta,$$

while crossing a horizontal line is

$$H:\quad y\le\frac l2\sin\theta.$$

Cosine gives horizontal reach toward a vertical line; sine gives vertical reach toward a horizontal line. Crossing the grid means $V\cup H$: at least one kind of line is crossed. Counting $P(V)+P(H)$ alone would count needles crossing both types twice.

Derive the grid formula under uniform independent placement

Assume the center is uniform within a rectangular cell and independent of uniform orientation. Folding each coordinate makes $x,y$ independent uniforms on their stated intervals. Conditional on $\theta$,

$$P(V\mid\theta)=\frac la\cos\theta,\qquad P(H\mid\theta)=\frac lb\sin\theta.$$

The two distance tests are conditionally independent because they involve independent center coordinates, so

$$P(V\cap H\mid\theta)=\frac{l^2}{ab}\sin\theta\cos\theta.$$

Averaging using density $2/\pi$ gives

$$P(V)=\frac{2l}{\pi a},\qquad P(H)=\frac{2l}{\pi b},\qquad P(V\cap H)=\frac{l^2}{\pi ab}.$$

Therefore

$$\boxed{P(\text{at least one grid line}) =\frac{2l(a+b)-l^2}{\pi ab}.}$$

The source gives the stricter condition $l<\min(a,b)$; the displayed formula also extends to equality, with the same continuous-model boundary conventions. For a square grid with $a=b=l$, it gives $3/\pi\approx0.9549$.

Conditional independence here does not mean $V,H$ are unconditionally independent: they share the random angle. That distinction will become important when studying conditional probability.

Other problems that use the same idea

The source asks for similar problems. The common method is to represent random choices as coordinates, draw the favorable region, and measure it using the specified distribution.

A dart in a square. Choose a point uniformly in a square of side $2r$ containing an inscribed circle of radius $r$. The circle event has probability

$$\frac{\pi r^2}{(2r)^2}=\frac\pi4.$$

Two people meeting. Each arrives independently and uniformly during a $60$-minute interval and waits $10$ minutes. Their arrival pair $(u,v)$ is uniform in a square of area $60^2$. They meet when $\lvert u-v\rvert\le10$. The excluded region is two triangles, each with legs $50$, giving

$$P(\text{meet})=1-\frac{2(50^2/2)}{60^2}=\frac{11}{36}.$$

Break a stick twice. Choose two independent uniform cut positions on a unit stick. After ordering them as $u<v$, the lengths are $u,v-u,1-v$. They form a triangle exactly when each is less than $1/2$. The ordered-cut region has area $1/2$. The favorable triangle has vertices $(0,1/2),(1/2,1/2),(1/2,1)$, with boundaries excluded where a piece has length exactly $1/2$. Its area is $(1/2)(1/2)(1/2)=1/8$, so the probability is $(1/8)/(1/2)=1/4$. A different procedure for choosing cuts need not give the same distribution.

Check your understanding

Let $l=d/2$, $x=d/5$, and $\theta=30^\circ$. Does this placement cross? What is the crossing probability over all standard random drops? Finally, what is the probability that the folded angle is exactly $30^\circ$?

Work through the answer

For this placement, the perpendicular reach is

$$\frac l2\sin30^\circ=\frac d4\frac12=\frac d8.$$

Since $d/5>d/8$, the needle misses. Across the whole random experiment,

$$P(\text{cross})=\frac{2(d/2)}{\pi d}=\frac1\pi\approx0.3183.$$

For a continuous uniform angle, $P(\theta=30^\circ)=0$. That does not make $30^\circ$ an impossible geometric placement. It means a single exact angle has zero width and therefore zero probability mass. By contrast, the interval from $25^\circ$ to $35^\circ$ has probability $10/90=1/9$.
