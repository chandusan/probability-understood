Coefficients & repeated choices · Slide 29

Extend the binomial theorem beyond whole powers

The finite binomial theorem comes from multiplying a whole number of identical factors. Expressions such as $\sqrt{1+x}$ and $1/(1+x)$ do not have that interpretation. Yet the same falling-product pattern for the coefficients survives, producing an infinite series near $x=0$.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment
Let’s understand it

Keep the number of factors in the coefficient discrete

For a nonnegative integer exponent, we can write

$$ \binom nk=\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!}. $$

Unlike the factorial quotient, this expression still makes immediate sense when the upper argument is a real number $\alpha$. Define

$$ \boxed{\binom\alpha k=\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-k+1)}{k!},\qquad k=1,2,3,\ldots,} $$

and define $\binom\alpha0=1$. The product for $k=0$ is an empty product, whose value is 1.

The upper argument may be real, but $k$ remains a nonnegative integer: it tells us how many factors to multiply and which power of $x$ receives the coefficient. We are not choosing “half an object.” When $\alpha$ is fractional or negative, these are algebraic coefficients, not literal counts of subsets. They can be negative.

Read the infinite expansion one term at a time

The generalized binomial theorem says

$$ \boxed{(1+x)^\alpha=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom\alpha kx^k,\qquad |x|<1.} $$

Its opening terms are

$$ 1+\alpha x+\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{2!}x^2 +\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)}{3!}x^3+\cdots. $$

The absolute-value bars matter: $|x|<1$ means $-1<x<1$, not merely $x<1$. In this interval, $1+x>0$, so the real power on the left is well defined for every real $\alpha$.

The infinite sum is exact. Stopping after a finite number of terms usually gives an approximation, not an identity.

A square root you can calculate by hand

Let $\alpha=1/2$. The first coefficients are

$$ \binom{1/2}{1}=\frac12, \qquad \binom{1/2}{2}=\frac{(1/2)(-1/2)}2=-\frac18, $$

$$ \binom{1/2}{3}=\frac{(1/2)(-1/2)(-3/2)}6=\frac1{16}. $$

Therefore

$$ \sqrt{1+x}=1+\frac x2-\frac{x^2}{8}+\frac{x^3}{16}-\frac{5x^4}{128}+\cdots. $$

At $x=0.1$, keeping through the cubic term gives

$$ \sqrt{1.1}\approx1+0.05-0.00125+0.0000625=1.0488125. $$

The actual value is approximately $1.04880885$. Small powers of $x$ make the later corrections small here. Near $|x|=1$, you may need many more terms; simply taking three terms does not promise a fixed accuracy.

Why whole-number expansions stop

For $\alpha=3$, the coefficient of $x^4$ contains

$$ 3\times2\times1\times0. $$

Every later coefficient also contains that zero. The series therefore terminates:

$$ (1+x)^3=1+3x+3x^2+x^3. $$

For $\alpha=1/2$, the factors $1/2,-1/2,-3/2,\ldots$ never reach zero. Nor do they reach zero for a negative integer exponent. So these series generally continue indefinitely.

A nonnegative integer exponent gives a polynomial identity valid for every real $x$. The convergence restriction is needed for the general infinite-series result; the theorem does not limit an already finite polynomial to $|x|<1$.

Negative exponents connect to a familiar series

For $\alpha=-1$,

$$ \binom{-1}{k}=\frac{(-1)(-2)\cdots(-k)}{k!}=(-1)^k. $$

Thus

$$ \frac1{1+x}=1-x+x^2-x^3+\cdots,\qquad |x|<1. $$

This is the geometric series with ratio $-x$. The signs alternate for positive $x$, but not for negative $x$: putting $x=-1/2$ produces $1+1/2+1/4+\cdots=2$.

Experiment with a partial sum

Predict whether the terms stop for exponent 3, and whether they stop for exponent $1/2$ or $-1$. Then move $x$ toward the ends of the interval and compare the partial sum with the actual function value.

Why these coefficients are forced by the function

There is a useful derivation if you know derivatives. Let $f(x)=(1+x)^\alpha$. Its derivative satisfies

$$ (1+x)f'(x)=\alpha f(x),\qquad f(0)=1. $$

Suppose $f$ has a power series $\sum_{k\geq0}c_kx^k$. Matching the coefficient of $x^k$ in the derivative relation gives

$$ (k+1)c_{k+1}+kc_k=\alpha c_k, $$

so

$$ c_{k+1}=\frac{\alpha-k}{k+1}c_k,\qquad c_0=1. $$

Starting from 1, this recurrence successively generates

$$ c_1=\alpha,\quad c_2=\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}2,\quad c_3=\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)}6, $$

and in general $c_k=\binom\alpha k$.

This identifies the only possible coefficients, but it is not yet a convergence proof. For a nonterminating series and $x\ne0$, the absolute ratio of consecutive terms tends to

$$ \left|\frac{\alpha-k}{k+1}x\right|\longrightarrow|x|. $$

The ratio test gives absolute convergence for $|x|<1$. A power series can be differentiated term by term inside its interval of convergence. The sum therefore satisfies the derivative relation above. Dividing it by $(1+x)^\alpha$ produces a function with derivative zero and value 1 at zero, so the sum must equal $(1+x)^\alpha$ throughout $(-1,1)$.

That closes both parts of the argument: the coefficients have the right pattern, and their convergent series gives the intended function.

Do the endpoints belong?

The condition $|x|<1$ is the general guarantee. At $x=1$ or $x=-1$, convergence must be checked separately and depends on $\alpha$. For example, the series for $\alpha=-1$ diverges at both endpoints: its terms fail to approach zero. Do not replace the strict inequality by $|x|\leq1$ as a universal rule.

Check your understanding

Find the first four terms of $(1+x)^{-2}$. Are these coefficients counts of choices? Does the resulting infinite series converge at $x=2$?

Reveal the coefficients and domain

$$ \binom{-2}{0}=1,\quad\binom{-2}{1}=-2,\quad \binom{-2}{2}=3,\quad\binom{-2}{3}=-4. $$

So

$$ (1+x)^{-2}=1-2x+3x^2-4x^3+\cdots,\qquad |x|<1. $$

These signed coefficients are not literal counts of choosing objects. At $x=2$, the terms have magnitudes $(k+1)2^k$, which do not approach zero, so the series diverges even though the function $(1+2)^{-2}=1/9$ is well defined. A function's domain can be larger than the domain of a particular series representing it.
