What independence really means · Slide 11

Three independent events: at least one versus exactly one

Let $A,B,C$ be mutually independent events, each with probability $0.1$. The slide asks for three different probabilities: (a) $A$ or $B$ occurs; (b) at least one of $A,B,C$ occurs; (c) exactly one of the three occurs. Their answers are $0.19$, $0.271$, and $0.243$, respectively.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

First record what independence gives us

The supplied probabilities are

$$P(A)=P(B)=P(C)=0.1.$$

Mutual independence gives every pair and the triple:

$$ P(A\cap B)=P(A\cap C)=P(B\cap C)=0.1^2=0.01, $$

$$P(A\cap B\cap C)=0.1^3=0.001.$$

The triple equality is part of the assumption; pairwise independence alone would not justify it. Each complement has probability $0.9$, and replacing events by their complements preserves mutual independence.

Part (a): P(A or B)

The word “or” includes the possibility that both occur. Use the union formula:

$$ \begin{aligned} P(A\cup B) &=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\\ &=0.1+0.1-0.01\\ &=\boxed{0.19}. \end{aligned} $$

Adding $0.1+0.1$ alone would count the overlap twice. Subtracting $0.01$ leaves it counted once. There is no restriction on $C$: it may occur or fail in part (a).

Part (b): at least one of all three

The event is $A\cup B\cup C$. Inclusion–exclusion gives

$$ \begin{aligned} P(A\cup B\cup C) ={}&P(A)+P(B)+P(C)\\ &-P(A\cap B)-P(A\cap C)-P(B\cap C)\\ &+P(A\cap B\cap C)\\ ={}&0.3-0.03+0.001\\ ={}&\boxed{0.271}. \end{aligned} $$

An outcome where all three occur enters the first sum three times and the pair subtraction three times. The final addition restores its count to one.

The complement is simpler to calculate: at least one occurs unless none occurs. Since the three complements are mutually independent,

$$ \begin{aligned} P(A\cup B\cup C) &=1-P(A^c\cap B^c\cap C^c)\\ &=1-(0.9)(0.9)(0.9)\\ &=1-0.729=0.271. \end{aligned} $$

The complement rule itself needs no independence. The product $0.9^3$ uses the independence assumption.

Part (c): exactly one event occurs

There are three distinct ways to satisfy the requirement:

Which event occurs? Event description Probability
Only $A$ $A\cap B^c\cap C^c$ $0.1(0.9)(0.9)=0.081$
Only $B$ $A^c\cap B\cap C^c$ $(0.9)0.1(0.9)=0.081$
Only $C$ $A^c\cap B^c\cap C$ $(0.9)(0.9)0.1=0.081$

For the first row, one factor requires $A$ to occur and the other two require $B,C$ to fail. These three rows are mutually exclusive: one outcome cannot have “only $A$” and “only $B$.” We therefore add them:

$$\boxed{P(\text{exactly one})=3(0.1)(0.9)^2=0.243.}$$

We can check one row without invoking the complement-independence shortcut. Start with $A$, subtract its overlap with $B$ and its overlap with $C$, then restore the triple intersection subtracted twice:

$$P(A\cap B^c\cap C^c)=0.1-0.01-0.01+0.001=0.081.$$

Check the difference between parts (b) and (c)

Exactly two events occur with probability $3(0.1)^2(0.9)=0.027$: choose which of the three fails. All three occur with probability $0.001$. Thus

$$0.243+0.027+0.001=0.271.$$

Part (b) includes these extra two-or-three-event outcomes; part (c) excludes them. Including the no-event probability checks the whole sample space:

$$0.729+0.243+0.027+0.001=1.$$

Check your understanding

Why is $3(0.1)=0.3$ not the probability that exactly one event occurs?

Reveal the reasoning

Each $0.1$ includes cases where other events occur too. For “only $A$,” we must also require $B^c$ and $C^c$, reducing its probability to $0.081$. Adding the three unconditional probabilities counts outcomes with two events twice and outcomes with three events three times; it does not enforce “exactly one.”
