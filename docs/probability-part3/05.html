Probability after new information · Slide 05

Renormalizing means giving the remaining probability a total of one

Conditioning on $B$ removes outcomes outside $B$ from consideration and rescales the probabilities that remain. The rescaling is called normalization: the remaining weights must add to one. It preserves the relative weights of outcomes inside $B$; it does not automatically make them equally likely.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment
Let’s understand it

Read the source diagram as a restriction

The diagram starts with a whole sample space and overlapping events $A$ and $B$. Once $B$ is known, the portion outside $B$ is crossed out. Only the overlap $A\cap B$ can contribute to the probability of $A$ under this condition.

The denominator is all of $B$, including the part outside $A$. It is not “$B$ only” in the sense of $B\setminus A$. Both the overlap and the rest of $B$ remain possible.

The diagram's labels illustrate event membership. Unless a diagram explicitly represents probability by area, the drawn sizes alone do not provide numerical probabilities.

Follow unequal weights through the process

Consider a complete four-outcome experiment with the following probabilities:

Outcome Original probability
$a$ $0.10$
$b$ $0.20$
$c$ $0.30$
$d$ $0.40$

Let $B=\{a,b,c\}$ be the information we receive, and let $A=\{c,d\}$ be the event we care about. We know the result is in $B$, so $d$ is excluded. The remaining original weight is

$$P(B)=0.10+0.20+0.30=0.60.$$

Divide each retained probability by $0.60$:

Outcome Probability given $B$
$a$ $0.10/0.60=1/6$
$b$ $0.20/0.60=1/3$
$c$ $0.30/0.60=1/2$
$d$ $0$

The new probabilities sum to one. Outcome $c$ is still three times as likely as $a$, because dividing both weights by the same number preserves their ratio.

Within $B$, event $A$ can occur only through outcome $c$. Therefore

$$ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} =\frac{0.30}{0.60}=\boxed{\frac12}. $$

Counting one favorable outcome out of three remaining outcomes would give $1/3$, which is wrong here because those three outcomes are not equally likely.

Why dividing by P(B) is the right rescaling

All the retained outcomes together have old probability $P(B)$. If we multiply every retained weight by the same constant $c$, their new total is $cP(B)$. Requiring the new total to equal one gives

$$cP(B)=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=\frac1{P(B)}.$$

That is precisely the factor in the conditional probability formula. It retains the old relative weights while making the condition certain:

$$P(B\mid B)=\frac{P(B)}{P(B)}=1.$$

For the same reason, $A$ and $A^c$ still divide the conditioned population into complementary parts:

$$P(A\mid B)+P(A^c\mid B)=1.$$

Both terms must use the same condition $B$.

Explore the changing denominator

Compare the unconditional part belonging to $A$ with the fraction of $B$ occupied by the overlap. Moving the overlap can raise or lower the conditional probability, even if the total probabilities of $A$ and $B$ stay fixed.

Check your understanding

In the four-outcome example, what is $P(A^c\mid B)$? Why does the original probability of $d$ not enter the conditional numerator?

Reveal the reasoning

$A^c=\{a,b\}$, so $P(A^c\mid B)=(0.10+0.20)/0.60=1/2$. It complements $P(A\mid B)=1/2$. Outcome $d$ is outside $B$, so it is incompatible with the information and contributes to neither conditional numerator.
