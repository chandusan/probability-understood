Condition on the next draw · Slide 14

Multiply conditional probabilities along a sequence

To find the probability that several requirements all hold, multiply the probability of the first requirement by the probability of each later one given all the earlier requirements. This is the multiplication rule, or chain rule. It works for dependent events; independence is the special case that allows the conditions to be removed.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Derive the two-event rule

If $P(A)>0$, the definition of conditional probability says

$$P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}.$$

Multiply both sides by $P(A)$:

$$\boxed{P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A).}$$

We can reverse the roles when $P(B)>0$:

$$\boxed{P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B).}$$

These are two ways to calculate the same intersection. Choose the direction whose conditional probability is easier to obtain. Neither equality requires independence.

If $A$ and $B$ are independent, $P(B\mid A)=P(B)$ wherever that conditional is defined, and the rule simplifies to $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$. Without independence, replacing the conditional factor with the unconditional one changes the problem.

Follow a concrete path without replacement

An urn contains three red balls and two blue balls. Two balls are drawn uniformly without replacement. Let $R_1$ mean the first is red and $R_2$ mean the second is red.

For the first draw, three of the five balls are red:

$$P(R_1)=\frac35.$$

Given $R_1$, there are four balls left, of which two are red:

$$P(R_2\mid R_1)=\frac24.$$

Therefore

$$P(R_1\cap R_2)=\frac35\cdot\frac24=\frac3{10}.$$

The second factor uses the composition after the first required red draw. Using $3/5$ again would treat the draw as though the first ball had been replaced.

For “first red, then blue,” the first red draw leaves two blue balls among four, so that path also has probability $(3/5)(2/4)=3/10$. These paths end in different color sequences; if the question permits either sequence, their disjoint probabilities may be added.

Extend the rule to three events

Apply the two-event rule to $A\cap B$ and $C$:

$$P(A\cap B\cap C)=P(A\cap B)P(C\mid A\cap B).$$

Then expand the first factor:

$$ \boxed{P(A\cap B\cap C) =P(A)P(B\mid A)P(C\mid A\cap B).} $$

The final factor conditions on both previous requirements. In general, it cannot be replaced by $P(C\mid B)$: knowing $A$ as well may provide additional information.

For $n$ events the same pattern is

$$ \begin{aligned} P(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_n) ={}&P(A_1)P(A_2\mid A_1)\\ &\cdot P(A_3\mid A_1\cap A_2)\cdots P(A_n\mid A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_{n-1}). \end{aligned} $$

Each displayed conditional requires its conditioning event to have positive probability. If an earlier required intersection has probability zero, the full intersection also has probability zero; do not continue by conditioning on an impossible path.

Why the factors fit together

Substituting the conditional definitions into the three-event rule gives

$$ P(A)\cdot \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}\cdot \frac{P(A\cap B\cap C)}{P(A\cap B)}. $$

The intermediate probabilities cancel, leaving $P(A\cap B\cap C)$. This explains why each new factor uses the entire previous intersection as its denominator.

Check your understanding

In the urn with three red and two blue balls, what is the probability that three draws without replacement are red, blue, red in that order?

Reveal the reasoning

The first factor is $3/5$. After a red, two of four remaining balls are blue, giving $2/4$. After red then blue, two of three remaining balls are red, giving $2/3$. Thus the required path has probability $(3/5)(2/4)(2/3)=1/5$. Each denominator counts the balls still available at that step.
