Probability after new information · Slide 04

Conditional probability measures the part of B where A occurs

For events $A$ and $B$, the probability of $A$ given $B$ is the fraction of $B$'s probability that also belongs to $A$. The denominator records the information we know; the numerator records the part of that information compatible with the event we want.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Read the definition one symbol at a time

Provided $P(B)>0$,

$$\boxed{P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.}$$

  • $A$ is the event whose probability we are finding.
  • $B$ is the condition: we know the outcome belongs to $B$.
  • $A\cap B$ is the overlap, where both statements hold.
  • Dividing by $P(B)$ makes the total probability within the condition equal to one.

The vertical bar means “given,” not division. The expression to its right is itself an event, not a number. The fraction on the right of the equation tells us how to calculate the conditional probability.

The source describes $B$ as something that has “already happened.” That is a useful introductory story, but chronological order is not required. We can use an observation made later to reassess an earlier event. What matters mathematically is the information being conditioned on.

Distinguish joint, conditional, and reversed conditional probabilities

Roll a fair six-sided die. Let $A=\{2\}$ and $B=\{2,4,6\}$. Then:

Expression Question Value
$P(A)$ Before further information, is the result 2? $1/6$
$P(A\cap B)$ Is the result both 2 and even? $1/6$
$P(A\mid B)$ Given even, is it 2? $(1/6)/(3/6)=1/3$
$P(B\mid A)$ Given 2, is it even? $(1/6)/(1/6)=1$

An intersection does not privilege either event: $A\cap B=B\cap A$. A conditional does privilege the event after the bar, because that event supplies the denominator. Therefore $P(A\mid B)$ and $P(B\mid A)$ generally differ.

When can we use a ratio of counts?

If the original finite sample space has equally likely outcomes, then

$$ P(A\mid B) =\frac{|A\cap B|/|S|}{|B|/|S|} =\boxed{\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|}}. $$

The common original denominator cancels. This familiar “favorable outcomes over remaining outcomes” rule is a consequence of equal weights. If some original outcomes are more likely than others, we must keep their probability weights in the numerator and denominator.

Why the condition must have positive probability

If $P(B)=0$, the displayed definition divides by zero and does not define $P(A\mid B)$. In the finite positive-weight examples here, such a condition describes an impossible event. For example, conditioning an ordinary die on showing 7 has no meaning under this rule.

More advanced continuous models sometimes define conditionals through other constructions, but they do not make division by zero valid. In this chapter, verify that the conditioning event has positive probability before using the ratio.

Because $A\cap B\subseteq B$, its probability lies between zero and $P(B)$. Hence the conditional probability always lies between zero and one. Two useful endpoints are

$$P(B\mid B)=1,\qquad P(B^c\mid B)=0.$$

Check your understanding

Suppose $P(A)=0.4$, $P(B)=0.5$, and $P(A\cap B)=0.3$. Find both directions of conditional probability.

Reveal the reasoning

$P(A\mid B)=0.3/0.5=0.6$, while $P(B\mid A)=0.3/0.4=0.75$. Both use the same overlap. They differ because one measures that overlap relative to all of $B$, and the other relative to all of $A$.
