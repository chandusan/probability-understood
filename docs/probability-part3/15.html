Condition on the next draw · Slide 15

King, queen, jack: one order or any order

Draw three cards uniformly without replacement from a standard 52-card deck with no jokers. (a) What is the probability of a king first, a queen second, and a jack third? (b) What is the probability of one king, one queen, and one jack in any order? The second event contains six disjoint rank orders, each with the probability calculated in part (a).

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

State the deck and the drawing mechanism

The deck contains four kings, four queens, and four jacks, one of each rank in each suit. The question specifies ranks, not suits; the heart cards in the slide's illustration do not restrict the draws to hearts.

Without replacement means a drawn card stays out of the deck. Each next draw is uniform among the remaining cards. Consequently, denominators change from 52 to 51 to 50.

Let

$$K_1=\{\text{first card is a king}\},$$

$$Q_2=\{\text{second card is a queen}\},\qquad J_3=\{\text{third card is a jack}\}.$$

Part (a) asks for $P(K_1\cap Q_2\cap J_3)$.

Part (a): explain each conditional factor

There are four kings among the initial 52 cards, so

$$P(K_1)=\frac4{52}.$$

Given that the first card is a king, 51 cards remain. No queen has been removed, so all four queens remain:

$$P(Q_2\mid K_1)=\frac4{51}.$$

Given a king followed by a queen, 50 cards remain, including all four jacks:

$$P(J_3\mid K_1\cap Q_2)=\frac4{50}.$$

Use the multiplication rule:

$$ \begin{aligned} P(K_1\cap Q_2\cap J_3) &=P(K_1)P(Q_2\mid K_1)P(J_3\mid K_1\cap Q_2)\\ &=\frac4{52}\cdot\frac4{51}\cdot\frac4{50}\\ &=\frac{64}{132{,}600} =\boxed{\frac8{16{,}575}\approx0.000482655}. \end{aligned} $$

That is about $0.04827\%$. The numerators stay at four because each required rank is different. If the question required three kings, the numerators would instead be $4,3,2$.

Part (b): list all the allowed orders

Let $E$ mean that the three drawn ranks consist of one king, one queen, and one jack. It is the union of these six rank-sequence events:

Rank order Conditional product
$K,Q,J$ $(4/52)(4/51)(4/50)$
$K,J,Q$ $(4/52)(4/51)(4/50)$
$Q,K,J$ $(4/52)(4/51)(4/50)$
$Q,J,K$ $(4/52)(4/51)(4/50)$
$J,K,Q$ $(4/52)(4/51)(4/50)$
$J,Q,K$ $(4/52)(4/51)(4/50)$

Why six? Choose the first rank in three ways, the second in two, and the third in one: $3!=6$. Every row has the same product because, along that row, no earlier draw removes a card of the next required rank.

The rows are disjoint: a particular ordered draw has exactly one rank sequence. Therefore we add their probabilities:

$$ \begin{aligned} P(E) &=6\left(\frac4{52}\frac4{51}\frac4{50}\right)\\ &=\frac{384}{132{,}600} =\boxed{\frac{16}{5525}\approx0.002895928}. \end{aligned} $$

Thus the probability in any order is about $0.28959\%$.

Check part (b) with unordered hands

There are $\binom{52}{3}=22{,}100$ equally likely three-card hands. A favorable hand is formed by choosing one of four kings, one of four queens, and one of four jacks:

$$4\cdot4\cdot4=64\text{ favorable hands}.$$

Hence

$$P(E)=\frac{4^3}{\binom{52}{3}}=\frac{64}{22{,}100}=\frac{16}{5525}.$$

Do not add a factor of $3!$ to this numerator: the denominator already counts unordered hands. In the ordered calculation, both the total and favorable counts are six times their unordered versions. Keeping numerator and denominator in the same representation is what makes the probabilities agree.

Check your understanding

Why is $(12/52)(11/51)(10/50)$ not the answer to part (b)?

Reveal the reasoning

That product counts drawing any three cards from the 12 kings, queens, and jacks. It includes three kings, two queens and a jack, and other unwanted rank patterns. Part (b) requires exactly one of each of the three ranks, so the rank-specific counts must be enforced.
