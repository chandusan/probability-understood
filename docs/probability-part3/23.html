Total probability & Bayes’ rule · Slide 23

Bayes' rule reverses a conditional probability

We often know how likely an observation is within each group, but want to know which group an observed outcome came from. Bayes' rule first calculates the joint probability of each group and observation, then divides by the total probability of the observation.

~4 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Specify the two possible groups

Let $A_1$ and $A_2$ be disjoint and exhaustive events, so $A_2=A_1^c$. Let B be the observed event. Assume $P(A_1),P(A_2)>0$ and $P(B)>0$.

The supplied quantities are usually the group probabilities $P(A_i)$ and the within-group observation probabilities $P(B\mid A_i)$. The desired quantity reverses the bar: $P(A_i\mid B)$.

For the first group,

$$\boxed{ P(A_1\mid B)= \frac{P(B\mid A_1)P(A_1)} {P(B\mid A_1)P(A_1)+P(B\mid A_2)P(A_2)}.}$$

The numerator is the probability that both $A_1$ and B occur. The denominator includes every route to B, through either group.

For the second group, use its joint contribution in the numerator and the same denominator:

$$P(A_2\mid B)= \frac{P(B\mid A_2)P(A_2)} {P(B\mid A_1)P(A_1)+P(B\mid A_2)P(A_2)}.$$

The two answers add to one because the groups still partition the possibilities after restricting attention to B.

Work a complete box example

Choose Box 1 with probability 0.30 and Box 2 with probability 0.70. Box 1 contains eight red and two black balls; Box 2 contains two red and eight black balls. Draw one ball uniformly from the chosen box.

Let $A_i$ mean Box i was selected and let B mean the ball is red. Then

$$P(B\mid A_1)=\frac8{10}=0.80, \qquad P(B\mid A_2)=\frac2{10}=0.20.$$

The joint red probabilities are

$$P(A_1\cap B)=(0.30)(0.80)=0.24,$$

$$P(A_2\cap B)=(0.70)(0.20)=0.14.$$

Total probability gives $P(B)=0.24+0.14=0.38$. Once a red ball is observed,

$$\boxed{P(A_1\mid B)=\frac{0.24}{0.38}=\frac{12}{19}\approx63.16\%,}$$

$$\boxed{P(A_2\mid B)=\frac{0.14}{0.38}=\frac7{19}\approx36.84\%.}$$

Box 1 becomes the more probable source, even though it was selected less often initially, because red is much more likely within Box 1.

Read the same computation as counts

In a model population of 1,000 repetitions, 300 use Box 1 and 700 use Box 2. The stipulated rates give 240 red outcomes from Box 1 and 140 from Box 2.

Among the total 380 red outcomes, 240 came from Box 1. The posterior fraction is $240/380=12/19$. This explains why neither $0.80$ nor $0.30$ alone is the answer: one describes the red rate inside Box 1, and the other describes Box 1's initial share of all trials.

Prior, likelihood and posterior: the intuition

Imagine you see wet ground and want to figure out whether it rained.

Prior is your starting belief.
Before looking at the ground, how plausible was rain? Perhaps the forecast and cloudy sky already made you think rain was likely. That starting probability is your prior.

Likelihood describes how well an explanation predicts the clue.
Temporarily assume it did rain. Wet ground would be an expected observation. So wet ground has a high likelihood under the rain explanation.

But sprinklers could also produce wet ground. To judge how useful the clue is, consider how readily each possible explanation would produce it.

Posterior is your belief after taking the clue into account.
You combine your starting belief with how strongly the wet ground supports rain compared with other possibilities. Your updated probability that it rained is your posterior.

The distinction is:

  • Prior: How plausible the explanation was before this clue.
  • Likelihood: How expected this clue would be if that explanation were true.
  • Posterior: How plausible the explanation is after considering this clue.

In your box example, Box 1 starts with a 30% prior. Drawing red has an 80% likelihood if Box 1 was chosen. After seeing red and comparing both boxes, Box 1 has a 63.16% posterior.

Likelihood helps you update a belief; posterior is the updated belief.

Keep the condition fixed when taking a complement

It is valid to write

$$P(A_2\mid B)=1-P(A_1\mid B),$$

because within the B group exactly one of the two boxes was selected. But $P(B\mid A_2)$ is not generally $1-P(B\mid A_1)$. Those probabilities use different reference groups. They sum to one in this particular box example only because we happened to choose red rates 0.80 and 0.20.

Check your understanding

If both boxes instead had red probability 0.50, how would observing red change the box probabilities?

Equal likelihoods leave the prior proportions unchanged

The two joint red contributions would be $0.30(0.50)=0.15$ and $0.70(0.50)=0.35$. Dividing by their sum 0.50 gives posterior probabilities 0.30 and 0.70. The observation provides no evidence favoring either box because it is equally likely under both.
