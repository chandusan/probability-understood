Compare risks & predict a category · Slide 32

A risk ratio compares two conditional probabilities

A risk ratio asks how many times as likely an event is in one group as in a comparison group. First compute the event probability separately within each group; then divide those two probabilities.

~2 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Start with a table of counts

Let $E$ denote the event of interest and $G$ membership in the group being studied. The usual two-group table is

Group Event $E$ No event $E^c$ Total
Group $G$ $a$ $b$ $a+b$
Comparison group $G^c$ $c$ $d$ $c+d$

The slide calls the rows “exposed” and “unexposed.” They can also represent two customer groups or any other clearly specified comparison. Here risk simply means the probability of the event.

Normalize each row before comparing

Within group $G$, there are $a+b$ people, of whom $a$ experienced the event. Within group $G^c$, there are $c+d$ people, of whom $c$ experienced it. Thus

$$ P(E\mid G)=\frac{a}{a+b}, \qquad P(E\mid G^c)=\frac{c}{c+d}. $$

The relative risk is

$$ \boxed{\operatorname{RR} =\frac{P(E\mid G)}{P(E\mid G^c)} =\frac{a/(a+b)}{c/(c+d)}}. $$

Both groups need positive totals, and the comparison probability must be positive for a finite ratio. Dividing $a$ by $c$ alone would compare event counts without accounting for different group sizes.

Interpret the ratio and the absolute change separately

Suppose 20 of 100 customers in one group churn, compared with 10 of 200 in another. The probabilities are $0.20$ and $0.05$, so

$$ \operatorname{RR}=\frac{0.20}{0.05}=4. $$

Churn is four times as likely in the first group. The absolute difference is $0.20-0.05=0.15$, or 15 percentage points. The relative increase is $(0.20-0.05)/0.05=3$, or 300%. These are three ways of describing the same comparison, with different units and denominators.

An RR of one means equal event probabilities; above one means a higher probability in the numerator group, and below one a lower probability.

Always name the comparison group

“Twice the risk” is incomplete without saying compared with whom. A comparison with the entire population can differ from a comparison with everyone outside the selected group. The next customer example explicitly asks for the overall baseline, so that baseline must be used.

A ratio computed from an observed joint table describes an association. The table alone does not establish that membership in one group causes the outcome.
