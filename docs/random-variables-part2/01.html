Density, accumulation & endpoints · Slide 01

Density measures probability per unit of the horizontal axis

A continuous density turns intervals into probabilities by measuring area. Before moving to two random variables, let us make the one-variable rules precise: a density is a rate of probability accumulation, while a CDF is the amount accumulated so far.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Start with the objects we are describing

Let $X$ be a random variable that has a probability density $f$. Its cumulative distribution function is

$$F(x)=P(X\le x).$$

The input $x$ is a threshold. The value $F(x)$ is the fraction of the probability lying at or below that threshold. The density $f(x)$ describes how quickly this fraction grows when we move the threshold near $x$.

For a small positive width $h$, at a point where the density is continuous,

$$P(x<X\le x+h)=\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt\approx f(x)h.$$

The probability belongs to the strip, not to its height alone. If $X$ is measured in minutes, $f(x)$ has units of probability per minute.

Why the three area rules are necessary

A density must be nonnegative: a region cannot have negative probability. Its total area must be one because $X$ must take some value. The probability of an interval is the portion of that total area above the interval:

$$f(x)\ge0,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1,\qquad P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.$$

For example, suppose $X$ is uniform on $(0,0.2)$ minutes. The density is $5$ on this interval and zero elsewhere. The total area is $5(0.2)=1$. The probability of $(0.05,0.10)$ is $5(0.05)=0.25$. A density of $5$ is valid because it is an area, not the height, that must stay between zero and one.

Why one exact value has probability zero

For a distribution with a density, an interval of zero width has zero area:

$$P(X=a)=\int_a^a f(x)\,dx=0.$$

Adding or removing the endpoints of an interval therefore changes its probability by zero. This explains all four equalities on the source slide:

$$P(a<X<b)=P(a\le X<b)=P(a<X\le b)=P(a\le X\le b).$$

This does not say that $X$ cannot take a particular value. It says that no individual point receives a positive share of the total probability. An uncountable collection of zero-probability points can still form an interval with positive probability.

Why the density is the derivative of the CDF

Move the right boundary from $x$ to $x+h$. The added probability is a thin strip:

$$F(x+h)-F(x)=\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,dt\approx f(x)h.$$

Dividing by $h$ gives the probability added per unit movement. As the strip narrows, its average height tends to $f(x)$, so

$$F'(x)=f(x)$$

where $f$ is continuous. In general, a distribution with a density has this equality almost everywhere; a density can be assigned different values at isolated points without changing any probabilities. The standalone calculus appendix explains the area–derivative connection geometrically.

A qualification to the slide's wording: these density statements apply when a density exists. A CDF is defined for every random variable; continuity of a CDF by itself is not enough to guarantee a density. No such exceptional distribution is needed for the exercises in this chapter.

A quick distinction to keep

Object What it tells you How to obtain an interval probability
Density $f(x)$ Probability per unit near $x$ Integrate over the interval
CDF $F(x)$ Probability accumulated through $x$ Subtract the two boundary values

The chapter will extend this same bookkeeping to pairs of values, then show how to keep one variable or condition on the other.
