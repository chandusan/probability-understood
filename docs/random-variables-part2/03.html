Density, accumulation & endpoints · Slide 03

The CDF works for discrete, continuous, and mixed distributions

A cumulative distribution function always means $F(x)=P(X\le x)$. It lets us ask the same threshold question whether probabilities live at isolated points, across intervals, or in a mixture of the two.

~2 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Derive the interval rule by splitting an event

For $a<b$, every outcome with $X\le b$ belongs to exactly one of two groups: $X\le a$, or $a<X\le b$. The groups are disjoint. Therefore,

$$P(X\le b)=P(X\le a)+P(a<X\le b).$$

Rearranging gives

$$\boxed{P(a<X\le b)=F(b)-F(a).}$$

The subtraction removes everything at or below $a$, including any probability exactly at $a$. It keeps everything at or below $b$, including any probability exactly at $b$. This is why the inequalities matter.

A jump is probability at one exact point

Write $F(a^-)$ for the CDF value approached from the left. It equals $P(X<a)$. The jump at $a$ is

$$F(a)-F(a^-)=P(X=a).$$

For example, if $F(2^-)=0.45$ and $F(2)=0.65$, then $P(X=2)=0.20$. Replacing $X>2$ with $X\ge2$ adds that $0.20$.

Event General formula
$a<X\le b$ $F(b)-F(a)$
$a\le X\le b$ $F(b)-F(a^-)$
$a<X<b$ $F(b^-)-F(a)$
$a\le X<b$ $F(b^-)-F(a^-)$

When the CDF is continuous at both endpoints, the left limits equal the endpoint values, so these four probabilities agree.

Understand the shape restrictions

The CDF never decreases. Raising the threshold can only include more outcomes. Formally, if $a<b$, then $\{X\le a\}\subseteq\{X\le b\}$, so $F(a)\le F(b)$.

The far-left limit is zero and the far-right limit is one. Moving the threshold far enough left eventually excludes any particular finite value of $X$. Moving it far enough right eventually includes it. These are limits: the CDF need not actually reach zero or one at finite thresholds.

The CDF is right-continuous. If thresholds decrease toward $x$ from above, their events shrink toward $\{X\le x\}$. The probabilities therefore approach $F(x)$. At a jump, the filled point belongs at the upper end, because $F(x)$ includes $X=x$.

The graph need not have a particular silhouette

A discrete CDF accumulates point masses through jumps; for the finite examples here it is a step graph. A distribution with a density accumulates through an integral. A mixed CDF can have both jumps and smoothly increasing portions.

The source says a continuous CDF can be “S-shaped.” That is an example of a shape, not a requirement. A uniform distribution has a straight rising segment; the density $2x$ on $(0,1)$ gives the curved segment $F(x)=x^2$. Neither needs the familiar bell-distribution S-shape.

When differentiating helps, and what it can miss

If $F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt$, differentiating recovers $f$ where the ordinary derivative identity applies. A jump, however, cannot be represented by the area under an ordinary finite density at just one point.

For a mixed distribution, handle jumps separately. Differentiating the smooth pieces finds the continuous probability contribution, but the jump sizes must also be counted. A CDF difference automatically accounts for both.
