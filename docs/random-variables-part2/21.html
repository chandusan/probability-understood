Sum out a variable · Slide 21

Marginal distributions keep one variable and add over the other

A joint distribution tells us how two measurements occur together. A marginal distribution answers a question about just one of them. To obtain it, keep the value we care about and add the probabilities of every possible value of the other variable.

~2 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Read the joint and marginal questions separately

Suppose a fair die is rolled independently five times. Let $X$ count sixes and $Y$ count fives. The joint probability

$$ p(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y) $$

answers: what is the probability of exactly $x$ sixes and exactly $y$ fives? The comma means both conditions must hold.

The marginal probability

$$ p_X(x)=P(X=x) $$

answers: what is the probability of exactly $x$ sixes, however many fives occurred? A marginal question leaves the other variable unrestricted.

For example, $X=2$ can happen together with $Y=0,1,2,$ or $3$. These possibilities cannot occur together, and they cover every way to have two sixes. Therefore

$$ p_X(2)=p(2,0)+p(2,1)+p(2,2)+p(2,3). $$

Terms such as $p(2,4)$ are zero because two sixes and four fives would require at least six rolls.

State the general rule

For any two discrete random variables,

$$ \boxed{p_X(x)=\sum_y p(x,y)}, \qquad \boxed{p_Y(y)=\sum_x p(x,y)}. $$

In the first sum, hold $x$ fixed and vary $y$. In the second, hold $y$ fixed and vary $x$. The symbol below the sum names the value that runs through its possibilities.

If rows represent $x$ and columns represent $y$, row totals give $p_X(x)$ and column totals give $p_Y(y)$. The name marginal comes from writing these totals along the table's margins. Always check the axis labels: the mathematics does not depend on which variable is drawn horizontally.

Why there is no division here

We still consider the entire original experiment. We have simply grouped together several disjoint outcomes, so we add their probabilities. Nothing has been observed that would restrict the population being considered.

For the dice example,

$$ p_X(2)=\frac{640+480+120+10}{7776} =\frac{1250}{7776}. $$

All $6^5=7776$ equally likely ordered roll sequences remain in the denominator. We count those with two sixes, including every allowable number of fives.

A marginal distribution is a complete PMF

Its entries are nonnegative, and

$$ \sum_x p_X(x)=\sum_x\sum_y p(x,y)=1. $$

Summing every row total counts each joint cell once. The same argument applies to the column totals. However, the two marginals alone generally cannot tell us how the variables occur together; that information is contained inside the joint table.
