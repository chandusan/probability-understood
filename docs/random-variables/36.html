Solve complete distribution problems · Slide 36

A lottery's net gain includes the ticket cost in every outcome

The prize amount and the buyer's net gain are different random variables. Subtracting the ticket price changes every possible value, including the losing outcome, while leaving each outcome's probability unchanged.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

State the experiment and the prize groups

There are 5,000 lottery tickets, each costing one dollar. One ticket wins 1,000 dollars, two tickets win 500 dollars each, and ten tickets win 100 dollars each. A ticket is chosen uniformly from all 5,000 tickets.

Assume the prize categories contain 13 distinct winning tickets, so no ticket receives more than one of the listed prizes. This is the usual interpretation of the exercise and makes the categories disjoint. The remaining number of tickets is

$$ 5000-(1+2+10)=4987. $$

Let $W$ be the prize received and $X$ the net gain, both measured in dollars. Then

$$ \boxed{X=W-1.} $$

The sample space consists of 5,000 ticket identities, each with probability $1/5000$. The random variable maps each identity to the buyer's net gain for that ticket.

Convert each prize into a net gain

A losing ticket pays no prize, so its net gain is $0-1=-1$. A ticket receiving a 100-dollar prize yields $100-1=99$, and the other prize categories are handled in the same way:

Prize $W$ Calculation Net gain $X$ Number of tickets
0 $0-1$ $-1$ 4,987
100 $100-1$ $99$ 10
500 $500-1$ $499$ 2
1,000 $1000-1$ $999$ 1

The losing value is negative because the buyer still paid for the ticket. Recording zero for a loss would describe the prize, rather than the net gain requested in the question.

Construct the complete PMF

For each gain, divide the number of tickets producing it by the total number of equally likely tickets:

$$ \boxed{p_X(x)= \begin{cases} 4987/5000,&x=-1,\\ 10/5000,&x=99,\\ 2/5000,&x=499,\\ 1/5000,&x=999,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}} $$

All entries are nonnegative, and

$$ \frac{4987+10+2+1}{5000}=1, $$

so this is a valid probability distribution. The four possible gains are not equally likely: they describe groups of tickets with very different sizes.

Read event probabilities from the distribution

Every winning ticket gives a positive net gain, so

$$ P(X>0)=\frac{10+2+1}{5000} =\frac{13}{5000}=0.0026. $$

Thus the probability of a positive net gain is $0.26\%$, while $P(X=-1)=4987/5000=0.9974$. These probabilities add to one because this lottery has no break-even outcome.

The cost also matters when reading a threshold. A prize of 500 dollars gives a net gain of 499 dollars, so

$$ P(X\ge500)=P(X=999)=\frac1{5000}, $$

whereas $P(W\ge500)=3/5000$. The events differ because their cutoffs refer to different random variables.

Express the same distribution cumulatively

Adding the masses from left to right gives

$$ F_X(x)= \begin{cases} 0,&x<-1,\\ 4987/5000,&-1\le x<99,\\ 4997/5000,&99\le x<499,\\ 4999/5000,&499\le x<999,\\ 1,&x\ge999. \end{cases} $$

For instance, $F_X(0)=4987/5000$ even though $P(X=0)=0$. Cumulative probability includes every gain below the cutoff; it need not be mass located at the cutoff itself.

Check your understanding

If the same prizes were offered with a two-dollar ticket price, what would change in the distribution?

Shift values while preserving their probabilities

The new net gains would be $-2,98,498,998$. The corresponding probabilities would remain $4987/5000,10/5000,2/5000,1/5000$, assuming the ticket counts and uniform selection stay the same. Changing a fixed cost shifts the recorded gains but does not change which tickets win.

Exercise 01 / Slide 5

Training stops at consecutive outcomes

After each training epoch, an automated tuning script compares a model's validation loss with the previous value. It records one of three outcomes: decrease ($D$), increase ($I$), or unchanged ($U$).

Training stops immediately when either of these happens:

  • The loss increases on two consecutive epochs ($II$).
  • The loss is unchanged on three consecutive epochs ($UUU$).

Let $N$ be the number of evaluated epochs up to and including the stopping epoch. Use a baseline loss before the first counted epoch, so that the first counted epoch already produces a label $D$, $I$, or $U$.

State the three smallest possible values of $N$ and list all exact sequences of outcomes corresponding to each value.

Show solutionHide solution: Training stops at consecutive outcomes

An exact sequence must stop for the first time at its end

A valid stopping sequence must meet both conditions:

  1. Its final letter completes $II$ or $UUU$.
  2. No earlier part of the sequence has already completed either pattern.

For example, $DII$ stops after three epochs. The string $IIU$ cannot be a three-epoch stopping sequence because the process would already have stopped after $II$.

The smallest possible value is 2

One letter cannot complete either stopping rule. After two letters, only $II$ can stop the process:

$$N=2:\qquad \boxed{II}.$$

Stop for the first time at epoch 3

There are two possibilities for the final stopping pattern.

End in $II$: write the sequence as $aII$. The first letter $a$ cannot be $I$, because $III$ would have stopped after its first two letters. It can be $D$ or $U$, giving $DII$ and $UII$.

End in $UUU$: the only three-letter sequence is $UUU$, and none of its earlier prefixes triggers a rule.

Thus

$$N=3:\qquad \boxed{DII,\ UII,\ UUU}.$$

Stop for the first time at epoch 4

End in $II$: write the sequence as $abII$. The second letter $b$ cannot be $I$, since positions 2 and 3 would then already be $II$.

  • If $b=D$, the first letter can be $D$, $I$, or $U$: $DDII$, $IDII$, $UDII$.
  • If $b=U$, the first letter can again be $D$, $I$, or $U$: $DUII$, $IUII$, $UUII$.

These six sequences contain no earlier $II$, and their first three letters cannot be $UUU$ because the third letter is $I$.

End in $UUU$: write the sequence as $aUUU$. The first letter cannot be $U$, because $UUUU$ would already stop at epoch 3. Thus $a$ is $D$ or $I$, giving $DUUU$ and $IUUU$.

Collecting the answer:

Value of $N$ All exact stopping sequences Count
2 $II$ 1
3 $DII,UII,UUU$ 3
4 $DDII,IDII,UDII,DUII,IUII,UUII,DUUU,IUUU$ 8

The three smallest possible values are therefore 2, 3, and 4. The question provides no probabilities for $D$, $I$, and $U$, so these sequence counts alone do not determine probabilities such as $P(N=4)$.

Exercise 02 / Slide 22

Recover a density from a polynomial CDF

A random variable $X$ has cumulative distribution function

$$F_X(x)=\frac{x^2+x^3}{12},\qquad 0\le x\le2.$$

Find its probability density function $f_X(x)$, including its value outside the given interval.

Show solutionHide solution: Recover a density from a polynomial CDF

First identify the full CDF

At the endpoints,

$$F_X(0)=0,\qquad F_X(2)=\frac{4+8}{12}=1.$$

A CDF is nondecreasing and stays between $0$ and $1$. Therefore it must be $0$ before $x=0$ and $1$ after $x=2$:

$$F_X(x)=\begin{cases} 0,&x<0,\\[2pt] \dfrac{x^2+x^3}{12},&0\le x\le2,\\[5pt] 1,&x>2. \end{cases}$$

Differentiate the whole middle expression term by term

The CDF is accumulated probability; its rate of increase is the density. For $0<x<2$,

$$\begin{aligned} f_X(x)=F_X'(x) &=\frac1{12}\left(\frac{d}{dx}x^2+\frac{d}{dx}x^3\right)\\ &=\frac{2x+3x^2}{12}. \end{aligned}$$

The factor $1/12$ remains unchanged. The power rule changes $x^2$ to $2x$ and $x^3$ to $3x^2$.

Outside $(0,2)$ the CDF is constant, so the density is zero. One complete version of the PDF is

$$\boxed{f_X(x)=\begin{cases} \dfrac{2x+3x^2}{12},&0<x<2,\\[5pt] 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}}$$

Check that this is a valid density

The expression is nonnegative on $(0,2)$, and its total area is

$$\begin{aligned} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(x)\,dx &=\int_0^2\frac{2x+3x^2}{12}\,dx\\ &=\left[\frac{x^2+x^3}{12}\right]_0^2\\ &=1. \end{aligned}$$

At $x=2$ the CDF has a corner, so the derivative does not exist there. That causes no problem: the PDF's value at a single point does not change an integral. We chose zero at the endpoints. The CDF has no jump at either endpoint, so neither endpoint carries a point mass.

Exercise 03 / Slide 36

Net gain from one lottery ticket

Five thousand lottery tickets are sold for 1 dollar each. One ticket wins 1,000 dollars, two tickets win 500 dollars each, and ten tickets win 100 dollars each. The winning tickets are distinct; each receives just one of these prizes.

One ticket is selected uniformly at random from the 5,000 tickets. Let $X$ be the buyer's net gain, in dollars, from purchasing that ticket.

Construct the probability distribution of $X$.

Show solutionHide solution: Net gain from one lottery ticket

Subtract the cost in every outcome

Net gain means money received minus money paid. If $W$ is the prize, then

$$X=W-1.$$

A losing ticket pays no prize, so its net gain is $-1$, not zero. The four possibilities are:

Prize in dollars Net gain $X$ Number of tickets
0 $0-1=-1$ $5000-(1+2+10)=4987$
100 $100-1=99$ 10
500 $500-1=499$ 2
1,000 $1000-1=999$ 1

The 13 winning tickets are distinct, so the number of losing tickets is 4,987.

Divide each group size by 5,000

Each individual ticket has probability $1/5000$ of being selected. A net gain's probability is therefore the number of tickets producing that gain divided by 5,000:

$$\boxed{p_X(x)=P(X=x)=\begin{cases} \dfrac{4987}{5000},&x=-1,\\[4pt] \dfrac{10}{5000},&x=99,\\[4pt] \dfrac{2}{5000},&x=499,\\[4pt] \dfrac{1}{5000},&x=999,\\[4pt] 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}}$$

These probabilities add to one:

$$\frac{4987+10+2+1}{5000}=1.$$

The four gain values are not equally likely. They collect different numbers of equally likely tickets. Subtracting the ticket cost changes the gain values while leaving those ticket counts, and hence their probabilities, unchanged.
