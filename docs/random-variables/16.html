Build a distribution from geometry · Slide 16

The complete distance CDF, with boundary and probability checks

The geometric pieces now give a complete distribution for the distance $X$ from the lower-left corner to a uniformly chosen point in the unit square. A CDF must be defined for every real threshold, even though the distance itself can only lie between 0 and $\sqrt2$.

~2 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment
Let’s understand it

Put the four branches together

The result is

$$ \boxed{ F(x)= \begin{cases} 0,&x<0,\\[2pt] \dfrac{\pi x^2}{4},&0\le x\le1,\\[5pt] \sqrt{x^2-1}+x^2\left(\dfrac\pi4-\arctan\sqrt{x^2-1}\right), &1<x<\sqrt2,\\[5pt] 1,&x\ge\sqrt2. \end{cases}} $$

For the middle branch, $\sqrt{x^2-1}$ is the combined area of two right triangles. The remaining term is the central sector area. These terms came from the overlap of the radius-$x$ disk with the square, rather than from a guess about the shape of the CDF.

Verify that the branches meet

At $x=0$, the quarter-disk expression is zero, agreeing with the branch to its left.

At $x=1$, the middle expression has

$$ \sqrt{1^2-1}=0,\qquad\arctan0=0. $$

Its value is therefore $0+1(\pi/4-0)=\pi/4$, equal to the first curved branch. At $x=\sqrt2$, the same expression has

$$ \sqrt{2-1}=1,\qquad\arctan1=\frac\pi4, $$

so it gives $1+2(\pi/4-\pi/4)=1$. There are no jumps at either transition.

The source assigns some endpoints to both adjacent branches. That is harmless because the formulas agree there. Our display uses nonoverlapping cases to make evaluation unambiguous.

Read the graph as accumulated probability

The CDF starts at zero, rises, and then stays at one. It can never decrease: enlarging the radius can add qualifying points but cannot remove any. Its height at $x$ is the probability of the entire event $X\le x$, not the probability of the single distance $X=x$.

For example,

$$ P(X>1)=1-F(1)=1-\frac\pi4\approx0.21460. $$

This is the area near the upper-right corner that lies outside the unit-radius quarter disk. Meanwhile $P(X=1)=0$, since the circular boundary has zero area. A positive CDF value at 1 is entirely compatible with zero probability at the exact value 1.

An exact example inside the harder range

Choose $x=2/\sqrt3$. This lies between 1 and $\sqrt2$, and it makes the angle easy to recognize:

$$ \sqrt{x^2-1}=\sqrt{\frac43-1}=\frac1{\sqrt3}, \qquad \arctan\frac1{\sqrt3}=\frac\pi6. $$

Substituting gives

$$ \begin{aligned} F(2/\sqrt3) &=\frac1{\sqrt3}+\frac43\left(\frac\pi4-\frac\pi6\right)\\ &=\frac1{\sqrt3}+\frac\pi9\approx0.92642. \end{aligned} $$

Therefore the probability of a distance between 1 and $2/\sqrt3$ is

$$ \begin{aligned} P(1<X\le2/\sqrt3) &=F(2/\sqrt3)-F(1)\\ &=\frac1{\sqrt3}-\frac{5\pi}{36}\approx0.14102. \end{aligned} $$

The subtraction removes all points already counted within radius 1, leaving just the additional area swept out by increasing the radius.

Check: what is the probability that X exceeds 3/2?

It is zero. Since $3/2>\sqrt2$, the final branch gives $F(3/2)=1$. Thus $P(X>3/2)=1-F(3/2)=0$. Check the threshold's range before substituting it into a formula containing square roots and inverse tangents.
