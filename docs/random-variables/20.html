Understand probability density · Slide 20

Differentiate accumulated probability to recover density

The relation $F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt$ tells us how a density produces a CDF. The reverse operation is differentiation. Where the usual Fundamental Theorem of Calculus applies, the slope of the CDF equals the density height.

~2 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Calculus refresher: derivatives as local rates of change.

Start with the probability of a short interval

For $h>0$,

$$ F(x+h)-F(x)=P(x<X\le x+h)=\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt. $$

Divide by the interval width:

$$ \frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h} =\frac1h\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt. $$

The left side is the average change in accumulated probability per unit of $x$. The right side is the average density height across that interval. This is the precise connection between CDF slope and density.

If $f$ is continuous at $x$, its nearby heights approach $f(x)$ as $h$ shrinks. The average height therefore approaches $f(x)$. The same reasoning for increments from the left gives

$$ \boxed{F'(x)=f(x)} $$

at every point where the density is continuous. More generally, a distribution with a density has this identity almost everywhere; the next lesson explains why “everywhere” needs qualification.

Use the derivative to interpret local probability

The derivative statement can be written as the approximation

$$ F(x+h)-F(x)\approx F'(x)h=f(x)h $$

for a small positive width $h$. A steep CDF means that a short interval adds relatively much probability. A nearly flat CDF means that the same width adds little.

This does not turn the density into $P(X=x)$. The derivative is the limiting ratio of an interval probability to its width. The interval probability itself tends to zero as the width tends to zero.

Work through the square-law example

Suppose

$$ F(x)= \begin{cases} 0,&x<0,\\ x^2,&0\le x\le1,\\ 1,&x>1. \end{cases} $$

On $0<x<1$, differentiation gives $f(x)=2x$. Outside $[0,1]$, the CDF is constant, so its derivative is zero. For $x$ and $x+h$ both inside $(0,1)$,

$$ \begin{aligned} P(x<X\le x+h) &=(x+h)^2-x^2\\ &=2xh+h^2. \end{aligned} $$

Dividing by $h$ gives $2x+h$, which tends to $2x$. This directly verifies the derivative without appealing only to a memorized formula.

At $x=0.5$ and $h=0.01$, the exact probability is

$$ 0.51^2-0.5^2=0.0101. $$

The density approximation gives $f(0.5)(0.01)=1(0.01)=0.01$. The difference $0.0001$ is the $h^2$ term.

Check the joins of a piecewise CDF

At $x=1$ in this example, the left derivative is 2 and the right derivative is 0. The CDF has a corner, so its ordinary two-sided derivative does not exist there. Nevertheless, the distribution has a valid density: choose $f(x)=2x$ on $(0,1)$, zero outside, and assign any nonnegative finite value at the endpoint.

Changing a function's value at one point changes none of its integrals. A missing derivative at an isolated boundary therefore does not invalidate the density model.

Check: what does the derivative at a CDF plateau mean?

Where $F$ is constant on an interval, $F'=0$. The interval carries no probability, and a valid density can be taken to be zero there. The CDF can resume increasing later if the distribution has another region of possible values.
