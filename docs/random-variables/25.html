Differentiate & combine distributions · Slide 25

Complete the chain rule and interpret the distance density

The remaining derivative is $d[\arctan\sqrt{x^2-1}]/dx$. Computing it carefully makes two large terms cancel and leaves a simple density. That final formula also has a geometric interpretation: the length of the growing circular boundary that still lies inside the square.

~2 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment
Let’s understand it

Calculus refresher: the chain rule, one layer at a time.

Differentiate the inverse tangent one layer at a time

For $1<x<\sqrt2$, set

$$ u(x)=\sqrt{x^2-1}. $$

There are two nested derivatives. First,

$$ u'(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x^2-1}}(2x) =\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}. $$

Second, the derivative of $\arctan u$ with respect to $u$ is $1/(1+u^2)$. The chain rule multiplies these:

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}\arctan u(x) &=\frac{1}{1+u(x)^2}\,u'(x)\\ &=\frac{1}{1+(\sqrt{x^2-1})^2} \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\\ &=\frac{1}{1+x^2-1}\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\\ &=\frac{x}{x^2\sqrt{x^2-1}}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{1}{x\sqrt{x^2-1}}}. \end{aligned} $$

The denominator becomes $x^2$ because $1+u^2=1+(x^2-1)$. Cancelling one factor of $x$ is valid on this interval, where $x>1$.

Substitute and show the exact cancellation

The product-rule expansion was

$$ F'(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}+\frac{\pi x}{2} -2x\arctan\sqrt{x^2-1} -x^2\frac{d}{dx}\arctan\sqrt{x^2-1}. $$

Its last term is now

$$ -x^2\frac{1}{x\sqrt{x^2-1}} =-\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}. $$

This cancels the first term, leaving

$$ \boxed{f(x)=\frac{\pi x}{2}-2x\arctan\sqrt{x^2-1}} \qquad(1<x<\sqrt2). $$

No infinite term remains. Although the two cancelled terms individually grow without bound as $x$ approaches 1 from above, their sum is zero for every $x>1$.

The full density and the transition points

Together with the other branches,

$$ f(x)= \begin{cases} 0,&x<0,\\[2pt] \dfrac{\pi x}{2},&0\le x\le1,\\[5pt] x\left(\dfrac\pi2-2\arctan\sqrt{x^2-1}\right),&1<x<\sqrt2,\\[5pt] 0,&x\ge\sqrt2. \end{cases} $$

The left and right limiting values at 1 are both $\pi/2$. At $\sqrt2$, the bracket becomes $\pi/2-2(\pi/4)=0$. At 0, the density is also zero. These endpoint assignments make the density continuous; changing any one endpoint value would leave all probabilities unchanged.

Why this expression is a circular arc length

For $0<x<1$, the entire first-quadrant circular arc lies inside the square. Its angle is $\pi/2$, so its length is radius times angle, $x\pi/2=f(x)$.

For $1<x<\sqrt2$, only the arc between the top and right intersections lies inside the square. The geometry gave

$$ \theta=\frac\pi2-2\phi, \qquad\phi=\arctan\sqrt{x^2-1}. $$

Its length is $x\theta$, exactly the second density formula. Increasing the radius by a small width $h$ adds a thin region whose area is approximately arc length times $h$. Since the square's area is 1, that added area is approximately $f(x)h$, the probability of the corresponding short distance interval.

Check normalization and the meaning of a tall density

The density integrates to

$$ \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx+\int_1^{\sqrt2}f(x)\,dx =\frac\pi4+\left(1-\frac\pi4\right)=1, $$

using the changes in the CDF across the two ranges. At 1, $f(1)=\pi/2>1$, which is allowed: this is probability per unit of distance, not $P(X=1)$. The exact-distance event is a circular boundary of zero area, so $P(X=1)=0$.

Check: why does the density approach zero at the farthest corner?

As $x$ approaches $\sqrt2$, the two circle–square intersections approach the upper-right corner. The angle $\theta$ and the arc length inside the square both shrink to zero. Increasing the radius then adds less and less area per unit radius. Once $x\ge\sqrt2$, the whole square is already included and the CDF cannot grow further.
