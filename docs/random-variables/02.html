Turn outcomes into numbers · Slide 02

A random variable is a fixed rule applied to a random outcome

The slide defines a random variable as a numerical quantity determined by a random experiment. More precisely, it is a function: each possible outcome is assigned one real number. The rule itself is fixed; the uncertainty comes from not knowing which outcome will occur.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Separate the outcome, the function, and its value

Let $S$ be a sample space and let $\omega$ denote one outcome in it. A real-valued random variable is written

$$X:S\longrightarrow\mathbb R.$$

This notation says that $X$ takes an outcome as its input and returns a real number. For example, when two dice are rolled,

$$\omega=(2,5),\qquad X(i,j)=i+j,\qquad X(\omega)=7.$$

The ordered pair is the outcome, adding the coordinates is the rule, and 7 is the resulting value. We usually write $P(X=7)$ as shorthand for the probability of the event containing all outcomes that the rule maps to 7.

Work through the source examples

Experiment or uncertain situation Definition of $X$ Kind of numerical value
Roll two dice Add their upward faces One of $2,3,\ldots,12$
Observe earthquakes in a specified San Francisco region over 2000–2050 Count qualifying earthquakes A nonnegative integer
Observe the future value of a financial index Record its value thirty days after the stated starting date A numerical measurement

For the earthquake example, a fully specified application would define the geographic boundary, time endpoints, and what qualifies as an earthquake in the record, such as a magnitude threshold. Those choices define what gets counted. They do not by themselves supply a probability distribution for the count.

The index value $51{,}415.55$ is the illustrative starting figure printed on the source slide, not a claim about today's market. The example's purpose is to distinguish a starting observation from an uncertain later measurement. A model would still be needed to assign probabilities to future values.

A function can combine many outcomes into one value

For dice sums, $(1,6)$ and $(2,5)$ are different outcomes with the same value $X=7$. A different random variable on the same experiment could be

$$Y(i,j)=\max(i,j),$$

the larger face. Then $Y(1,6)=6$ while $Y(2,5)=5$. The experiment did not change; the numerical question changed.

This is why the random variable's definition must come before its distribution. “Two dice are rolled” does not tell us whether to calculate a sum, maximum, difference, or number of sixes.

Defining X does not make its values equally likely

If the dice are independent and fair, all 36 ordered face pairs are equally likely. But there is one pair giving a sum of 2 and six pairs giving a sum of 7. Their probabilities differ even though the same deterministic rule is used in both cases.

A random variable need not itself range over every real number. A count has isolated values such as $0,1,2,\ldots$; a distance can range over an interval. These lead to different ways of describing distributions, but both begin with a numerical mapping.

Check your understanding

For two dice, define $Z$ as the number of dice showing six. What values can $Z$ take, and what are $Z(2,5)$, $Z(6,2)$, and $Z(6,6)$?

Reveal the reasoning

The possible values are $0,1,2$. The three outcomes give $Z(2,5)=0$, $Z(6,2)=1$, and $Z(6,6)=2$. The random variable reports a count of sixes, so it is different from either the sum or the largest face.
