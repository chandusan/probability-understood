Turn outcomes into numbers · Slide 04

A point in a square becomes a distance

Choose a point uniformly from a square of side length 1. Let $X$ be its distance from the lower-left corner. The outcome is a two-coordinate location, while the random variable records one number. Many different locations produce the same distance, so understanding $X$ means understanding the regions of points associated with its values.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Put coordinates on the picture

Place the lower-left corner at $(0,0)$ and the upper-right corner at $(1,1)$. The sample space is

$$S=[0,1]\times[0,1].$$

An outcome is a point $(u,v)$ with $0\le u,v\le1$. By the Pythagorean theorem, its distance from the origin is

$$\boxed{X(u,v)=\sqrt{u^2+v^2}.}$$

The rule is deterministic: once the point is chosen, no additional randomness is needed to compute its distance.

For example,

$$X(0.3,0.4)=\sqrt{0.09+0.16}=0.5.$$

The distinct points $(0,0.5)$ and $(0.5,0)$ also produce $X=0.5$. Recording only the distance does not tell us which direction the point lies from the corner.

Find the possible range

The minimum distance is 0, at the lower-left corner. The maximum is the diagonal length, attained at $(1,1)$:

$$\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2.$$

Every intermediate distance occurs somewhere in the square, so the range is

$$0\le X\le\sqrt2.$$

Do not use $[0,1]$ merely because the side length is 1. A point near the opposite corner can be more than one unit from the origin.

Translate numerical statements into geometric events

The statement $X=0.5$ means

$$u^2+v^2=0.25.$$

Its outcomes lie on a quarter-circle arc. The statement $X\le0.5$ instead means

$$u^2+v^2\le0.25,$$

which describes the entire quarter-disk inside that arc, including its boundary. These are different sets of outcomes: a curve versus a two-dimensional region.

More generally, $\{X\le r\}$ consists of the points inside the square that are also within radius $r$ of the origin. When $r$ grows, the associated region grows. At $r=\sqrt2$, it includes the whole square.

Uniform in location does not mean uniform in distance

“Uniformly chosen” means that probabilities are proportional to area in the square. It does not mean that all distances between 0 and $\sqrt2$ have the same probability density.

For instance, the points within distance 1 occupy a quarter of a unit-radius disk, whose area is $\pi/4$. Since the square has area 1,

$$P(X\le1)=\frac{\pi}{4}\approx0.7854.$$

If distance were uniform on $[0,\sqrt2]$, this probability would instead be $1/\sqrt2\approx0.7071$. The disagreement shows why we must derive the distance distribution from the point-selection model.

The mapping $X(u,v)$ can be defined without a probability model. The uniform-area assumption is what lets us calculate probabilities for its values and intervals. The later slides develop this geometry into a full cumulative distribution function.

Check your understanding

Does the point $(0.8,0.8)$ have distance $0.8$, $1.6$, or $\sqrt{1.28}$? Does it belong to the event $X\le1$?

Reveal the reasoning

Its Euclidean distance is $\sqrt{0.8^2+0.8^2}=\sqrt{1.28}\approx1.1314$. It lies inside the square but outside the unit-radius quarter-disk, so it does not belong to $\{X\le1\}$. Adding the coordinates would measure a different quantity.
