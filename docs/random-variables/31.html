CDFs, endpoints & jumps · Slide 31

A CDF is right-continuous and its jumps are point probabilities

Every CDF includes the mass at its current cutoff. That convention explains both its right-continuity and the jump formula: the difference between its value at a point and its left limit is exactly the probability of that point.

~2 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Identify the left limit as a probability

Fix a real number $x$ and approach it from below using the cutoffs $x-1/n$. The events

$$ A_n=\{X\le x-1/n\} $$

increase as $n$ grows. Their union is $\{X<x\}$. Indeed, every value strictly below $x$ has a positive gap from $x$, so it eventually lies below $x-1/n$. The value $x$ itself never enters these events.

By continuity of probability for increasing events,

$$ \boxed{F(x-)=\lim_{n\to\infty}F(x-1/n)=P(X<x).} $$

Because $F$ is nondecreasing, this increasing sequence of cutoffs gives its full left limit. The notation $F(x-)$ records all probability accumulated just before including $x$.

Derive the jump formula

The event $\{X\le x\}$ is the disjoint union of $\{X<x\}$ and $\{X=x\}$. Therefore

$$ F(x)=F(x-)+P(X=x), $$

and hence

$$ \boxed{P(X=x)=F(x)-F(x-).} $$

A point with positive probability is called an atom. Its mass is the vertical jump size, not the CDF's total height. For the four-value example, $F(8-)=0.5$ and $F(8)=0.8$, so the atom at eight has mass $0.8-0.5=0.3$.

Prove right-continuity rather than assume it

Now approach $x$ from above, using $x+1/n$. The events

$$ B_n=\{X\le x+1/n\} $$

decrease. Their intersection is $\{X\le x\}$: the value $x$ belongs to every event, while any value strictly greater than $x$ is eventually excluded as $1/n$ becomes smaller than its gap from $x$.

Continuity of probability for decreasing events gives

$$ \lim_{n\to\infty}F(x+1/n) =P\left(\bigcap_n B_n\right) =P(X\le x)=F(x). $$

Monotonicity again identifies this with the full right limit. Thus

$$ \boxed{F(x+)=F(x)\quad\text{for every real }x.} $$

The probability rules used here follow from countable additivity: increasing events can be split into disjoint increments; decreasing events are handled by taking complements. They remain valid when the CDF has jumps.

Combine the two sides

A function is continuous at $x$ when both one-sided limits equal its value:

$$ F(x-)=F(x)=F(x+). $$

Equality between the two limits alone is not enough for a general function; its actual value must agree too. For a CDF, the right-hand equality is automatic. The only possible failure is on the left, and the jump formula measures it exactly:

$$ \boxed{F\text{ is continuous at }x \iff P(X=x)=0.} $$

In the mixed spending example, $F(0-)=0$ but $F(0)=F(0+)=0.4$. The CDF is right-continuous at zero while being discontinuous there. At one, the left limit, value, and right limit all equal $0.55$.

A continuous CDF does not automatically supply a density

If $P(X=x)=0$ at every point, the CDF is continuous everywhere. Any distribution described by an ordinary density has this property because a single point has zero area.

The converse needs a stronger condition. Continuity alone does not guarantee that there exists a density $f$ satisfying $F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt$. That stronger property is called absolute continuity. The source uses “continuous” for having no point masses; keep that meaning separate from the additional assumption that probabilities can be computed from a density.

Check your understanding

Suppose $F(4-)=0.25$ and $F(4)=0.6$. What are $P(X=4)$, $P(X<4)$, and $F(4+)$?

Use the left limit, jump, and right-continuity

$P(X=4)=0.6-0.25=0.35$, while $P(X<4)=0.25$. Right-continuity gives $F(4+)=F(4)=0.6$. The CDF can jump at four while still agreeing with its right-hand limit there.
