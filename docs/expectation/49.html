Moment-generating functions · Slide 49

Recover the uniform mean and choose the simplest method

For $X$ uniform on $[0,1]$, the MGF is $(e^t-1)/t$ when $t\ne0$ and equals $1$ at zero. Its derivative at zero should give the mean $1/2$. The quotient looks awkward there, but the underlying function is smooth; the apparent singularity comes from the chosen formula.

~2 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Use the definition of the derivative at zero

Because $M_X(0)=1$,

$$ \begin{aligned} M_X'(0) &=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{M_X(h)-M_X(0)}h\\ &=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(e^h-1)/h-1}{h}\\ &=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^h-1-h}{h^2}. \end{aligned} $$

The numerator and denominator both approach zero. L'Hôpital's rule, applied twice, evaluates this limit:

$$ \lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^h-1-h}{h^2} =\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^h-1}{2h} =\lim_{h\to0}\frac{e^h}{2} =\boxed{\frac12}. $$

Each use differentiates numerator and denominator separately to evaluate a limit; it is not the quotient rule for differentiating their ratio.

Connect this with the derivative shown on the source

For $t\ne0$, the quotient rule gives

$$M_X'(t)=\frac{te^t-e^t+1}{t^2}.$$

Its limit at zero is also $1/2$. One application of L'Hôpital gives $te^t/(2t)$, which simplifies to $e^t/2$ for nonzero $t$. Since this bounded-variable MGF is smoothly differentiable at zero, the limiting derivative agrees with the derivative calculated directly above.

An alternative that needs no L'Hôpital rule

Expand the exponential:

$$e^h=1+h+\frac{h^2}{2}+\frac{h^3}{6}+\cdots.$$

Then

$$\frac{e^h-1-h}{h^2}=\frac12+\frac h6+\cdots\longrightarrow\frac12.$$

Thus L'Hôpital's rule is one useful method, not a necessity.

For this particular problem, direct expectation is shorter

The density is simply one on $[0,1]$, so

$$E(X)=\int_0^1x\,dx=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^1=\frac12.$$

An MGF is valuable when its compact form makes multiple moments or sums easier to analyze. It need not be the quickest tool for every individual expectation. Choose the method that exposes the structure with the fewest opportunities for error, while keeping the definition and assumptions clear.

Exercise 01 / Slide 6

Expected profit from a bet on red

Place a one-dollar bet on red. Assume every roulette pocket is equally likely, and red pays even money: a win returns your stake plus one dollar, while a loss forfeits your stake.

(a) European roulette

Find the expected net profit on the bet for a wheel with 18 red pockets, 18 black pockets, and one green zero.

(b) American roulette

Find the expected net profit for a wheel with 18 red pockets, 18 black pockets, and two green zeros.

Show solutionHide solution: Expected profit from a bet on red

Define the random variable carefully

Let $Y$ be the player's net profit from the bet. Then $Y=1$ on red and $Y=-1$ on every other color. A win returns the original dollar plus one additional dollar, so a two-dollar cash return corresponds to only one dollar of profit.

European wheel: 37 equally likely pockets

There are 18 winning and $18+1=19$ losing pockets. Thus

$$ P(Y=1)=\frac{18}{37},\qquad P(Y=-1)=\frac{19}{37}. $$

Weight the two profits:

$$ E(Y)=1\frac{18}{37}+(-1)\frac{19}{37} =\boxed{-\frac1{37}\text{ dollars}}\approx-0.0270. $$

The expected loss is about 2.70 cents per dollar wagered. There is no outcome “lose 2.70 cents”; this describes the average over many comparable bets.

American wheel: 38 equally likely pockets

Now there are 18 winning and 20 losing pockets:

$$ E(Y)=1\frac{18}{38}-1\frac{20}{38} =\boxed{-\frac1{19}\text{ dollars}}\approx-0.0526. $$

The expected loss is about 5.26 cents per dollar wagered. The extra green pocket increases the losing probability while leaving the winning payoff unchanged.

The same calculation using cash returned

Let $R$ be total money returned by the casino: $R=2$ after a win and $R=0$ after a loss. For Europe,

$$ E(R)=2\frac{18}{37}=\frac{36}{37}. $$

Subtract the one-dollar stake: $E(Y)=E(R)-1=-1/37$, exactly as before. Defining net profit first prevents the common mistake of assigning payoffs $+2$ and $-1$, which counts the stake inconsistently.

Exercise 02 / Slide 12

Length of a best-of-seven series

Two evenly matched teams play a best-of-seven series. Games are independent, and either team has probability $1/2$ of winning each game. The series stops as soon as one team wins four games.

Let $X$ denote the number of games actually played. Find:

1. The distribution

Find the probability distribution of $X$.

2. The expectation

Find the expected value $E(X)$.

Show solutionHide solution: Length of a best-of-seven series

Identify the possible lengths

A team cannot get four wins before game four, and by game seven at least one team has four wins. Thus $X\in\{4,5,6,7\}$.

For the series to end at game $k$, its eventual winner must have exactly three wins in the first $k-1$ games, then win game $k$. The other team has $k-4$ wins before the last game.

Count valid paths of a specified length

Choose which team wins the series: $2$ choices. Choose the positions of its first three wins among the first $k-1$ games: $\binom{k-1}{3}$ choices. Its last win is forced to occur at game $k$.

Every such path has probability $(1/2)^k$, by independence. Neither team can have won earlier: the eventual winner had only three wins, and the loser has at most three. Therefore

$$ \boxed{P(X=k)=2\binom{k-1}{3}\left(\frac12\right)^k, \qquad k=4,5,6,7.} $$

$k$ Valid length-$k$ paths $P(X=k)$
$4$ $2\binom33=2$ $1/8$
$5$ $2\binom43=8$ $1/4$
$6$ $2\binom53=20$ $5/16$
$7$ $2\binom63=40$ $5/16$

Their sum is $2/16+4/16+5/16+5/16=1$.

Average the length

$$ \begin{aligned} E(X)&=4\frac18+5\frac14+6\frac5{16}+7\frac5{16}\\ &=\frac8{16}+\frac{20}{16}+\frac{30}{16}+\frac{35}{16}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{93}{16}=5.8125\text{ games}}. \end{aligned} $$

Check using the chance each game is played

The first four games always occur. Game five occurs with probability $7/8$, game six with probability $5/8$, and game seven with probability $5/16$. Count one game each time it is played:

$$E(X)=4+\frac78+\frac58+\frac5{16}=\frac{93}{16}.$$

For example, the chance of reaching game six is $P(X\ge6)=5/16+5/16=5/8$. This agrees with the direct weighted average and connects the mean length to survival past successive stopping points.

Exercise 03 / Slide 18

Expected reciprocal of a continuous variable

Let $X$ be a continuous random variable with density

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}3x^2,&0<x<1,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$$

Find $E(1/X)$.

Show solutionHide solution: Expected reciprocal of a continuous variable

Check the model and identify the transformed quantity

The density integrates to one:

$$\int_0^13x^2\,dx=[x^3]_0^1=1.$$

Set $g(x)=1/x$. The expectation-of-a-function rule gives

$$E(1/X)=\int_0^1\frac1x\,3x^2\,dx.$$

There is no need to derive the density of $Y=1/X$ before calculating this average.

Simplify before integrating

On the support, $x>0$, so

$$\frac1x\,3x^2=3x.$$

Hence

$$ \boxed{E(1/X)=\int_0^13x\,dx =\left[\frac32x^2\right]_0^1=\frac32.} $$

More formally, because the original integrand involves $1/x$, the integral can be read as a limit from $\varepsilon$ to $1$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. After simplification it equals $\frac32(1-\varepsilon^2)$, whose limit is finite.

Why $1/E(X)$ would answer a different question

Here

$$E(X)=\int_0^13x^3\,dx=\frac34,$$

so $1/E(X)=4/3$, not $3/2$. Averaging reciprocals gives more influence to small observations than taking the reciprocal of a single average does.

Exercise 04 / Slide 19

Expected value of a reciprocal transformation

Let $X$ be a continuous random variable with density

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}x^2\left(2x+\frac32\right),&0<x<1,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$$

Find

$$E\left(\frac2X+3\right).$$

Show solutionHide solution: Expected value of a reciprocal transformation

Confirm the density first

Expand $f(x)=2x^3+(3/2)x^2$. It is nonnegative on its support, and

$$ \int_0^1f(x)\,dx =\left[\frac{x^4}{2}+\frac{x^3}{2}\right]_0^1=1. $$

The fraction in the source is $3/2$ inside the parentheses, so the density is $x^2(2x+3/2)$.

Write the complete expectation

$$ E\left(\frac2X+3\right) =\int_0^1\left(\frac2x+3\right)x^2\left(2x+\frac32\right)dx. $$

We may separate a sum and factor out fixed constants:

$$E\left(\frac2X+3\right)=2E(1/X)+3.$$

The second term is $3$, because the expectation of a constant is that constant.

Calculate the one nonconstant piece

$$ \begin{aligned} E(1/X)&=\int_0^1\frac1x\,x^2\left(2x+\frac32\right)dx\\ &=\int_0^1\left(2x^2+\frac32x\right)dx\\ &=\left[\frac23x^3+\frac34x^2\right]_0^1\\ &=\frac23+\frac34=\frac{17}{12}. \end{aligned} $$

Therefore

$$ \boxed{E\left(\frac2X+3\right)=2\frac{17}{12}+3 =\frac{35}{6}\approx5.8333.} $$

Check by expanding the original integral

Multiplying out its integrand gives

$$ \left(\frac2x+3\right)\left(2x^3+\frac32x^2\right) =6x^3+\frac{17}{2}x^2+3x. $$

Its integral is $3/2+17/6+3/2=35/6$, confirming the result. The simplified integrand remains integrable near zero, even though the transformed value $2/x+3$ is unbounded there.

Since $0<X<1$, we always have $2/X+3>5$. The resulting expectation $35/6>5$ passes this basic range check.
