Add probabilities without double counting · Slide 18

Split the union into three disjoint regions

The addition rule becomes a proof when we break the union into pieces to which the probability axioms apply directly. Every outcome in A or B has exactly one of three descriptions: only A, only B, or both.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Name each piece precisely

Let $B^c$ mean the complement of B, containing the outcomes in the sample space that are not in B. Define

$$R_1=A\cap B^c,\qquad R_2=A^c\cap B,\qquad R_3=A\cap B.$$

These are the three regions inside the union. The remaining region, $A^c\cap B^c$, means neither event occurs and belongs outside the union.

In A? In B? Region Included in the union?
Yes No $A\cap B^c$ Yes
No Yes $A^c\cap B$ Yes
Yes Yes $A\cap B$ Yes
No No $A^c\cap B^c$ No

The table exhausts the possibilities because an outcome either belongs to each event or does not.

Why the pieces cannot overlap

An outcome in $R_1$ belongs to A and does not belong to B. It cannot also lie in $R_3$, which requires membership in B. It cannot lie in $R_2$, which requires nonmembership in A.

Similarly, $R_2$ and $R_3$ disagree about whether the outcome belongs to A. Thus all three pairwise intersections are empty. This is pairwise disjointness, the condition needed for adding their probabilities without an overlap correction.

Why the pieces cover the entire union

Choose any outcome $\omega\in A\cup B$. If it belongs to both events, it is in $R_3$. If it belongs to A but not B, it is in $R_1$. The only other possibility is B but not A, placing it in $R_2$.

Conversely, every member of each $R_i$ belongs to at least one of A and B. Therefore,

$$A\cup B=R_1\cup R_2\cup R_3.$$

This proves both directions of the set equality. The three pieces are disjoint and collectively cover exactly the target event.

Apply additivity only after establishing disjointness

The probability axiom gives

$$P(A\cup B)=P(A\cap B^c)+P(A^c\cap B)+P(A\cap B).$$

We have not assumed the addition formula we are trying to prove. We used only additivity for disjoint events. The next slide rewrites the first two terms using $P(A)$, $P(B)$, and the shared overlap.

For a numerical picture, suppose only A has probability 0.12, only B has probability 0.27, and both has probability 0.08. Then the union has probability $0.12+0.27+0.08=0.47$. A totals $0.12+0.08=0.20$, and B totals $0.27+0.08=0.35$.

A diagram is a map, not an extra assumption

The slide's Venn diagram makes the regions easy to see, but the argument works even if events have no geometric shape. They might represent cities selected as finalists, card hands, measurements, or infinite sequences. What matters is membership, disjointness, and coverage.

Check your understanding

A student proposes the pieces A and $A^c\cap B$ instead of three pieces. Is that a valid partition of $A\cup B$?

Find the shorter partition

Yes. A contains both the only-A and the both-region. The event $A^c\cap B$ supplies exactly the B outcomes missing from A. These two pieces are disjoint, and their union is $A\cup B$. Thus $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(A^c\cap B)$. Replacing the second term by $P(B)-P(A\cap B)$ proves the same addition rule in one fewer step.
