Occupancy & birthday collisions · Slide 13

Why 23 people are enough for a better-than-even chance

Under the 365-day independent uniform birthday model, the probability of at least one shared birthday first exceeds $50\%$ at 23 people. This is not because 23 is close to 365. It is because a room of 23 people contains 253 possible pairs, each providing a chance for a match.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment
Let’s understand it

Read the table as probabilities of rooms

Using

$$P(E)=1-\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{j}{365}\right),$$

we obtain:

People $n$ Possible pairs $\binom n2$ Probability of at least one match
10 45 $0.1169$
20 190 $0.4114$
22 231 $0.4757$
23 253 $0.5073$
30 435 $0.7063$
40 780 $0.8912$
50 1,225 $0.9704$

The first six rows reproduce the slide's values; the last row extends the pattern. Since adding a person cannot remove an existing match, the probability increases with room size. The 22- and 23-person rows locate the first crossing of $1/2$.

A value of $0.5073$ means that, across many independently generated rooms of 23 people under this model, about 50.73% of rooms have at least one match. It does not mean that 50.73% of people in each room share a birthday.

Use pairs for intuition, not an exact union calculation

For a specified pair, the second person's birthday matches the first with probability $1/365$. If $M$ counts matching pairs in the whole room, linearity of expectation gives

$$E[M]=\frac{\binom n2}{365}.$$

At $n=23$, this is $253/365\approx0.6932$ matching pairs per room on average. The actual probability of any match is lower, about $0.5073$, because some rooms contribute several pairs to the average.

For every room, $\mathbf1_{\{M\ge1\}}\le M$. Averaging yields the useful union bound

$$P(E)\le\frac{\binom n2}{365},$$

or the smaller of that expression and 1. The bound explains the role of the pair count without incorrectly treating overlapping pair-match events as disjoint.

Derive a quick approximation

Write the logarithm of the no-match probability as

$$\log P(E^c)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\log\left(1-\frac j{365}\right).$$

When each $j/365$ is small, use $\log(1-x)\approx-x$. Since $0+1+\cdots+(n-1)=n(n-1)/2$,

$$ P(E^c)\approx e^{-n(n-1)/(2\cdot365)}, $$

and hence

$$P(E)\approx1-e^{-n(n-1)/(2\cdot365)}.$$

Setting this near $1/2$ gives

$$n(n-1)\approx2\cdot365\log2\approx506,$$

placing the transition around 23 people. This is an approximation to the exact product, not a replacement for it when precise values are needed. At 23 it gives about $0.5000$, compared with the exact $0.5073$.

Explore the collision threshold

Increase the room size gradually. Compare the exact probability with repeated simulated rooms. A simulation estimate fluctuates, especially with few trials; the formula supplies the long-run target under the same model.

Explore the ideaOpen larger ↗

For $d$ equally likely dates or labels, the same argument suggests a collision threshold proportional to $\sqrt d$, since the number of pairs grows roughly like $n^2/2$. This explains why collisions become relevant long before all labels have been used.

Check your understanding

The table gives a probability near $0.7063$ for 30 people. Is it surprising if one room of 30 has no shared birthdays? What about 20 independently sampled such rooms all having no match?

Reveal the reasoning

One room has no match with probability about $1-0.706316=0.293684$, so it is quite plausible. If 20 rooms are independently generated under the model, the probability that all avoid a match is about $(0.293684)^{20}$, roughly $2.3\times10^{-11}$. An individual exception is compatible with a high probability; a long independent sequence of exceptions can be extremely unlikely.
