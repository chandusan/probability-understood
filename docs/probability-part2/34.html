Inclusion–exclusion in action · Slide 34

Inclusion–exclusion corrects overlaps of every size

For many events, the same accounting continues: add single events, subtract pair intersections, add triple intersections, and alternate until every intersection has been considered. The formula works because an outcome belonging to several events ends with total weight exactly one.

~2 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Write the general rule precisely

For any finite collection of events $A_1,\ldots,A_n$,

$$ \begin{aligned} P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_i\right) ={}&\sum_iP(A_i)-\sum_{i<j}P(A_i\cap A_j)\\ &+\sum_{i<j<k}P(A_i\cap A_j\cap A_k)-\cdots\\ &+(-1)^{n+1}P(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_n). \end{aligned} $$

The restrictions $i<j$ and $i<j<k$ ensure each unordered choice of events is used once. For example, $A_1\cap A_2$ and $A_2\cap A_1$ describe the same intersection; counting both would introduce a new error.

A compact version is

$$ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_i\right) =\sum_{\varnothing\ne J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}} (-1)^{|J|+1}P\!\left(\bigcap_{j\in J}A_j\right). $$

Here J is a nonempty set of event indices and $|J|$ is the number selected. Odd-sized intersections have positive signs; even-sized intersections have negative signs. There are $\binom nk$ intersections involving k events.

Why the alternating signs always work

Fix one outcome that belongs to exactly r of the events. It appears in r single-event terms, $\binom r2$ pair terms, $\binom r3$ triple terms, and so on. Its total coefficient is

$$ \binom r1-\binom r2+\binom r3-\cdots+(-1)^{r+1}\binom rr. $$

The binomial theorem gives

$$ 0=(1-1)^r=1-\binom r1+\binom r2-\cdots+(-1)^r\binom rr $$

for $r\ge1$. Rearranging shows that the coefficient is one. An outcome belonging to none of the events appears in no term and has coefficient zero. These are exactly the coefficients defining membership in the union.

In a general probability space, the same argument is an identity between indicator functions. Taking expectations proves the probability formula without assuming equally likely outcomes. Only finitely many events are involved, so there is no issue of rearranging an infinite series.

Apply the rule to the convenient events

In the ten-dice exercise, the useful events were “face j is missing.” Their union described failure, so we took its complement afterward. More generally,

$$ P(\text{none of }A_1,\ldots,A_n) =1-\sum_iP(A_i)+\sum_{i<j}P(A_i\cap A_j)-\cdots. $$

If all intersections of k specified events share the same probability $q_k$, symmetry collapses many terms into one:

$$ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_i\right) =\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k+1}\binom nk q_k. $$

This simplification uses equal intersection probabilities, not independence. Symmetry in missing-face problems and name-drawing problems supplies exactly this structure.

Know the special cases and useful bounds

For pairwise disjoint events, all intersections involving at least two events are empty. The full formula becomes the familiar sum of the individual probabilities.

For arbitrary events, stopping after the first sum gives an upper bound:

$$ P\!\left(\bigcup_iA_i\right)\le\sum_iP(A_i). $$

Stopping after subtracting the pairs gives a lower bound:

$$ \sum_iP(A_i)-\sum_{i<j}P(A_i\cap A_j) \le P\!\left(\bigcup_iA_i\right). $$

These bounds can be useful when higher intersections are hard to compute. A negative lower bound or an upper bound above one is still mathematically valid, though the basic bounds zero and one are then tighter.

Check your understanding

With four events, how many terms occur at each stage, and what coefficient does an outcome in all four receive?

Count choices of event subsets

There are four single terms, six pair terms, four triple terms, and one four-event term. The central outcome receives $4-6+4-1=1$. There are $2^4-1=15$ terms altogether. The formula is exact, but its number of terms grows quickly; look for symmetry or an easier complement before expanding it blindly.
