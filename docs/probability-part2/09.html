Occupancy & birthday collisions · Slide 09

Build the numerator without omissions or double counting

To count assignments in which one chosen bin receives exactly $k$ of $m$ balls, select the arriving balls and distribute every remaining ball elsewhere. The resulting count, $\binom mk(n-1)^{m-k}$, is more than a memorized formula: it is a reversible recipe that produces every desired assignment exactly once.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Count all assignments first

Each of the $m$ labeled balls has $n$ possible destination bins. By the multiplication principle,

$$|S|=\underbrace{n\times n\times\cdots\times n}_{m\text{ factors}}=n^m.$$

Under independent uniform placements, every assignment has probability $1/n^m$. Multiple balls may share a destination. A bin has no capacity limit in this model, so earlier placements do not remove any destinations for later balls.

This is why the denominator is $n^m$ rather than a permutation count: repetition of destination labels is allowed.

Step 1: choose the balls in the target bin

Let the target be Bin 1. A qualifying assignment determines a unique set $K$ of balls that entered it, with $|K|=k$. There are

$$\binom mk$$

possible sets $K$. We use a combination because the target bin does not record an internal order. Choosing balls $\{1,3,5\}$ is the same selection as choosing $\{5,1,3\}$.

Once $K$ is chosen, these balls have no destination choices left: all go to Bin 1. There is no additional $k!$ factor because ordering them would not create new assignments.

Step 2: assign the remaining balls

The other $m-k$ balls must avoid Bin 1. Each has $n-1$ allowed destinations, giving

$$\underbrace{(n-1)\cdots(n-1)}_{m-k\text{ factors}}=(n-1)^{m-k}$$

assignments. The other bins may be empty, receive one ball, or receive several; the event puts no restrictions on their totals.

For every choice of $K$, there are exactly this many assignments of its complement. The multiplication principle gives

$$\boxed{|\{X=k\}|=\binom mk(n-1)^{m-k}.}$$

The slide calls these “independent choices.” For this counting step, the essential point is that every initial selection has the same number of completions. Multiplying counts does not itself require a probabilistic claim about independent events.

See the recipe on a concrete example

Take $m=5$, $n=3$, and $k=2$. There are $\binom52=10$ possible pairs entering Bin 1. If that pair is $\{2,5\}$, balls 1, 3, and 4 independently have destination choices from $\{2,3\}$, giving $2^3=8$ completions.

For example, the destination list $(3,1,2,3,1)$ is one completion. Reading it backwards identifies both the chosen pair $\{2,5\}$ and the destinations of the other balls. Thus the recipe neither misses nor duplicates this assignment. The same argument applies to every favorable outcome.

There are $10\cdot8=80$ favorable assignments out of $3^5=243$, so $P(X=2)=80/243\approx0.3292$.

Check the extremes and the total

For $k=0$, the count becomes $(n-1)^m$: every ball avoids the target. For $k=m$, it becomes 1: all balls have the fixed target destination. Both agree with direct reasoning.

Every assignment has exactly one target-bin count, so summing over $k$ must recover the whole sample space:

$$ \sum_{k=0}^m\binom mk(n-1)^{m-k} =(1+n-1)^m=n^m. $$

This binomial-theorem check catches errors such as using $n^{m-k}$ in the numerator, which would incorrectly allow extra balls into the target.

Check your understanding

Why would $\binom mk n^{m-k}$ be wrong for “exactly $k$”? Does it at least count “at least $k$” correctly?

Reveal the reasoning

It allows the remaining balls to enter the target bin, violating “exactly.” It also overcounts “at least”: an assignment with $r>k$ balls in the target can be generated by choosing any of its $\binom rk$ subsets as the initial designated group. The number of duplicate counts varies with $r$, so a single simple correction factor cannot fix it.
