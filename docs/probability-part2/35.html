Permutations & the appearance of e · Slide 35

Names in a hat lead to derangements and one over e

When everyone draws a different name from a hat, a complete assignment is a permutation. Inclusion–exclusion counts assignments containing self-matches. Surprisingly, the chance of at least one self-match approaches about 63.2%, even as the family becomes arbitrarily large.

~4 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment
Let’s understand it

Make the drawing model explicit

Suppose n distinct people draw the n distinct names without replacement, with all $n!$ assignments equally likely. This is the distribution obtained by thoroughly shuffling the names and handing one to each person in a fixed order.

The question concerns one unrestricted drawing. If the family instead keeps redrawing until nobody has their own name, the final accepted assignment has no self-matches by construction, which is a different experiment.

Let $A_i$ be the event that person i draws their own name. The first question asks for

$$ P(A_1\cup A_2\cup\cdots\cup A_n). $$

Each individual self-match has probability $1/n$. The events are not independent: once some people are fixed, the remaining names are assigned among fewer people.

Count a specified group of self-matches

If a specified set of k people must draw themselves, their assignments are fixed. The other $n-k$ people may receive the other names in any order, giving $(n-k)!$ assignments. Therefore

$$ P(A_{i_1}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_k})=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}. $$

Other people may also draw themselves. This is an intersection of k specified self-match events, not the event of exactly k self-matches.

There are $\binom nk$ ways to choose the specified group. Their contribution at level k simplifies beautifully:

$$ \binom nk\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}=\frac1{k!}. $$

Inclusion–exclusion gives the exact answer

$$ \boxed{P(\text{at least one self-match}) =\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k!}.} $$

Count assignments with no self-matches

A permutation with no fixed points is called a derangement. Let $D_n$ be the number of derangements. Taking the complement yields

$$ \boxed{P(\text{no self-match})=\frac{D_n}{n!} =\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}.} $$

For four people,

$$ P(\text{none})=1-1+\frac12-\frac16+\frac1{24}=\frac38. $$

Thus $D_4=4!\cdot3/8=9$, and at least one self-match has probability $5/8$. This gives a concrete interpretation of the fractions: nine of 24 equally likely assignments avoid every person's own name.

Explore the ideaOpen larger ↗

Use the experiment to change the family size and compare full derangements with the event that only a specified subset must avoid their own names. Setting the restricted subset size equal to the family size gives the exact ratio $D_n/n!$ studied here. Reducing that subset relaxes the requirements and prepares the next slide's extension.

Explain the limiting value

The exponential series at $x=-1$ is

$$ e^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}. $$

Therefore

$$ P(\text{none})\longrightarrow\frac1e\approx0.3678794412, $$

and

$$ \boxed{P(\text{at least one})\longrightarrow1-\frac1e\approx0.6321205588.} $$

The alternating-series error is at most the first omitted term:

$$ \left|\frac{D_n}{n!}-\frac1e\right|\le\frac1{(n+1)!}. $$

For ten people this error is below $1/11!$, about $2.51\times10^{-8}$. The limiting approximation becomes accurate very quickly.

More people does not mean a steady increase

Family size n No self-match At least one self-match
1 0 1
2 $1/2$ $1/2$
3 $1/3$ $2/3$
4 $3/8$ $5/8$
5 $11/30$ $19/30$
6 $53/144$ $91/144$

The values oscillate around their limits. There are more opportunities for a self-match as n grows, but each person’s probability is $1/n$. If X counts self-matches, linearity of expectation gives $E[X]=n(1/n)=1$ for every positive n. An expected count of one does not mean at least one is guaranteed.

Check your understanding

Why is $1-(1-1/n)^n$ not the exact answer here?

The name draws share a without-replacement constraint

That expression treats the self-match indicators as independent. For two specified people and $n\ge2$, the actual probability both draw themselves is $(n-2)!/n!=1/[n(n-1)]$, rather than $1/n^2$. With two people, the true probability of at least one self-match is $1/2$, while the independent calculation gives $3/4$. The incorrect finite-n formula happens to have the same large-n limit, so agreeing on a limit does not validate the model.
