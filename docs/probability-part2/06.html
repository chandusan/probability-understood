The power of the complement · Slide 06

Count lottery tickets by how many numbers match

A six-number lottery ticket is an unordered selection without replacement. Once the six winning numbers are fixed, every ticket can be classified by its number of matches. This gives a clean solution to all the slide's counting questions and shows why the phrase “not winning anything” needs a stated prize rule.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment
Let’s understand it

Part (a): count all possible tickets

A ticket selects six different numbers from $\{1,2,\ldots,49\}$. Choosing $1,7,12,23,35,49$ in a different order does not make a new ticket. Therefore

$$N=\binom{49}{6}=\frac{49!}{6!43!}=13{,}983{,}816.$$

Assume the draw chooses each six-number subset with equal probability. We can fix a ticket and randomize the draw, or fix the winning set and uniformly select a ticket; symmetry gives the same match-count distribution. The counting below uses a fixed winning set.

Parts (b)–(d): choose matches and nonmatches separately

There are six winning numbers and 43 nonwinning numbers. To create a ticket with exactly $k$ matches, choose $k$ numbers from the six winners and $6-k$ from the 43 nonwinners:

$$N_k=\binom6k\binom{43}{6-k},\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,6.$$

These choices identify a unique ticket. Every ticket with exactly $k$ matches can be obtained once in this way, so there is neither omission nor duplicate counting.

Matches $k$ Number of tickets Probability
0 $\binom60\binom{43}6=6{,}096{,}454$ $0.435965$
1 $\binom61\binom{43}5=5{,}775{,}588$ $0.413019$
2 $\binom62\binom{43}4=1{,}851{,}150$ $0.132378$
3 $\binom63\binom{43}3=246{,}820$ $0.0176504$
4 $\binom64\binom{43}2=13{,}545$ $0.00096862$
5 $\binom65\binom{43}1=258$ $0.00001845$
6 $\binom66\binom{43}0=1$ $0.0000000715$

Each probability is the count divided by $N$. This distribution is called hypergeometric: it counts how many members of a marked group appear in a sample taken without replacement.

Part (e): define “no prize” before calculating it

The slide does not state a prize schedule. Under the classroom convention that a prize requires at least three of the six main winning numbers, with no bonus-number or two-match prize, losing means $K\in\{0,1,2\}$. These three events are disjoint, so

$$ \begin{aligned} P(\text{no prize}) &=\frac{N_0+N_1+N_2}{N}\\ &=\frac{13{,}723{,}192}{13{,}983{,}816}\\ &\approx0.981362455. \end{aligned} $$

That is about 98.1362%. Its complement, about 1.8638%, is the probability of three or more matches under this convention. Matching no numbers at all is a different event, with probability about 43.5965%.

If a game awards a prize for two matches, the losing probability instead uses $N_0+N_1$, about $84.8984\%$. If bonus numbers affect eligibility, those outcomes must also be modeled. The combinatorial answers in parts (a)–(d) are fully determined by the slide; a real game's payout probability additionally depends on its rules.

Why a binomial shortcut is not exact

It is tempting to treat the six ticket numbers as independent chances of matching with probability $6/49$. But drawing one winning number changes the remaining pool. After one success, there are five winners among 48 remaining numbers; after one failure, there are six among 48. This dependence is exactly what the combination formula accounts for.

A useful arithmetic check is

$$\sum_{k=0}^6\binom6k\binom{43}{6-k}=\binom{49}{6}.$$

Every ticket must appear in exactly one row of the table.

Check your understanding

Why does the count for exactly one match contain a factor of six? Why are the other five numbers chosen from 43 rather than 48?

Reveal the reasoning

There are six choices for which winning number appears. The other five entries must all be nonwinning numbers, so they come from the 43-number nonwinning pool. Choosing from all 48 remaining numbers would include other winners and would accidentally count tickets with more than one match.
