Repeated objects & rare patterns · Slide 15

Can a random arrangement spell a poem?

The slide's story turns repeated-letter counting into a probability problem. Mickey randomly arranges 59 Scrabble tiles and hopes to reproduce a particular passage. Before counting, we must decide what an outcome records and confirm that the available letters can actually spell the target.

~4 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Translate the story into a precise experiment

The target is the opening of Byron's poem:

She walks in beauty, like the night
Of cloudless climes and starry skies

We treat uppercase and lowercase as the same letter and ignore spaces, punctuation, and the line break. Those features are supplied when the result is read; they are not extra tiles being randomly placed. The experiment produces a sequence of 59 letters, using each available tile exactly once.

Assume the physical tiles are shuffled uniformly, so every ordering of the 59 distinguishable physical tiles is equally likely. We will justify on the next slide why this also makes all visible letter strings with the fixed inventory equally likely.

The phrase “randomly rearranges” needs this model. Randomness alone does not guarantee equal probabilities: an imperfect shuffle might favor certain arrangements.

Audit the inventory before using a formula

The image on the slide provides these counts:

The counts sum to 59, and counting letters in the target gives this same inventory. This is a feasibility check: if the target needed a P or nine S's, its probability would be zero regardless of how large the sample space was.

Group equal counts to make the denominator readable

There are two letters occurring four times, six occurring twice, three occurring three times, two occurring five times, one occurring seven times, and one occurring eight times. The remaining present letters occur once; absent letters occur zero times.

Thus the number of different visible arrangements is

$$N=\frac{59!}{(4!)^2(2!)^6(3!)^3(5!)^2\,7!\,8!}.$$

The omitted $1!$ and $0!$ factors all equal one. The grouped denominator is simply a shorter version of multiplying the factorial of every count in the table.

Identify exactly what counts as success

Only one of these letter sequences is the specified target. We are not asking for any meaningful sentence, any poem, or any rearrangement with the same words. Each of those would be a different event with a different favorable count.

Under the uniform model, the requested probability is therefore $1/N$. A formula this small can hide several modeling choices, so stating those choices is part of the solution.

Check your understanding

Suppose success allowed the two target lines in either order. Would the probability double? What if one tile were replaced by a P?

Separate a larger event from an impossible event

The two line orders give two distinct letter sequences with the same inventory. They are disjoint single outcomes, so the probability would be $2/N$.

Replacing any tile by a P changes the inventory. The original target contains no P, and all 59 tiles must be used, so success would become impossible. Counting more permutations cannot repair a mismatch between the inventory and the target.
