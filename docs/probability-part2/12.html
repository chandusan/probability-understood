Occupancy & birthday collisions · Slide 12

Three equivalent forms of the birthday calculation

The birthday formula can be written using permutations, combinations and factorials, or a product of probabilities. Each form counts the same no-match event, but each highlights a different idea. The product is usually the most transparent way to understand and calculate the answer.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Form 1: ordered selections without replacement

Under independent uniform birthdays on 365 days, all $365^n$ assignments are equally likely. A no-match assignment chooses $n$ different dates and assigns them to $n$ labeled people. Its count is the falling factorial

$$ (365)_n=365\cdot364\cdots(365-n+1) =\frac{365!}{(365-n)!}, $$

for $0\le n\le365$. The slide denotes the same permutation count by $P_{365,n}$; that symbol is a count, not a probability.

Dividing by all assignments and taking the complement gives

$$P(E)=1-\frac{(365)_n}{365^n}.$$

The denominator allows repeated dates because the actual experiment allows matches. Only the numerator forbids repeats because it is counting the no-match event.

Form 2: choose the dates, then assign the people

First choose a set of $n$ dates in $\binom{365}{n}$ ways. Then assign those distinct dates to the $n$ distinct people in $n!$ ways. Therefore

$$ (365)_n=\binom{365}{n}n!, $$

so

$$ P(E)=1-\frac{\binom{365}{n}n!}{365^n} =1-\frac{365!}{(365-n)!\,365^n}. $$

Why is the $n!$ necessary? With two people, choosing the date set $\{\text{March 2},\text{July 8}\}$ does not say who has which birthday. There are two assignments of those dates, and they are different outcomes in the original sample space. Forgetting $n!$ mixes unordered date sets in the numerator with ordered assignments in the denominator.

Form 3: avoid an expanding collection of dates

Divide each factor in the falling factorial by 365:

$$ \boxed{P(E)=1-\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{j}{365}\right).} $$

The factor for $j=0$ is 1: the first person cannot match an earlier person. Given that the first $j$ birthdays differ, the next person must avoid their $j$ occupied dates, leaving $365-j$ safe choices.

These are conditional probabilities. For example,

$$ P(\text{first three all differ}) =1\cdot\frac{364}{365}\cdot\frac{363}{365}. $$

The birthdays themselves are independent in the model. The intermediate events “no match among the first two” and “no match among the first three” are nested and are not independent. The multiplication here is justified by successive conditioning, not by pretending those events are independent.

Calculate with the product

Starting from $q_0=1$, update the no-match probability using

$$q_{r+1}=q_r\frac{365-r}{365},\qquad r=0,1,\ldots,n-1.$$

Then $P(E)=1-q_n$. This avoids constructing enormous factorials, and every intermediate number stays between zero and one. For generalized collision problems with much larger numbers, one can compute $\log q_n=\sum_j\log(1-j/365)$ with suitable numerical routines to preserve precision.

For $n>365$, use the answer 1 directly. A factorial such as $(365-n)!$ is not defined for the negative integers that would arise, and there is no need to extend that expression: a repeated date is certain by the pigeonhole principle.

Check your understanding

A simplified calendar has five equally likely days, and three independent people enter a room. Calculate the probability of a shared birthday by both counting and a sequential product.

Reveal the reasoning

There are $5^3=125$ assignments, of which $5\cdot4\cdot3=60$ have distinct birthdays. Thus the answer is $1-60/125=13/25=0.52$. The sequential calculation is $1-(5/5)(4/5)(3/5)=0.52$. The combinations version gives the same numerator: $\binom53\,3!=10\cdot6=60$.
