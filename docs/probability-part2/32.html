Inclusion–exclusion in action · Slide 32

Ten dice: make every face appear at least once

Ten independent, fair six-sided dice are rolled. What is the probability that every face—1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6—appears at least once? We will solve this using a binomial distribution and then using direct counting, with a full explanation of the inclusion–exclusion corrections both approaches need.

~7 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment
Let’s understand it

State the requirement and define the six events

For example,

$$ (1,2,3,4,5,6,1,2,3,4) $$

satisfies the requirement. Repetitions are allowed; we only need every face to occur somewhere among the ten dice.

Label the dice $D_1,\ldots,D_{10}$. An outcome records their results in that order. Rolling simultaneously does not make the dice indistinguishable. Since each die has six equally likely results and the dice are independent, there are $6^{10}$ equally likely ordered outcomes.

The useful idea is: instead of directly requiring all six faces to appear, exclude outcomes in which some face is missing. Define

$$ \begin{aligned} A_1&=\{\text{face 1 is missing}\},\\ A_2&=\{\text{face 2 is missing}\},\\ A_3&=\{\text{face 3 is missing}\},\\ A_4&=\{\text{face 4 is missing}\},\\ A_5&=\{\text{face 5 is missing}\},\\ A_6&=\{\text{face 6 is missing}\}. \end{aligned} $$

Then

$$ U=A_1\cup A_2\cup A_3\cup A_4\cup A_5\cup A_6 $$

means at least one face is missing. We will calculate $P(U)$ first. Our desired probability is then

$$ P(\text{every face appears})=1-P(U). $$

Method 1: use the binomial distribution

Start with one particular face, say 1. For each die, define:

  • Success: the die shows 1, with probability $1/6$.
  • Failure: the die shows something else, with probability $5/6$.

There are ten independent trials with the same success probability. Therefore, if $X_1$ counts how many dice show 1,

$$ X_1\sim\operatorname{Binomial}\left(10,\frac16\right). $$

Face 1 is missing exactly when $X_1=0$. Using the binomial formula,

$$ \begin{aligned} P(A_1) &=P(X_1=0)\\ &=\binom{10}{0}\left(\frac16\right)^0 \left(\frac56\right)^{10}\\ &=\left(\frac56\right)^{10}. \end{aligned} $$

The same calculation works for each of the six faces.

Now consider two particular faces, say 1 and 2. To calculate the probability that both are missing, redefine success on each die as “shows either 1 or 2.” The success probability is now $2/6$. If $X_{\{1,2\}}$ counts such dice,

$$ X_{\{1,2\}}\sim\operatorname{Binomial}\left(10,\frac26\right). $$

Both faces are missing precisely when this count is zero:

$$ \begin{aligned} P(A_1\cap A_2) &=P(X_{\{1,2\}}=0)\\ &=\binom{10}{0}\left(\frac26\right)^0 \left(\frac46\right)^{10}\\ &=\left(\frac46\right)^{10}. \end{aligned} $$

Extend this to any specified set $S$ of $k$ faces. Let $X_S$ count dice showing any face in that set. Then

$$ X_S\sim\operatorname{Binomial}\left(10,\frac{k}{6}\right), $$

and

$$ \begin{aligned} P(\text{all faces in }S\text{ are missing}) &=P(X_S=0)\\ &=\boxed{\left(\frac{6-k}{6}\right)^{10}}. \end{aligned} $$

Other faces may also be missing. This is not the probability that exactly $k$ faces are missing. For example, an outcome missing 1, 2 and 3 still belongs to $A_1\cap A_2$.

The binomial distribution gives each missing-face probability. We now need inclusion–exclusion to combine these overlapping events and enforce the requirement that every face appears.

Why inclusion–exclusion alternates between adding and subtracting

We add and subtract because the same dice outcome can belong to several missing-face events. Inclusion–exclusion makes sure each outcome is counted exactly once. Here we are counting outcomes in $U$, the event that at least one face is missing.

Step 1: add the six individual probabilities. Start with

$$ \begin{aligned} &P(A_1)+P(A_2)+P(A_3)\\ &\quad+P(A_4)+P(A_5)+P(A_6). \end{aligned} $$

An outcome missing only face 1 appears in just $A_1$, so it is counted once. Good.

But consider this outcome:

$$ (3,4,5,6,3,4,5,6,3,4). $$

It is missing exactly faces 1 and 2. It belongs to both $A_1$ and $A_2$, so the sum counts its probability twice. We want to count it once.

Step 2: subtract all pairwise intersections. The intersection $A_1\cap A_2$ means both faces 1 and 2 are missing. Subtracting $P(A_1\cap A_2)$ corrects the outcome above:

$$ \underbrace{2}_{\text{individual events}} -\underbrace{1}_{\text{pair intersection}}=1. $$

We must do this for all 15 pairs. Explicitly, the sum we subtract is

$$ \begin{aligned} &P(A_1\cap A_2)+P(A_1\cap A_3)+P(A_1\cap A_4)\\ &+P(A_1\cap A_5)+P(A_1\cap A_6)+P(A_2\cap A_3)\\ &+P(A_2\cap A_4)+P(A_2\cap A_5)+P(A_2\cap A_6)\\ &+P(A_3\cap A_4)+P(A_3\cap A_5)+P(A_3\cap A_6)\\ &+P(A_4\cap A_5)+P(A_4\cap A_6)+P(A_5\cap A_6). \end{aligned} $$

But this creates another issue. Consider an outcome missing exactly faces 1, 2 and 3:

$$ (4,5,6,4,5,6,4,5,6,4). $$

It belongs to three individual events:

$$ A_1,\quad A_2,\quad A_3, $$

and three pairwise intersections:

$$ A_1\cap A_2,\quad A_1\cap A_3,\quad A_2\cap A_3. $$

So far, its net count is

$$ 3-3=0. $$

We have accidentally removed it completely, even though it belongs to the event “at least one face is missing.”

Step 3: add all triple intersections. Our outcome belongs to $A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3$. Adding that intersection restores its count:

$$ \underbrace{3}_{\text{individual events}} -\underbrace{3}_{\text{pair intersections}} +\underbrace{1}_{\text{triple intersection}}=1. $$

We therefore add all $\binom63=20$ triple-intersection probabilities. This is why we add here: the previous subtraction removed too much.

Steps 4–6: continue correcting overlaps. For an outcome missing exactly four faces, its count after the first three steps is

$$ \binom41-\binom42+\binom43=4-6+4=2. $$

We subtract its four-event intersection:

$$ 4-6+4-1=1. $$

For an outcome missing exactly five faces, its count after four steps is

$$ 5-10+10-5=0. $$

We add its five-event intersection:

$$ 5-10+10-5+1=1. $$

Finally, the general formula subtracts the six-event intersection. Here that intersection is impossible: ten dice cannot show none of the six faces. Its probability is zero.

The corrections work because an outcome missing $r$ faces belongs to:

  • $r$ individual missing-face events;
  • $\binom r2$ pair intersections;
  • $\binom r3$ triple intersections;
  • and so on.

The alternating corrections leave it counted exactly once. An outcome with no missing faces belongs to none of these events, so it contributes zero to $P(U)$, as it should.

Apply inclusion–exclusion to all six events

All indices below run from 1 through 6. The inequalities such as $i<j$ ensure we include each pair or larger group only once:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} P(U) ={}&\sum_i P(A_i)\\ &-\sum_{i<j}P(A_i\cap A_j)\\ &+\sum_{i<j<k}P(A_i\cap A_j\cap A_k)\\ &-\sum_{i<j<k<\ell}P(A_i\cap A_j\cap A_k\cap A_\ell)\\ &+\sum_{i<j<k<\ell<m} P(A_i\cap A_j\cap A_k\cap A_\ell\cap A_m)\\ &-P(A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3\cap A_4\cap A_5\cap A_6). \end{aligned} } $$

There are $\binom6k$ ways to choose which $k$ faces are specified as missing. Each corresponding intersection has the binomial zero-success probability $((6-k)/6)^{10}$ calculated above. Substituting gives:

Intersections Number of terms Probability for each specified set Contribution to $P(U)$
Single events $\binom61=6$ $(5/6)^{10}$ $+6(5/6)^{10}$
Pairs $\binom62=15$ $(4/6)^{10}$ $-15(4/6)^{10}$
Triples $\binom63=20$ $(3/6)^{10}$ $+20(3/6)^{10}$
Groups of four $\binom64=15$ $(2/6)^{10}$ $-15(2/6)^{10}$
Groups of five $\binom65=6$ $(1/6)^{10}$ $+6(1/6)^{10}$
All six $\binom66=1$ $(0/6)^{10}=0$ $0$

Therefore,

$$ \begin{aligned} P(U)={}&6\left(\frac56\right)^{10} -15\left(\frac46\right)^{10} +20\left(\frac36\right)^{10}\\ &-15\left(\frac26\right)^{10} +6\left(\frac16\right)^{10}. \end{aligned} $$

This counts outcomes with at least one missing face. Taking its complement reverses all those signs:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} P(\text{every face appears}) ={}&1-6\left(\frac56\right)^{10} +15\left(\frac46\right)^{10}\\ &-20\left(\frac36\right)^{10} +15\left(\frac26\right)^{10} -6\left(\frac16\right)^{10}. \end{aligned} } $$

In this final formula, we start with all outcomes, subtract those missing individual faces, add back the pair overlaps that were subtracted twice, and continue the corrections.

Method 2: count outcomes without a binomial distribution

We can obtain every term using direct counting. There are $6^{10}$ equally likely outcomes, so

$$ P(\text{every face appears}) =\frac{\text{number of outcomes containing all six faces}}{6^{10}}. $$

Now count outcomes to exclude:

  • Face 1 is missing: each die has five allowed results, so there are $5^{10}$ outcomes.
  • Faces 1 and 2 are both missing: each die has four allowed results, so there are $4^{10}$ outcomes.
  • Any specified $k$ faces are missing: each die has $6-k$ allowed results, so there are $(6-k)^{10}$ outcomes.

No binomial-distribution formula is needed—these counts follow directly from multiplying the number of choices for each die.

Accounting for overlaps as before, the number of favorable outcomes is

$$ \begin{aligned} N={}&6^{10}-\binom61 5^{10}+\binom62 4^{10} -\binom63 3^{10}\\ &+\binom64 2^{10}-\binom65 1^{10}+\binom66 0^{10}\\ ={}&60{,}466{,}176-58{,}593{,}750+15{,}728{,}640\\ &-1{,}180{,}980+15{,}360-6\\ ={}&\boxed{16{,}435{,}440}. \end{aligned} $$

Here, $\binom6k$ simply counts choices of excluded faces. Using combinations does not mean we are using a binomial distribution.

Divide by the total number of equally likely outcomes:

$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} P(\text{every face appears}) &=\frac{16{,}435{,}440}{60{,}466{,}176}\\ &=\frac{38{,}045}{139{,}968}\\ &\approx0.271812\approx27.18\%. \end{aligned} } $$

Both approaches express the same counting logic. For any specified $k$ missing faces, the direct-counting probability is

$$ \frac{(6-k)^{10}}{6^{10}} =\left(\frac{6-k}{6}\right)^{10}, $$

which is exactly the zero-success probability we obtained from the binomial distribution. The complete calculation can also be written compactly as

$$ P(\text{every face appears}) =\frac{1}{6^{10}}\sum_{k=0}^{6}(-1)^k\binom6k(6-k)^{10}. $$

The $k=0$ term is the unrestricted sample space. The $k=6$ term is zero because ten dice cannot take values from an empty set. Even with ten rolls, complete coverage occurs only about $27.18\%$ of the time. Repeated faces consume rolls without introducing a missing face.

Explore how coverage changes with the number of dice

Explore the ideaOpen larger ↗

For n dice, replace the exponent 10 with n in the same formula. Fewer than six dice make coverage impossible. With exactly six dice, each face must appear once, so the favorable count is $6!$ and the probability is $6!/6^6$. As the number of independent rolls grows, the coverage probability approaches one.

The missing-face events are dependent. For instance, knowing that face 1 never appears changes the distribution of the remaining observed faces. We therefore cannot multiply six “this face appears” probabilities to obtain the coverage probability.

Verify the count by building sequences

A recurrence gives an independent exact check

Let $a_{r,k}$ be the number of length-r dice sequences using exactly k distinct faces. Start with $a_{0,0}=1$, and take impossible states to have count zero. To form a sequence using k faces after the next roll, either reuse one of k faces already seen, or introduce one of the $7-k$ faces absent from a sequence that previously used $k-1$ faces. Thus

$$ a_{r+1,k}=k\,a_{r,k}+(7-k)a_{r,k-1}. $$

Iterating to $r=10$ gives $a_{10,6}=16{,}435{,}440$, matching inclusion–exclusion. Equivalently, distribute the ten labeled positions into six nonempty groups and label the groups by the six faces: the count is $6!S(10,6)$, where $S(10,6)=22{,}827$ is a Stirling number of the second kind.

Check your understanding

Why does $6!\,6^4$ fail as a favorable count for ten dice?

The proposed construction adds an unrequested condition

It forces the first six dice to show every face, then leaves the last four free. That is a valid subset of successful outcomes, but it misses outcomes whose sixth distinct face first appears on die 7, 8, 9, or 10. Choosing one representative position for each face instead creates a different problem: outcomes with repeated faces can have many possible representative choices and become overcounted.
