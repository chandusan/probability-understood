A complete reference for the main discrete distributions

This table collects the PMFs, possible values, means, and variances from the chapter. Read the experiment and the support together: a formula is useful only after its variable and assumptions match the problem. In particular, the geometric and negative-binomial variables below count total trials, including the final success.

~3 min reading/Source + intuition + practice/1 interactive experiment
Let’s understand it

The distribution reference

Write $q=1-p$. For the waiting-time models assume $0<p\le1$, and let $r$ be a positive integer. For the hypergeometric model, $N$ is population size, $K$ is its number of successes, and $n$ is sample size.

Distribution PMF $P(X=x)$ Support Mean Variance
Bernoulli $p^xq^{1-x}$ $0,1$ $p$ $pq$
Binomial $\binom nxp^xq^{n-x}$ $0,\ldots,n$ $np$ $npq$
Poisson $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x/x!$ $0,1,\ldots$ $\lambda$ $\lambda$
Geometric, total trials $pq^{x-1}$ $1,2,\ldots$ $1/p$ $q/p^2$
Negative binomial, total trials $\binom{x-1}{r-1}p^rq^{x-r}$ $r,r+1,\ldots$ $r/p$ $rq/p^2$
Discrete uniform on integers $a,\ldots,b$ $1/m$, where $m=b-a+1$ $a,\ldots,b$ $(a+b)/2$ $(m^2-1)/12$
Hypergeometric $\binom Kx\binom{N-K}{n-x}/\binom Nn$ $\max(0,n-N+K),\ldots,\min(n,K)$ $nK/N$ $n(K/N)(1-K/N)(N-n)/(N-1)$

Every PMF is zero outside its support. The hypergeometric variance formula assumes $N>1$; when $N=1$, any permitted sample count is deterministic and has variance zero. In the discrete-uniform row, $m$ is the number of supported integers; the source calls this quantity $N$ without defining it beside that row. The source leaves the hypergeometric support and moments blank; they are completed here.

Why the waiting-time means and variances fit together

Waiting for $r$ successes can be split into $r$ independent stretches: wait for the first success, then the next, and continue. Each stretch has a geometric distribution with mean $1/p$ and variance $q/p^2$. Adding the independent stretches gives mean $r/p$ and variance $rq/p^2$.

If $Y$ instead counts failures before success number $r$, then $Y=X-r$. Its mean is $r/p-r=rq/p$, its support starts at 0, and its variance is unchanged. Always check this convention before using a table or software function.

Derive the discrete-uniform variance

Let $U$ be uniform on $1,\ldots,m$. Using the finite-sum identities,

$$E[U]=\frac1m\frac{m(m+1)}2=\frac{m+1}2,$$

$$E[U^2]=\frac1m\frac{m(m+1)(2m+1)}6=\frac{(m+1)(2m+1)}6.$$

Subtract the square of the mean:

$$\operatorname{Var}(U)=\frac{(m+1)(2m+1)}6-\frac{(m+1)^2}4 =\frac{(m+1)(m-1)}{12}.$$

Moving the support from $1,\ldots,m$ to $a,\ldots,b$ only adds the constant $a-1$, so variance stays $(m^2-1)/12$.

Derive the geometric mean and variance from its MGF

For $X$ counting trials to the first success, start with the entire expectation:

$$M_X(t)=\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}e^{tx}pq^{x-1} =pe^t\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(qe^t)^j =\frac{pe^t}{1-qe^t},$$

provided $qe^t<1$. When $p=1$, $X=1$ with certainty and $M_X(t)=e^t$ for every $t$.

For $0<p<1$, take the logarithm to simplify the differentiation:

$$K_X(t)=\log p+t-\log(1-qe^t).$$

The chain rule gives

$$K_X'(t)=1+\frac{qe^t}{1-qe^t}=\frac1{1-qe^t},$$

$$K_X''(t)=\frac{qe^t}{(1-qe^t)^2}.$$

Since $1-q=p$, evaluating at zero gives

$$E[X]=K_X'(0)=\frac1p,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=K_X''(0)=\frac q{p^2}.$$

Why does the second derivative of the logarithm give variance? Because $K''=M''/M-(M'/M)^2$ and $M(0)=1$, so $K''(0)=E[X^2]-E[X]^2$.

Exercise 01 / Slide 5

MGF of a two-value distribution

Suppose $X$ is a discrete random variable with probability mass function

$$p(x)=\begin{cases}\frac13,&x=1,\\\frac23,&x=2,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$$

Find the moment generating function $m_X(t)$.

Show solutionHide solution: MGF of a two-value distribution

Transform the values, preserving their probabilities

When $X=1$, the transformed variable $e^{tX}$ equals $e^t$. When $X=2$, it equals $e^{2t}$. Therefore

$$\boxed{m_X(t)=\frac13e^t+\frac23e^{2t}.}$$

This is an expectation, so the two terms are added. We are averaging over mutually exclusive possible values of $X$, not multiplying probabilities of successive events.

Because the sum has only two finite terms, it is finite for every real $t$. In particular, it exists around zero.

Check normalization

At zero,

$$m_X(0)=\frac13+\frac23=1.$$

This is a useful check on any proposed MGF. A function with $m(0)\ne1$ cannot be the MGF of a probability distribution.

Exercise 02 / Slide 21

Guessing on a true-or-false exam

A student takes a ten-question true-or-false exam and guesses on every question. Assume the guesses are independent and each answer is equally likely to be chosen.

(a) Exactly six correct

Find the probability that the student answers exactly six questions correctly.

(b) Passing the exam

Find the probability that the student obtains a passing grade of 60% or greater.

Show solutionHide solution: Guessing on a true-or-false exam

Identify the random variable and its distribution

Each question is a Bernoulli trial with success meaning a correct answer. There are ten fixed trials, and the stated independence and equal guessing probabilities give

$$X\sim\operatorname{Binom}(10,1/2).$$

All $2^{10}=1024$ answer-correctness sequences have equal probability. A count of $k$ correct answers has $\binom{10}{k}$ such sequences.

Part a: exactly six correct answers

Choose the six questions answered correctly. Every choice determines which four were answered incorrectly, so

$$\boxed{P(X=6)=\binom{10}{6}\left(\frac12\right)^{10} =\frac{210}{1024}=0.205078125.}$$

The coefficient counts the locations of the six correct answers; it does not assume that the first six questions were correct.

Part b: a passing grade

At least 60% of ten means at least six correct answers:

$$\begin{aligned} P(X\ge6)&=\frac{\binom{10}6+\binom{10}7+\binom{10}8+\binom{10}9+\binom{10}{10}}{1024}\\ &=\frac{210+120+45+10+1}{1024}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{386}{1024}=0.376953125.} \end{aligned}$$

Thus the probability of passing by guessing is about 37.70%, larger than the probability of exactly six correct.

Check using symmetry

A fair guess gives $P(X=k)=P(X=10-k)$. The outcomes below five correct and above five correct therefore have equal total probability. Removing the middle outcome gives

$$P(X\ge6)=\frac{1-P(X=5)}2 =\frac{1-252/1024}{2}=\frac{386}{1024}.$$

The middle probability prevents this tail from being exactly one half.

Source labeling note: The slide labels both questions (a). The passing-grade question is labeled (b) here to distinguish the two parts.

Exercise 03 / Slide 22

Insurance among four homeowners

In this exercise's model, 30% of California homeowners are insured against earthquake damage. Four homeowners are selected at random. Let $X$ denote the number among the four with earthquake insurance.

Use independent selections, each with insured probability $0.30$; this makes explicit the sampling assumption used in this lesson.

(a) Distribution

Find the probability distribution of $X$.

(b) Exactly two

Find $P(X=2)$.

(c) Bar plot

Draw the corresponding probability bar plot.

(d) Most likely value

What is the most likely value of $X$, called the mode of the distribution?

(e) At least two

What is the probability that at least two of the four selected homeowners have earthquake insurance?

Show solutionHide solution: Insurance among four homeowners

Part a: the probability distribution

Under this model, $X\sim\operatorname{Binom}(4,0.3)$, so

$$P(X=x)=\binom4x(0.3)^x(0.7)^{4-x},\qquad x=0,1,2,3,4.$$

Insured homeowners $x$ Calculation Probability
0 $0.7^4$ 0.2401
1 $4(0.3)(0.7)^3$ 0.4116
2 $6(0.3)^2(0.7)^2$ 0.2646
3 $4(0.3)^3(0.7)$ 0.0756
4 $0.3^4$ 0.0081

The entries sum to one.

Parts b and c: two insured, and the bars

Exactly two has probability $\boxed{0.2646}$. The factor $6=\binom42$ accounts for which two of the four people are insured.

The bar plot puts the number insured on the horizontal axis and its probability on the vertical axis. The bar heights are exactly the probabilities in the table.

Part d: the most likely count

The highest probability is $0.4116$ at $x=1$. Thus the unique mode is $\boxed{1}$. The mean is $4(0.3)=1.2$; a most likely value and an average are different concepts.

Part e: at least two insured

$$\begin{aligned} P(X\ge2)&=P(X=2)+P(X=3)+P(X=4)\\ &=0.2646+0.0756+0.0081=\boxed{0.3483}. \end{aligned}$$

Equivalently, $1-P(X=0)-P(X=1)=1-0.2401-0.4116=0.3483$.

If sampling is strictly without replacement from a specified finite population, the exact model is hypergeometric; the 30% figure alone does not specify that population's size.

Exercise 04 / Slide 25

Binomial cumulants and the third central moment

Let $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$, where $n$ is a positive integer and $0\le p\le1$. Its probability mass function is

$$P(X=x)=\binom nxp^x(1-p)^{n-x},\qquad x=0,1,\ldots,n,$$

and its moment generating function is

$$m_X(t)=(1-p+pe^t)^n.$$

Find the cumulant generating function $K_X(t)$ and the third central moment

$$\mu_3^0=E\big[(X-E(X))^3\big].$$

Show solutionHide solution: Binomial cumulants and the third central moment

Begin with the whole function

Write $q=1-p$. Then

$$\boxed{K_X(t)=\log m_X(t)=n\log(q+pe^t).}$$

This is a cumulant generating function; the source's phrase “cumulant distribution function” should be read as “cumulant generating function.”

Introduce a short expression to expose the derivatives

Let

$$r(t)=\frac{pe^t}{q+pe^t}.$$

Differentiating the logarithm gives $K_X'(t)=nr(t)$. Use the quotient rule to differentiate $r$:

$$r'(t)=\frac{pe^t(q+pe^t)-pe^t(pe^t)}{(q+pe^t)^2} =\frac{pq e^t}{(q+pe^t)^2}.$$

Since $1-r(t)=q/(q+pe^t)$, this is

$$r'(t)=r(t)(1-r(t)).$$

Therefore

$$K_X''(t)=nr(t)(1-r(t)).$$

Differentiate once more

Using the product rule,

$$\begin{aligned} K_X^{(3)}(t) &=n\{r'(t)(1-r(t))-r(t)r'(t)\}\\ &=nr'(t)(1-2r(t))\\ &=nr(t)(1-r(t))(1-2r(t)). \end{aligned}$$

At zero, $r(0)=p/(q+p)=p$. The third cumulant equals the third central moment, so

$$\boxed{E[(X-np)^3]=np(1-p)(1-2p).}$$

Interpret the sign

When $p=1/2$, the moment is zero, consistent with symmetry about $n/2$. When $p<1/2$, it is positive: the distribution has a longer tail toward larger success counts. When $p>1/2$, it is negative.

This is an unstandardized measure. For $0<p<1$, dividing by $(npq)^{3/2}$ gives skewness $(1-2p)/\sqrt{npq}$.

Exercise 05 / Slide 44

Prove the even-count probability for a Poisson variable

Let $X$ be a Poisson random variable with parameter $\lambda>0$. Show that

$$P(X\text{ is even})=\frac12\left(1+e^{-2\lambda}\right).$$

Hint from the source:

$$\begin{aligned} e^\lambda+e^{-\lambda} &=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^k}{k!}\\ &=2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2k}}{(2k)!}. \end{aligned}$$

Show solutionHide solution: Prove the even-count probability for a Poisson variable

Write exactly the terms we want

The even counts are $0,2,4,\ldots$, giving

$$P(X\text{ even})=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2j}}{(2j)!}.$$

We need a way to keep the even powers in the exponential series and remove the odd powers.

Add the series at opposite inputs

Write the expansions side by side:

$$e^{\lambda}=1+\lambda+\frac{\lambda^2}{2!}+\frac{\lambda^3}{3!}+\cdots,$$

$$e^{-\lambda}=1-\lambda+\frac{\lambda^2}{2!}-\frac{\lambda^3}{3!}+\cdots.$$

On addition, every odd power cancels and every even power appears twice:

$$e^{\lambda}+e^{-\lambda}=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2j}}{(2j)!}.$$

Therefore

$$\begin{aligned} P(X\text{ even}) &=e^{-\lambda}\frac{e^{\lambda}+e^{-\lambda}}2\\ &=\boxed{\frac{1+e^{-2\lambda}}2}. \end{aligned}$$

The complementary odd-count probability is $(1-e^{-2\lambda})/2$.

An equivalent probability argument using a sign

The variable $(-1)^X$ is $+1$ when $X$ is even and $-1$ when it is odd. Its expectation is therefore

$$E[(-1)^X]=P(\text{even})-P(\text{odd})=e^{-2\lambda},$$

where the final equality follows from the same exponential series. Combining this difference with $P(\text{even})+P(\text{odd})=1$ gives the answer.

Check the extremes

As $\lambda\to0$, almost all probability is at zero, so the answer tends to one. As $\lambda$ becomes large, $e^{-2\lambda}$ becomes tiny and the even and odd probabilities approach one half. They are not exactly equal for a finite positive $\lambda$.
