Moments, transforms & cumulants · Slide 06

Read probabilities from an expanded MGF

Suppose a random variable has MGF $m_X(t)=((1+2e^t)/3)((1+3e^t)/4)$. We want $P(X=1)$. The useful step is to expand the expression into the standard discrete-MGF form.

~2 min reading/Source + intuition + practice
Let’s understand it

Identify the template

For a finite discrete distribution,

$$m_X(t)=\sum_x P(X=x)e^{tx}.$$

The coefficient of $e^{tx}$ is the probability attached to the value $x$. In particular, a constant is a multiple of $e^{0t}$, so it represents mass at zero.

Multiply out before interpreting

$$\begin{aligned} m_X(t) &=\frac{(1+2e^t)(1+3e^t)}{12}\\ &=\frac{1+3e^t+2e^t+6e^{2t}}{12}\\ &=\frac1{12}e^{0t}+\frac5{12}e^{1t}+\frac12e^{2t}. \end{aligned}$$

Thus

$$\boxed{P(X=0)=\frac1{12},\quad P(X=1)=\frac5{12},\quad P(X=2)=\frac12.}$$

The coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one, as they must.

Why does the middle coefficient have two contributions?

The factors are MGFs of independent Bernoulli variables with success probabilities $2/3$ and $3/4$. If $X$ is their sum, obtaining one success has two disjoint possibilities:

$$P(0,1)=\frac13\frac34=\frac3{12},\qquad P(1,0)=\frac23\frac14=\frac2{12}.$$

Adding produces $5/12$. This illustrates how multiplying MGFs automatically combines ways in which independent random variables can add to the same total.

The algebraic expansion already solves the stated question; this independent-sum interpretation explains the structure of its answer.
