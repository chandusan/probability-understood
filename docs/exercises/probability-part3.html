Exercise 01 / Slide 11

Exactly one of three independent events

Let $A$, $B$, and $C$ be mutually independent events, with

$$P(A)=P(B)=P(C)=0.1.$$

(c) Exactly one event

Find the probability that exactly one of $A$, $B$, and $C$ occurs.

This is the part labeled “Exercise” on the slide; parts (a) and (b) belong to its preceding worked example.

Show solutionHide solution: Exactly one of three independent events

(c) List the three possible ways

“Exactly one” means that one event occurs and the other two do not. It can happen in these three ways:

Which event occurs? What must happen Probability
Only $A$ $A\cap B^c\cap C^c$ $0.1(0.9)(0.9)=0.081$
Only $B$ $A^c\cap B\cap C^c$ $(0.9)0.1(0.9)=0.081$
Only $C$ $A^c\cap B^c\cap C$ $(0.9)(0.9)0.1=0.081$

Each complement has probability $1-0.1=0.9$. Mutual independence lets us multiply the three probabilities in each row, including when an event is replaced by its complement.

The rows are mutually exclusive: “only $A$” and “only $B$” cannot both happen. We therefore add their probabilities:

$$\boxed{P(\text{exactly one})=3(0.1)(0.9)^2=0.243.}$$

The two factors of $0.9$ are essential. Multiplying $3\times0.1$ alone would not require the other two events to fail.

We can check one row directly using intersections. Start with all outcomes in $A$, remove those also in $B$ or $C$, and add back the triple overlap because it was removed twice:

$$P(A\cap B^c\cap C^c)=0.1-0.01-0.01+0.001=0.081.$$

Exercise 02 / Slide 13

Lost fox tags

Identical tags are placed on both ears of a fox. After the fox has been released for some time, define

$$C_1=\{\text{the left ear tag is lost}\},\qquad C_2=\{\text{the right ear tag is lost}\}.$$

Assume the two loss events are independent and $P(C_1)=P(C_2)=p$.

Find the probability that exactly one tag is lost, given that at most one tag is lost. State when this conditional probability is defined.

Show solutionHide solution: Lost fox tags

Translate the condition carefully

Let $E$ mean “exactly one tag is lost” and $T$ mean “at most one tag is lost.” The condition $T$ allows either zero losses or one loss; it excludes losing both tags.

Even though the tags look identical, the two ears are distinct. These are the four possible states:

Left tag Right tag Probability Allowed by $T$?
Retained Retained $(1-p)^2$ Yes
Lost Retained $p(1-p)$ Yes
Retained Lost $(1-p)p$ Yes
Lost Lost $p^2$ No

For each row, independence lets us multiply the probability of the left outcome by the probability of the right outcome.

Count the probability in the numerator

Exactly one loss is the union of the second and third rows. Those cases cannot overlap, so

$$P(E)=p(1-p)+(1-p)p=2p(1-p).$$

Count the probability in the denominator

“At most one loss” excludes only the last row:

$$P(T)=1-P(C_1\cap C_2)=1-p^2.$$

Every outcome in $E$ already belongs to $T$, so $E\cap T=E$. Thus

$$\begin{aligned} P(E\mid T) &=\frac{P(E\cap T)}{P(T)}\\ &=\frac{2p(1-p)}{1-p^2}\\ &=\frac{2p(1-p)}{(1-p)(1+p)}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{2p}{1+p}},\qquad 0\le p<1. \end{aligned}$$

Dividing by $1-p^2$ rescales the three allowed rows so that their conditional probabilities add to one.

At $p=1$, both tags are always lost. The condition “at most one lost” then has probability zero, so the conditional probability is undefined. Canceling $1-p$ does not make the formula valid at $p=1$.

For example, if $p=0.2$, exactly one loss has probability $0.32$ and at most one loss has probability $0.96$. The answer is $0.32/0.96=1/3$. The three permitted states generally have different probabilities, so simply counting two favorable states out of three is not valid.

Exercise 03 / Slides 16, 20

Two draws and the last ball

An urn contains $r$ red balls and $b$ black balls, where $N=r+b\ge2$. Balls are selected uniformly at random from those remaining, without replacement.

(a) Exactly one red

If two balls are drawn sequentially, what is the probability that exactly one of the two is red?

(b) The final ball

Now suppose every ball is drawn, one by one without replacement, until the urn is empty. Before observing any draws, what is the probability that the very last ball is red?

Show solutionHide solution: Two draws and the last ball

(a) Include both possible orders

Exactly one red can occur as red then black or black then red. These are disjoint cases.

For red then black, the first draw has red probability $r/N$. After a red ball is removed, all $b$ black balls remain among $N-1$ balls. Thus

$$P(R_1\cap B_2)=\frac rN\frac b{N-1}.$$

For black then red, the first draw has black probability $b/N$. Then all $r$ red balls remain among $N-1$ balls:

$$P(B_1\cap R_2)=\frac bN\frac r{N-1}.$$

Add the two routes:

$$\boxed{P(\text{exactly one red}) =\frac{rb+br}{N(N-1)} =\frac{2rb}{(r+b)(r+b-1)}.}$$

The factor $2$ accounts for the two color orders. The draws are not independent: the denominator on the second draw has decreased by one.

A counting check gives the same result. There are $\binom N2$ equally likely pairs of distinct balls. A favorable pair contains one of the $r$ red balls and one of the $b$ black balls, giving $rb$ favorable pairs. Therefore $rb/\binom N2=2rb/[N(N-1)]$.

(b) Count complete orderings

Label the $N$ individual balls, even if balls of the same color look alike. Any specified ordering has probability

$$\frac1N\frac1{N-1}\cdots\frac11=\frac1{N!}.$$

Consequently, all $N!$ complete orderings are equally likely.

To make the final ball red:

  1. Choose which red ball occupies the last position: $r$ choices.
  2. Arrange the other $N-1$ balls in the earlier positions: $(N-1)!$ choices.

There are $r(N-1)!$ favorable orderings, so

$$\boxed{P(\text{last ball is red}) =\frac{r(N-1)!}{N!} =\frac rN =\frac r{r+b}.}$$

This explains why the last position has the same initial red probability as the first. Each individual ball appears last in exactly $(N-1)!$ equally likely orderings.

The answer is calculated before seeing earlier draws. If those earlier colors are revealed, the conditional probability can change; with only one ball remaining, its color may already be known.

Exercise 04 / Slide 36

A fraud flag followed by SMS verification

A bank uses an automated AI system to monitor credit card transactions. In the model, 95% of transactions are legitimate and 5% are fraudulent.

  • If a transaction is fraudulent, the AI flags it as suspicious 85% of the time and marks it safe 15% of the time.
  • If a transaction is legitimate, the AI marks it safe 92% of the time and incorrectly flags it as suspicious 8% of the time.

(a) A suspicious flag

A new transaction has been flagged as suspicious. What is the probability that it is legitimate?

(b) A flag and an unconfirmed SMS

The bank adds a second verification step. Whenever the AI flags a transaction, an SMS is sent to the cardholder asking them to confirm it.

  • For a legitimate transaction, confirmation succeeds 90% of the time and fails 10% of the time.
  • For a fraudulent transaction, confirmation fails 95% of the time, while a spoofed confirmation succeeds 5% of the time.

Assume the confirmation behavior is independent of the AI flagging mechanism given whether the transaction is legitimate or fraudulent.

A new transaction is flagged as suspicious and its SMS goes unconfirmed. What is the probability that this transaction is fraudulent?

Show solutionHide solution: A fraud flag followed by SMS verification

Define the events

Let $L$ mean legitimate, $F$ fraudulent, $S$ flagged suspicious, and $U$ unconfirmed. We are given

$$P(L)=0.95,\quad P(F)=0.05,\quad P(S\mid L)=0.08,\quad P(S\mid F)=0.85.$$

(a) Restrict to transactions that were flagged

A flag can arise in two ways: a legitimate transaction gets a false alarm, or a fraudulent transaction gets detected. Multiply along each route:

$$P(L\cap S)=P(L)P(S\mid L)=0.95(0.08)=0.076,$$

$$P(F\cap S)=P(F)P(S\mid F)=0.05(0.85)=0.0425.$$

Every flagged transaction is in one of these two disjoint groups, so

$$P(S)=0.076+0.0425=0.1185.$$

Among flagged transactions, the legitimate share is

$$\boxed{P(L\mid S)=\frac{P(L\cap S)}{P(S)} =\frac{0.076}{0.1185} =\frac{152}{237}\approx0.64135.}$$

Thus about 64.14% of flagged transactions are legitimate. The legitimate group is initially so much larger that its false alarms outnumber the fraud alerts, despite its lower flagging rate.

(b) Restrict to transactions with both pieces of evidence

The question now gives us $S\cap U$: a flag and an unconfirmed message. The denominator must include all transactions satisfying both conditions.

Conditional independence means that, after fixing the true status, knowing about the flag does not alter the stated confirmation rate:

$$P(U\mid S,L)=0.10,\qquad P(U\mid S,F)=0.95.$$

For a fraudulent transaction to produce both observations, follow the complete branch:

$$\begin{aligned} P(F\cap S\cap U) &=P(F)P(S\mid F)P(U\mid S,F)\\ &=0.05(0.85)(0.95)\\ &=0.040375. \end{aligned}$$

For a legitimate transaction to produce both observations,

$$P(L\cap S\cap U)=0.95(0.08)(0.10)=0.0076.$$

Add these disjoint explanations for the evidence:

$$P(S\cap U)=0.040375+0.0076=0.047975.$$

Therefore

$$\boxed{P(F\mid S\cap U) =\frac{0.040375}{0.047975} =\frac{1615}{1919}\approx0.84158.}$$

The probability of fraud is now about 84.16%. An unconfirmed message is much more likely for a fraudulent transaction than for a legitimate one, so this additional evidence raises the fraud probability.

See the same calculation as counts

Imagine 40,000 transactions whose proportions match the model exactly:

True status Initially Flagged Flagged and unconfirmed
Legitimate 38,000 3,040 304
Fraudulent 2,000 1,700 1,615
Total 40,000 4,740 1,919

Part (a) takes $3040/4740$. Part (b) takes $1615/1919$. In each case, the denominator is the group matching what we have observed, and the numerator is the requested status within that group.
