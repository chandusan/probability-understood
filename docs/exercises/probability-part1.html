Exercise 01 / Slide 10

When do two random chords intersect?

What is the probability that two random chords $AB$ and $CD$ intersect at a point inside a circle?

Use the following explicit interpretation of “random”: choose the four endpoints $A,B,C,D$ independently and uniformly on the circumference, then join $A$ to $B$ and $C$ to $D$. Coincident endpoints have probability zero.

Show solutionHide solution: When do two random chords intersect?

The chords cross inside the circle precisely when their endpoints alternate as you go around the circumference. Equivalently, $C$ and $D$ must lie on opposite sides of the chord $AB$.

Start the clockwise reading at $A$. The remaining labels $B,C,D$ can appear in six equally likely orders. They are equally likely because their locations are independent and identically distributed: exchanging their labels cannot favor one order over another.

Clockwise order starting at $A$ Do the chords cross?
$A,B,C,D$ No
$A,B,D,C$ No
$A,C,B,D$ Yes
$A,C,D,B$ No
$A,D,B,C$ Yes
$A,D,C,B$ No

For example, $A,C,B,D$ places $C$ on the arc from $A$ to $B$ and $D$ on the other arc, so joining $C$ to $D$ crosses $AB$. If both lie on the same arc, their chord stays on that side.

There are two favorable orders out of six:

$$ \boxed{P(AB\text{ intersects }CD\text{ inside})=\frac26=\frac13.} $$

The endpoint model matters. The phrase “random chord” alone does not specify how chords are sampled; the answer above is for the process stated in the question.

Exercise 02 / Slide 14

Describe a grid-search sample space

A machine-learning grid search chooses one value for each setting:

  • n_estimators: 100, 200, 300, or 400;
  • max_depth: 10, 20, or 30;
  • criterion: gini or entropy.

What is the sample space $S$ of possible configurations?

Show solutionHide solution: Describe a grid-search sample space

One outcome must record all three settings together. For example, $(100,20,\text{gini})$ is one configuration; changing its depth to 30 produces a different configuration.

Using coordinates in the order (n_estimators, max_depth, criterion), the sample space is

$$ \boxed{S=\{100,200,300,400\}\times\{10,20,30\}\times\{\text{gini},\text{entropy}\}.} $$

The multiplication signs denote a Cartesian product: take every triple formed by choosing one element from each set.

There are 4 choices for the first coordinate, 3 for the second, and 2 for the third. Hence

$$ |S|=4\cdot3\cdot2=24. $$

Describing a sample space does not by itself assign probabilities. These 24 configurations would have equal probability only if the selection procedure made them equally likely.

Exercise 03 / Slides 20, 41

Three cars turning at an intersection

Three cars at an intersection can each turn left ($L$) or right ($R$). Record their decisions in car order: first car, second car, third car.

(a) Sample space

List the sample space $S$.

(b) Events

List the outcomes in

$$ A=\{\text{exactly one car turns right}\},\qquad C=\{\text{all cars turn in the same direction}\}. $$

(c) Probabilities

Find $P(A)$ and $P(C)$, assuming that each car independently chooses left or right with probability $1/2$ each.

Show solutionHide solution: Three cars turning at an intersection

(a) Sample space

Each of three positions has two choices, so there are $2^3=8$ outcomes:

$$ \boxed{S=\{LLL,LLR,LRL,LRR,RLL,RLR,RRL,RRR\}.} $$

The order matters. $LLR$ and $RLL$ describe different cars turning right.

(b) Events

For exactly one right turn, the single $R$ can occupy any one of the three positions:

$$ \boxed{A=\{LLR,LRL,RLL\}.} $$

For all cars to choose the same direction, either all turn left or all turn right:

$$ \boxed{C=\{LLL,RRR\}.} $$

(c) Probabilities

Independence and the $1/2$ probabilities give each complete sequence probability

$$ \frac12\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac18. $$

The three outcomes in A are disjoint, so their probabilities add:

$$ \boxed{P(A)=\frac38=0.375.} $$

Similarly,

$$ \boxed{P(C)=\frac28=\frac14=0.25.} $$

There are four possible counts of right turns, $0,1,2,3$, but those counts are not equally likely. We count the eight equally likely sequences instead.

Exercise 04 / Slide 24

Sketch a union and an intersection

Sketch the regions in the $xy$-coordinate plane corresponding to $A\cup B$ and $A\cap B$, where

$$ A=\{(x,y):0<x<4,\ 0<y<3\}, $$

$$ B=\{(x,y):2<x<4,\ 2<y<4\}. $$

Show solutionHide solution: Sketch a union and an intersection

First draw the two open rectangles:

  • A runs from $x=0$ to $x=4$ and from $y=0$ to $y=3$.
  • B runs from $x=2$ to $x=4$ and from $y=2$ to $y=4$.

The strict inequalities mean the boundary of each rectangle is excluded from that rectangle.

Intersection: keep only the overlap

A point must satisfy both sets of inequalities. Its $x$ coordinate must be between 2 and 4; its $y$ coordinate must be between 2 and 3:

$$ \boxed{A\cap B=\{(x,y):2<x<4,\ 2<y<3\}.} $$

Shade the rectangle with corners $(2,2),(4,2),(4,3),(2,3)$, using dashed edges to show they are excluded.

Union: keep everything in either rectangle

Shade all of A and all of B. The resulting shape is the large lower rectangle A with B's upper-right extension attached. A useful exact description is

$$ \boxed{ A\cup B= \{(x,y):0<x\le2,\ 0<y<3\} \ \cup\ \{(x,y):2<x<4,\ 0<y<4\}. } $$

Its outer outline runs through $(0,0),(4,0),(4,4),(2,4),(2,3),(0,3)$ and back to $(0,0)$. These outer edges are excluded.

Be careful with internal edges: a boundary excluded by one rectangle can still belong to the other. For example, $(3,3)$ is not in A but is inside B, so it belongs to the union. Likewise, $(2,2.5)$ belongs to A. Do not remove an artificial seam through the shaded union.

Exercise 05 / Slide 27

Set operations for a die roll

Roll a standard six-sided die, with sample space $S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$. Let

$$ A=\{\text{roll an even number}\}=\{2,4,6\}, $$

$$ B=\{\text{roll a number less than 4}\}=\{1,2,3\}. $$

Find $A\cup B$, $A\cap B$, and the complement $A^c$.

Show solutionHide solution: Set operations for a die roll

The union means “in A or in B, including both.” Collect all the distinct outcomes from either list:

$$ \boxed{A\cup B=\{1,2,3,4,6\}.} $$

The outcome 2 appears in both lists but is written once in the union.

The intersection means “in A and in B.” Only 2 is both even and less than 4:

$$ \boxed{A\cap B=\{2\}.} $$

The complement means “in the sample space but not in A.” Remove the even faces from $S$:

$$ \boxed{A^c=\{1,3,5\}.} $$

No probability assumptions are needed for these set operations. They identify outcomes; fairness would matter if we subsequently assigned numerical probabilities.

Exercise 06 / Slide 33

Prove the subset properties

Let $A$ and $B$ be subsets of the same sample space $S$. Use $\subseteq$ for inclusion that allows equality.

Prove the properties discussed on the slide:

  1. If $A\subseteq B$, then $A\cap B=A$.
  2. If $A\subseteq B$, then $A\cup B=B$.
  3. $A\subseteq B$ if and only if $B^c\subseteq A^c$.
Show solutionHide solution: Prove the subset properties

To prove a set identity, take an arbitrary outcome and check what membership means. To prove an inclusion, show that every member of the first set must belong to the second.

1. The intersection is A

Suppose $A\subseteq B$. Every outcome in A is already in B, so every outcome in A is in both: $A\subseteq A\cap B$.

Conversely, an outcome in $A\cap B$ is in A by the definition of intersection: $A\cap B\subseteq A$.

The two inclusions give $\boxed{A\cap B=A}$.

2. The union is B

Every outcome in B is in $A\cup B$. Conversely, an outcome in $A\cup B$ lies either in B already or in A. In the latter case, $A\subseteq B$ puts it in B too.

Therefore $\boxed{A\cup B=B}$. Adding the smaller set A contributes no new outcomes to B.

3. Taking complements reverses inclusion

First assume $A\subseteq B$. Take $x\in B^c$, so $x\notin B$. It cannot be in A, because every member of A belongs to B. Hence $x\in A^c$. This proves $B^c\subseteq A^c$.

For the reverse direction, assume $B^c\subseteq A^c$ and take $x\in A$. If $x\notin B$, then $x\in B^c$, so the assumption would force $x\in A^c$, a contradiction. Thus $x\in B$, proving $A\subseteq B$.

Both directions together establish

$$ \boxed{A\subseteq B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B^c\subseteq A^c.} $$

Geometrically, a larger set leaves a smaller outside region, which explains the reversed inclusion.

Exercise 07 / Slide 34

Determine which set inclusions are guaranteed

For arbitrary events $A$ and $B$, determine which of the following sets are subsets of which others:

$$ A,\qquad B,\qquad A\cup B,\qquad A^c\cap B. $$

Find the inclusions that must hold without any further assumptions about A and B. Inclusion is allowed to be equality.

Show solutionHide solution: Determine which set inclusions are guaranteed

Write $U=A\cup B$ and $D=A^c\cap B$.

U contains everything in A or B. D contains the part of B that is outside A: $D=B\setminus A$.

Every member of A lies in U. Every member of B lies in U. Every member of D lies in B and consequently also in U. The nontrivial guaranteed inclusions are therefore

$$ \boxed{A\subseteq U,\qquad D\subseteq B\subseteq U.} $$

The chain includes $D\subseteq U$. Each of the four sets is also a subset of itself.

Here is the complete comparison. Read a cell as “the row set is a subset of the column set.”

Row set A B U D
A Always Not always Always Not always
B Not always Always Always Not always
U Not always Not always Always Not always
D Not always Always Always Always

For example, take $A=\{1,2\}$ and $B=\{2,3\}$. Then $U=\{1,2,3\}$ and $D=\{3\}$. This one example disproves all the “not always” inclusions: A has an element outside B and D, B has elements outside A and D, U has elements outside each smaller set, and D is not contained in A.

“Not always” means the relationship is not guaranteed. Some extra inclusions could hold in special cases; for example, if $A=\varnothing$, then $D=B=U$.

Exercise 08 / Slide 42

A sum of at least ten on two dice

Two independent fair six-sided dice are rolled. Let $A$ be the event that their sum is greater than or equal to 10. Find $P(A)$.

Show solutionHide solution: A sum of at least ten on two dice

Record an outcome as an ordered pair $(d_1,d_2)$, distinguishing the first die from the second. There are $6\cdot6=36$ equally likely outcomes.

List all outcomes with the required sum:

Sum Outcomes Count
10 $(4,6),(5,5),(6,4)$ 3
11 $(5,6),(6,5)$ 2
12 $(6,6)$ 1

These cases cannot overlap: an outcome has only one sum. The favorable count is $3+2+1=6$. Therefore,

$$ \boxed{P(A)=\frac{6}{36}=\frac16.} $$

The sums 2 through 12 are not equally likely, so dividing the three favorable sums by eleven possible sums would be incorrect. Ordered die results are the equally likely outcomes.
